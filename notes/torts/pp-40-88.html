1. RULES

Rule: Sudden Incapacitation Negates Intent

  • Source: Textbook narrative citing Goodrich v. Blair, Cohen v. Petty, and Buckley and Toronto Transp. Comm. v. Smith Transp.

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Where a defendant loses all capacity for voluntary action, such as by unexpectedly becoming incapacitated (heart attack, fainting, sudden insane delusion caused by disease), the defendant will not be held capable of forming the requisite intent for an intentional tort

  • Legal Question: Can a defendant who suddenly and unforeseeably loses conscious control over her body be held liable for an intentional tort committed during that episode?

  • Requirements:

    1. The defendant experienced a sudden, unexpected loss of capacity for voluntary action

    2. The defendant had no prior reason to know the episode would occur

  • Application: Used to defeat intentional tort claims (e.g., battery from a car crash) where the defendant's act during the incident was not a product of conscious volition

  • Exceptions: Does not apply, and defendant may be liable on a negligence theory, where the defendant had reason to know she was subject to such seizures/episodes and nonetheless engaged in high-risk activity (see next rule)

 

Rule: Negligence Liability for Known Susceptibility to Incapacitating Episodes

  • Source: Textbook narrative citing Reliance Insurance Co. v. Dickens, Breunig v. American Family Insurance Co., and Goldman v. New York Railways

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Even though a defendant cannot form the intent necessary for an intentional tort during a sudden incapacitating episode, she may still be liable under a negligence theory if she had reason to know she was subject to such seizures/episodes and engaged in activities creating a high degree of risk to others

  • Legal Question: How does the law handle harm caused during an incapacitating episode when the defendant knew of her own susceptibility to such episodes?

  • Requirements:

    1. The defendant had actual or constructive knowledge (e.g., lucid intervals from which a jury could infer awareness) that she was subject to seizures/hallucinations/dizzy spells

    2. The defendant nonetheless engaged in conduct posing high risk to others (e.g., continued driving)

  • Application: Converts what would otherwise be a no-liability incident into an ordinary negligence claim based on the defendant's own prior knowledge and choice to continue risky conduct

  • Exceptions: Does not apply if the defendant had no reason to anticipate the episode (first-time, unforeseeable incapacitation)

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) of Torts Battery Definition

  • Source: Restatement, quoted in Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Comms., Inc

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor is subject to liability for battery if he acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the person of another, and a harmful or offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results

  • Legal Question: What are the elements of the tort of battery?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the person of another

    2. A harmful or offensive contact directly or indirectly results

  • Application: Applied by the Ohio Court of Appeals to hold that Furman's deliberate blowing of cigar smoke into Leichtman's face stated a claim for battery

  • Exceptions: Distinguish from the "smoker's battery" theory (imposing liability merely on substantial certainty that exhaled smoke will contact a nonsmoker), which the Leichtman court expressly declined to adopt or credit

 

Rule: Offensive Contact Standard

  • Source: Case holding, Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Comms., Inc, quoting Ohio Supreme Court precedent

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Contact which is offensive to a reasonable sense of personal dignity is offensive contact, defined as contact "disagreeable or nauseating or painful because of outrage to taste and sensibilities or affronting insultingness"

  • Legal Question: What makes a contact "offensive" for purposes of battery liability?

  • Requirements: The contact must be evaluated against a reasonable sense of personal dignity, not the plaintiff's subjective sensitivity

  • Application: Applied to hold that intentionally blowing cigar smoke, a "particulate matter" capable of making contact, into someone's face is an offensive contact

  • Exceptions: Ordinary, customary, and reasonably necessary contacts of everyday life (a tap on the shoulder, a friendly grasp of the arm) are not offensive because of implied consent (see Prosser rule below); courts struggle to define the standard's contours for unusually sensitive plaintiffs or plaintiffs with conditions like autism (Notes 4-5)

 

Rule: Liability for Encouraging or Inciting a Battery

  • Source: Case holding, Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Comms., Inc (common law principle)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: One who is present and encourages or incites the commission of a battery by words can be equally liable as a principal

  • Legal Question: Can a person who did not physically commit the battery nonetheless be liable for it?

  • Requirements: Presence plus words of encouragement or incitement directed at the person committing the battery

  • Application: Applied to deny Cunningham's motion to dismiss because the complaint alleged that "[a]t Defendant Cunningham's urging, Defendant Furman repeatedly blew cigar smoke in Plaintiff's face"

  • Exceptions: Mere presence without encouragement is not enough (this principle is developed further in the Halberstam aiding-abetting framework)

 

Rule: Respondeat Superior for Employee Intentional Torts

  • Source: Case holding, Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Comms., Inc

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An employer is not legally responsible for the intentional torts of its employees that do not facilitate or promote its business, but whether an employer is liable under respondeat superior because the employee acted within the scope of employment is ordinarily a question of fact

  • Legal Question: When is an employer vicariously liable for an employee's intentional tort?

  • Requirements: The employee's tortious conduct must facilitate or promote the employer's business to fall within the scope of employment

  • Application: Applied to deny WLW's motion to dismiss because whether Furman's on-air smoke-blowing was within the scope of his employment as a talk-show host was a factual question

  • Exceptions: None stated beyond the "facilitate or promote business" limitation itself

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons § 1 — Battery General Definition

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, Council Draft No. 5 (2018), quoted in Notes following Leichtman

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor is subject to liability for battery if the actor intends to cause a contact with the person of another, the actor's affirmative conduct causes such a contact, and the contact either causes bodily harm or is offensive

  • Legal Question: How does the modern Restatement redefine battery, and does it require intent to cause harm/offense specifically (dual intent) or merely intent to cause contact (single intent)?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intent to cause a contact (not necessarily intent that the contact be harmful or offensive)

    2. Affirmative conduct causing the contact

    3. The contact causes bodily harm or is offensive

  • Application: Raised as a point of comparison to the Restatement Second and Leichtman's formulation, which both describe intent as "intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact" — this comparison frames the single-intent versus dual-intent debate

  • Exceptions: Under the Third Restatement's single-intent approach, it is not necessary that the defendant intended the contact to be harmful or offensive, only that he intended the contact itself

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 4 — Purposeful Infliction of Bodily Harm

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, quoted in Notes following Leichtman

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor is subject to liability for purposeful infliction of bodily harm if the actor purposely causes bodily harm to another, either by affirmative conduct or by failing to prevent bodily harm when the actor has a duty to prevent it

  • Legal Question: How does the law address purposeful infliction of bodily harm that does not fit the contact-based definition of battery?

  • Requirements:

    1. Purpose to cause bodily harm

    2. Causation by affirmative conduct or by omission where a duty to prevent harm exists

  • Application: Illustrated by hypotheticals (a guard lowering a cell's temperature to make a prisoner ill; a manager removing an employee's chair to cause pain from a herniated disc) where no "contact" occurs, so battery is unavailable, but this proposed tort would impose liability

  • Exceptions: Requires a duty to prevent harm for omission-based liability; mere failure to help a stranger would not qualify absent such a duty

 

Rule: Contact with Objects Closely Attached to the Person Suffices for Battery

  • Source: Textbook narrative, Notes following Leichtman, citing Fisher v. Carrousel Motor Hotel and Dodwell v. Burford

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: The contact required for battery need not be with the body itself; contact with something attached or closely related to a person's body (a hat, a cane, a plate held in hand, a horse being ridden, an occupied car) is sufficient

  • Legal Question: How far does the "contact with the person" requirement of battery extend?

  • Requirements: The object contacted must be closely associated with or attached to the plaintiff's person at the time of contact

  • Application: Applied in Fisher v. Carrousel Motor Hotel to hold that snatching a plate from plaintiff's hand while saying he could not be served constituted battery

  • Exceptions: None stated, though the Restatement Third's contact-plus-harm/offense structure raises the question whether physical injury should matter to this extension

 

Rule: Common Law Definition of Assault

  • Source: Case holding, Dickens v. Puryear (North Carolina common law)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An assault is an offer to show violence to another without striking him; the interest protected is freedom from apprehension of a harmful or offensive contact with one's person, and the apprehension created must be one of an immediate harmful or offensive contact, as distinguished from contact in the future

  • Legal Question: What distinguishes an assault from a battery, and what temporal quality must the threatened contact have?

  • Requirements:

    1. An offer/threat of violence

    2. Creating apprehension of imminent (not future) harmful or offensive contact

  • Application: Applied to hold that the pointed pistol, the brandished knife, and the votes on killing/castrating plaintiff were assaults, while the later threat to kill plaintiff if he did not leave the state was not an assault because it threatened future, not imminent, harm

  • Exceptions: Threats of future harm are not assaults; they may instead be actionable as intentional infliction of emotional distress

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 29(1) — Imminence Requirement

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 29(1), quoted in Dickens v. Puryear

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: To make the actor liable for assault he must put the other in apprehension of an imminent contact; "imminent" does not mean instantaneous, but rather that there will be no significant delay

  • Legal Question: How immediate must the threatened contact be to qualify as an assault?

  • Requirements: Apprehension of contact with no significant delay, though not necessarily instantaneous

  • Application: Used by the North Carolina Supreme Court to distinguish the imminent threats (pistol, knife, votes on killing) from the non-imminent threat of future death if plaintiff didn't leave the state

  • Exceptions: None stated beyond the imminent/future distinction itself

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 31 — Words Alone Insufficient

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 31, quoted in Dickens v. Puryear

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Words do not make the actor liable for assault unless together with other acts or circumstances they put the other in reasonable apprehension of an imminent harmful or offensive contact with his person

  • Legal Question: Can words alone constitute an assault?

  • Requirements: Words must be accompanied by other acts or circumstances creating reasonable apprehension of imminent contact

  • Application: Relevant to evaluating threats such as "Y'all come on out" combined with a pointed pistol

  • Exceptions: "Threats for the future . . . are simply not present breaches of the peace" and thus never fall within assault's narrow boundaries, per Prosser, quoted in the case

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 5 — Assault (Dual Intent)

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, Council Draft No. 5 (2018), quoted in Notes following Dickens

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor is subject to liability for assault if the actor intends to cause the other to anticipate an imminent, and harmful or offensive, contact with his or her person, and the actor's affirmative conduct causes the other to so anticipate

  • Legal Question: What intent must a defendant have to commit assault, and how does it differ from the intent required for battery?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intent to cause anticipation of an imminent contact that is harmful or offensive

    2. Affirmative conduct causing that anticipation

  • Application: Applied via the Restatement's hypotheticals (Note 4) to test whether various punch-swinging, gun-pointing, and threat scenarios satisfy both the imminence and the harmful/offensive-contact anticipation requirements

  • Exceptions: Courts adopt a dual-intent approach for assault (intent to cause apprehension of a harmful/offensive contact specifically) even where they adopt single intent for battery — it is not enough to merely intend to cause anticipation of any imminent contact

 

Rule: Conditional Threats May Constitute Assault

  • Source: Textbook narrative, Note 3 following Dickens

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: A threat conditioned on some prior occurrence (e.g., "stop talking or I'll knock you down") is not excused from assault liability merely because the plaintiff is nominally "in control" of whether the condition is triggered; courts have roundly rejected the argument that such conditionality defeats liability

  • Legal Question: Does a threat that is conditional on the plaintiff's own conduct defeat the imminence or intent requirements of assault?

  • Requirements: The conditional threat must otherwise satisfy imminence and apprehension requirements

  • Application: Used to test hypotheticals like a knife-wielding party guest's statement, "If all of these people weren't here, I would stab you"

  • Exceptions: Where the condition negating the threat is itself present and obviously prevents the threatened act (e.g., a crowd that will never leave), imminence may be lacking

 

Rule: Nature of the Action Determined by Facts and Relief Sought, Not by Plaintiff's Label

  • Source: Case holding, Dickens v. Puryear

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: The nature of a legal action is not determined by what either party calls it, but by the issues arising on the pleadings and the relief sought, and by the facts proved or forecast by the evidentiary showing

  • Legal Question: Can a plaintiff avoid a shorter statute of limitations by re-labeling assault and battery claims as intentional infliction of emotional distress?

  • Requirements: Courts look past the label to the substance of the facts alleged/shown

  • Application: Applied to recharacterize Dickens's "intentional infliction of mental distress" claim as one for assault and battery (mostly time-barred) based on the facts shown at the summary judgment hearing

  • Exceptions: The one true future threat (death if plaintiff didn't leave the state) survived recharacterization because its facts did not fit the assault definition

 

Rule: Infant Liability for Intentional Torts

  • Source: Case holding, Singer v. Marx, interpreting California Civil Code § 41 and Ellis v. D'Angelo

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An infant is liable for his torts even though he lacks the mental development and capacity to recognize the wrongfulness of his conduct, so long as he has the mental capacity to have the state of mind necessary to the commission of the particular tort charged

  • Legal Question: Can a child be held liable for an intentional tort despite lacking a mature moral understanding of right and wrong?

  • Requirements: The child must have sufficient mental capacity to intend the specific act constituting the tort (e.g., intend the violent contact of a battery), even if incapable of recognizing wrongfulness or of forming the different capacity required for negligence

  • Application: Applied to hold that nine-year-old Tim Marx could be liable for battery for throwing a rock, distinguishing battery's intent requirement from negligence's requirement that a child appreciate the risk of "heedless conduct"

  • Exceptions: A child may be capable of the intent for battery while simultaneously being incapable, as a matter of law, of negligence (as with the four-year-old in Ellis v. D'Angelo)

 

Rule: Transferred Intent

  • Source: Case holding, Singer v. Marx, citing Lopez v. Surchia and Reynolds v. Pierson

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: If a defendant unlawfully aims at one person and hits another, he is liable for assault and battery on the party he actually hit, the injury being the direct, natural, and probable consequence of the wrongful act

  • Legal Question: Is a defendant liable when he intends to commit a tort against one person but instead injures a different, unintended person?

  • Requirements:

    1. Defendant intended to commit a tortious act (e.g., throw a rock at) against one person

    2. The act instead struck/affected a different person

  • Application: Applied where the jury could infer Tim threw a rock at Barbara but accidentally struck Denise; the doctrine renders him liable to Denise

  • Exceptions: The rule is not confined to criminal cases; it applies equally in civil tort actions

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 11 — Transferred Intent (Persons and Torts)

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons § 11, Council Draft No. 5 (2018), referenced in Notes following Singer v. Marx

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Transferred intent operates to transfer intent not only between persons but also between torts, such that if A fires a gun intending to injure B, and B is frightened (assault) while C is instead struck (battery), the intent requirement is satisfied for both C's battery claim and B's assault claim even though A intended neither specific outcome

  • Legal Question: Does transferred intent apply only when the same tort is transferred between persons, or can it also convert intent for one tort into intent for a different tort?

  • Requirements: Defendant's original tortious intent (toward any person, for any of the enumerated torts) suffices to establish intent for whichever combination of tort/victim actually results

  • Application: Used to analyze hypothetical where A shoots at B, frightening B (assault) and missing but hitting C (battery) — both B and C have viable claims via transferred intent

  • Exceptions: None stated in the excerpt

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 876 — Persons Acting in Concert

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 876 (1979), quoted in Halberstam v. Welch

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: For harm resulting to a third person from the tortious conduct of another, one is subject to liability if he (a) does a tortious act in concert with the other or pursuant to a common design with him [conspiracy], or (b) knows that the other's conduct constitutes a breach of duty and gives substantial assistance or encouragement to the other so to conduct himself [aiding-abetting], or (c) gives substantial assistance to the other in accomplishing a tortious result and his own conduct, separately considered, constitutes a breach of duty to the third person

  • Legal Question: Under what theories can a secondary, non-directly-acting defendant be held liable for the tortious acts of a primary tortfeasor?

  • Requirements: Satisfy any one of subsections (a), (b), or (c)

  • Application: The D.C. Circuit organized its entire analysis of Hamilton's liability for Welch's burglaries and killing of Halberstam around subsections (a) (conspiracy) and (b) (aiding-abetting)

  • Exceptions: Subsection (c) requires the defendant's own conduct, separately considered, to itself be a breach of duty — not explored in depth in Halberstam

 

Rule: Elements of Civil Conspiracy

  • Source: Case holding, Halberstam v. Welch

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Civil conspiracy requires (1) an agreement between two or more persons, (2) to participate in an unlawful act or a lawful act in an unlawful manner, (3) an injury caused by an unlawful overt act performed by one of the parties to the agreement, and (4) that overt act was done pursuant to and in furtherance of the common scheme

  • Legal Question: What must a plaintiff prove to hold a defendant liable as a coconspirator for a tort actually committed by another?

  • Requirements:

    1. Agreement (which may be tacit, not explicit)

    2. Participation in an unlawful act/lawful act unlawfully

    3. Unlawful overt act causing injury

    4. The overt act done in furtherance of the common scheme

  • Application: Applied to hold Hamilton liable because she agreed with Welch (tacitly, inferred from years of cohabitation and knowing participation) to pursue a burglary/fencing enterprise, and Welch's killing of Halberstam during a burglary escape was in furtherance of that scheme

  • Exceptions: A conspirator need not have participated in, benefited from, planned, or even known about the specific injurious overt act, so long as its purpose was to advance the conspiracy's overall object

 

Rule: Elements of Aiding-Abetting Liability

  • Source: Case holding, Halberstam v. Welch

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Aiding-abetting requires (1) that the party the defendant aids perform a wrongful act causing an injury, (2) that the defendant be generally aware of his role as part of an overall illegal or tortious activity at the time he provides assistance, and (3) that the defendant knowingly and substantially assist the principal violation

  • Legal Question: When is a defendant who assisted, but did not agree to join, a tortfeasor's wrongdoing liable for the resulting harm?

  • Requirements:

    1. Primary wrongful act causing injury

    2. Defendant's general awareness of her role in the overall illegal/tortious activity

    3. Knowing and substantial assistance

  • Application: Applied to hold Hamilton liable as an aider-abettor because she was generally aware Welch was engaged in a continuing criminal (burglary/fencing) enterprise and knowingly provided substantial assistance as bookkeeper, banker, and recordkeeper for the stolen-goods operation

  • Exceptions: Mere presence at the scene is not enough (Rael v. Cadena); normal spousal/housemate support activities are viewed cautiously and given low weight in the "relation to tortfeasor" factor (Duke v. Feldman)

 

Rule: Restatement § 876(b) Five (Six) Factor Test for "Substantial Assistance"

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 876 comment, applied in Halberstam v. Welch

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Whether assistance/encouragement is "substantial" enough for aiding-abetting liability depends on the nature of the act encouraged, the amount and kind of assistance given, the defendant's presence or absence at the time of the tort, the defendant's relation to the tortfeasor, and the defendant's state of mind; the Halberstam court added a sixth factor — the duration of the assistance

  • Legal Question: How much aid or encouragement is "substantial" enough to trigger joint liability?

  • Requirements: Balancing of all six factors, none individually dispositive

  • Application: Applied factor-by-factor to Hamilton (nature of act = long burglary/laundering enterprise; amount of assistance = essential bookkeeping/banking role; absence at time of murder weighed against liability but success of enterprise required her role; relation as housemate given low weight; state of mind = knowing, deliberate, long-term participation; duration = five years) to conclude her assistance was substantial

  • Exceptions: Verbal encouragement alone can suffice where the act encouraged is one (like a beating) for which words matter greatly (Rael v. Cadena); physical distance from the tortious act does not defeat liability where the assistance (e.g., selling a photo, laundering goods) is otherwise integral (Russell v. Marboro Books)

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 10 — Participation in an Intentional Tort

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, Tentative Draft No. 3 (2018), quoted in Notes following Halberstam

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor who knowingly and substantially instigates, encourages, or assists another's commission of battery, purposeful infliction of bodily harm, assault, intentional infliction of emotional harm, or false imprisonment is subject to liability for that tort, even if the actor's own conduct does not independently satisfy all elements of the underlying tort

  • Legal Question: How does the modern Restatement recharacterize the conspiracy/aiding-abetting doctrines developed at common law?

  • Requirements: Knowing and substantial instigation, encouragement, or assistance of another's commission of one of the enumerated intentional torts

  • Application: Provides the doctrinal label ("participation claims") for the theories analyzed in Halberstam

  • Exceptions: Participation liability is not true vicarious liability (a form of strict liability); it turns on the defendant's own wrongfulness in instigating, assisting, or participating — and courts often relax/dispense with strict but-for causation requirements for the participating defendant's own acts (though the primary tortfeasor's causation of the injury must still be shown)

 

Rule: Scope of Liability for Foreseeable Acts in Furtherance of Concerted Activity

  • Source: Case holding, Halberstam v. Welch, citing Cobb v. Indian Springs and American Family Mutual Insurance Co. v. Grim

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: A person who assists a tortious act may be liable for other reasonably foreseeable acts done in connection with it, even acts not specifically planned or known about

  • Legal Question: How far does a conspirator's or aider-abettor's liability extend beyond the specific act she agreed to or assisted?

  • Requirements: The additional act must be a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the activity assisted/agreed to

  • Application: Applied to hold Hamilton liable for Welch's killing of Halberstam because violence and killing is a foreseeable risk of a nighttime property-crime enterprise, even though Hamilton did not know specifically that Welch committed burglaries (as opposed to fencing or armed robbery)

  • Exceptions: None explicitly stated, though the foreseeability inquiry itself operates as the limiting principle

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 7 — False Imprisonment General Definition

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, Council Draft No. 5 (2018)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor is subject to liability for false imprisonment if the actor intends to confine the other within a limited area (or has intent sufficient under the transferred intent provision), the actor's affirmative conduct causes a confinement (or the actor fails to release the other from confinement despite owing a duty to do so), and the other is aware of the confinement or suffers bodily harm as a result

  • Legal Question: What are the elements of false imprisonment under the modern Restatement?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intent to confine within a limited area

    2. Affirmative conduct causing confinement, or failure to release despite a duty to do so

    3. Plaintiff's awareness of confinement, or bodily harm resulting from it

  • Application: Frames the doctrinal structure used to analyze McCann and Fojtik

  • Exceptions: Confinement may be established either by the plaintiff's contemporaneous awareness or, alternatively, by proof of resulting bodily harm even absent awareness (e.g., an unconscious infant harmed by confinement)

 

Rule: Restatement (Third) § 9 — Confinement by Assertion of Legal Authority

  • Source: Restatement (Third) of Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons, quoted in Notes following Fojtik

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actor confines another if the actor asserts legal authority to take the other into custody or otherwise confine the other, and the other submits because he believes either that he has a duty to comply or that he might face adverse legal or physical consequences for failure to comply

  • Legal Question: Can confinement occur through a false or genuine claim of legal authority, without physical force or barriers?

  • Requirements:

    1. Actor's assertion of legal authority to confine

    2. Plaintiff's submission based on a felt duty to comply or fear of adverse consequences

  • Application: Applies to roles "ordinarily understood to be vested with legal authority" — teachers, security guards, hospital doctors, store managers, casino employees, flight attendants — and illustrated by the hypothetical of a police officer telling a minority plaintiff to stand against a wall

  • Exceptions: Either the felt legal duty or the fear of physical harm alone is independently sufficient to effectuate confinement — the plaintiff need not prove both

 

Rule: Means of Confinement

  • Source: Textbook narrative, Notes preceding McCann/Fojtik

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: An actionable confinement may occur by a variety of means: barriers, force, express or implied threat of force, duress, fraudulent inducement, or the assertion of legal authority

  • Legal Question: What forms of conduct can establish the "confinement" element of false imprisonment?

  • Requirements: The specific means asserted must, on the facts, actually restrict the plaintiff's freedom of movement in a legally sufficient way (e.g., barriers must be substantial enough to prevent reasonable escape; duress must be substantial pressure)

  • Application: Used as an organizing checklist for analyzing McCann (implicit threat + assertion of authority) and Fojtik (alleged threat of commitment)

  • Exceptions: Each means requires its own threshold determination (e.g., a climbable barrier may not be substantial enough; mild social pressure may not amount to duress)

 

Rule: Elements of False Imprisonment (Texas)

  • Source: Case holding, Fojtik v. Charter Medical Corp

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: The elements of a false imprisonment cause of action are (1) willful detention by the defendant, (2) without consent of the detainee, and (3) without authority of law; a detention may be accomplished by violence, by threats, or by any other means that restrain a person from moving from one place to another

  • Legal Question: What must a plaintiff prove to establish false imprisonment under Texas law?

  • Requirements:

    1. Willful detention

    2. Lack of consent

    3. Lack of legal authority

  • Application: Applied to grant summary judgment against Fojtik because his own evidence (leaving on passes, voluntarily returning, never insisting on leaving) failed to raise a fact issue on detention by threat

  • Exceptions: "Lack of authority of law" is more properly an affirmative defense (privilege) than a true element of the plaintiff's prima facie case, per the casebook's Note 3

 

Rule: "Just Fear of Injury" Standard for Threat-Based Detention

  • Source: Case holding, Fojtik v. Charter Medical Corp

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Where a detention is alleged to be effected by a threat, the plaintiff must demonstrate that the threat was such as would inspire in the threatened person a just fear of injury to person, reputation, or property; threats to call the police are not ordinarily sufficient by themselves; factors such as relative size, age, experience, sex, and physical demeanor of the participants are considered

  • Legal Question: When does a threat (as opposed to physical restraint) rise to the level of confinement for false imprisonment?

  • Requirements:

    1. A threat inspiring a "just" (reasonable-under-the-circumstances) fear of injury

    2. Evaluated in light of participant characteristics such as relative size, age, experience, sex, and physical demeanor

  • Application: Applied to hold that Fojtik, a 45-year-old former business owner threatened only with police/commitment and no additional oppressive circumstances, did not raise a fact issue, unlike the vulnerable young plaintiffs in Black v. Kroger and Skillern & Sons v. Stewart

  • Exceptions: Threats of imprisonment alone, without other overbearing circumstances, are not enough (Safeway Stores v. Amburn); leaving and voluntarily returning negates a false imprisonment claim (Randall's Food Markets v. Johnson)

 

Rule: Confinement Without Actual Physical Restraint (Implicit Threats and False Legal Authority)

  • Source: Case holding, McCann v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc, applying Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 35, 40

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: While confinement can be imposed by physical barriers or force, much less will suffice: mere threats of physical force can be enough, threats may be implicit as well as explicit, and confinement can be based on a false assertion of legal authority to confine, or other forms of "duress"

  • Legal Question: Does false imprisonment require "actual, physical restraint," or can implicit threats and false claims of authority suffice?

  • Requirements: Conduct (words, actions, presence) sufficient to induce a reasonable person to believe either that she would be physically restrained if she tried to leave, or that the defendant was lawfully entitled to confine her until authorities arrived

  • Application: Applied to affirm the jury verdict for the McCanns because Wal-Mart employees blocked their path, directed them to come along, invoked the (false) claim that police were being called, and stood guard, all inducing a reasonable belief of confinement despite no actual touching beyond possibly a hand on the cart

  • Exceptions: The First Circuit rejected reading isolated Maine case language requiring "actual, physical restraint" literally, distinguishing Knowlton (where the plaintiff entered voluntarily, was not told she couldn't leave, and was not physically stopped) as a case of insufficient confinement, not a rule requiring literal physical restraint in all cases

 

Rule: Minimal Intent Requirement for Trespass to Land

  • Source: Textbook narrative, citing Restatement (Second) of Torts § 164

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: The intent necessary to commit trespass to land is quite minimal — only the act of entering the land must be intended, not entry specifically onto another's land; one who enters another's land under a mistaken but reasonable belief that it is his own or public land is nonetheless liable for trespass, though this element carries a strict-liability-like quality

  • Legal Question: Must a trespasser know or intend that the land belongs to someone else?

  • Requirements: Intent only to perform the physical act of entry (or to cause an invasion, e.g., via drone or third party), regardless of belief about ownership

  • Application: Distinguishes trespass (intentional tort with minimal intent) from cases of mere negligent entry (e.g., skidding off the road through a fence — actionable, if at all, only in negligence) or non-negligent accidental entry (e.g., a non-negligent stumble onto another's land — no liability at all)

  • Exceptions: Negligent (non-intentional) entry is not trespass; and a wholly non-negligent, non-intentional entry is not actionable under either theory

 

Rule: Nominal Damages Available Regardless of Actual Harm or Good Faith

  • Source: Textbook narrative

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Once a trespass to land is shown, the plaintiff has a cause of action for at least nominal damages regardless of whether the property suffered actual harm and regardless of the defendant's good faith

  • Legal Question: Must a trespass plaintiff prove actual damage to recover?

  • Requirements: Proof of the trespass itself (unauthorized intentional entry) is sufficient

  • Application: Reflects the high social value placed on the inviolability of real property and, particularly, the home, and functions to discourage self-help/breach of the peace

  • Exceptions: Where bad faith is shown, punitive damages may also be available, which can make an otherwise nominal-damages suit economically worthwhile

 

Rule: Trespass by Exceeding Scope of License or Overstaying Welcome

  • Source: Textbook narrative

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: A trespass arises when a person, although initially on another's land lawfully, overstays her welcome after permission is revoked, strays into areas of the land to which she was not invited, or engages in behavior exceeding her license

  • Legal Question: Can a lawful entrant become a trespasser without a new physical entry?

  • Requirements: Initial lawful presence, followed by conduct outside the scope of the granted permission (remaining after revocation, exceeding the area or purpose of the license)

  • Application: Illustrated by examples: refusing to remove a car from a garage after permission is revoked; a water meter reader peering into windows; a customer hiding to remain after closing; a party guest rummaging through a host's master bathroom cabinets

  • Exceptions: None stated

 

Rule: Trespass vs. Nuisance Distinction

  • Source: Case holding, John Larkin, Inc. v. Marceau, citing Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 158, 821D and Prosser & Keeton

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Trespass is an invasion of the plaintiff's interest in the exclusive possession of his land, while nuisance is an interference with his use and enjoyment of it; traditionally, trespass requires a direct and tangible invasion of property, while nuisance addresses indirect or intangible interference

  • Legal Question: How should courts classify claims based on the dispersion of invisible/intangible substances (e.g., pesticide drift) onto neighboring land?

  • Requirements: Trespass traditionally requires invasion by tangible matter interfering with exclusive possession; nuisance requires interference with use/enjoyment, which typically requires proof of actual and substantial injury

  • Application: Applied to reject Larkin's attempt to plead pesticide drift as trespass (to avoid Vermont's right-to-farm law, which only creates a rebuttable presumption against nuisance claims) absent any showing of physical impact on the land

  • Exceptions: Trespass and nuisance are not necessarily mutually exclusive and may overlap in the same case (Restatement (Second) § 821D cmt. e)

 

Rule: Traditional (Tangible-Invasion) View of Trespass

  • Source: Case law discussed in Larkin (Adams v. Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Co.; Maddy v. Vulcan Materials Co.)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Recovery for trespass is available only upon proof of an unauthorized direct or immediate intrusion of a physical, tangible object onto land over which the plaintiff has a right of exclusive possession

  • Legal Question: Should trespass liability ever extend to invasions by intangible or microscopic particles?

  • Requirements: Physical, tangible object; direct/immediate intrusion

  • Application: Adopted by some courts (e.g., Michigan in Adams) to preserve a clear line between trespass (automatic liability, nominal damages available) and nuisance (requires substantial injury)

  • Exceptions: Rejected by courts following the "modern trend" (below)

 

Rule: Modern Trend of Trespass for Intangible/Airborne Invasions

  • Source: Case law discussed in Larkin (Maddy v. Vulcan Materials Co.; Bradley v. American Smelting & Refining Co.; Borland v. Sanders Lead Co.)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Invasions of intangible matter (smoke, gas, microscopic particles) are actionable in trespass only if they cause substantial damage to the plaintiff's property, sufficient to be considered an infringement on the plaintiff's right to exclusive possession — this departs from traditional trespass rules by refusing to infer damages as a matter of law for intangible invasions

  • Legal Question: Under what circumstances can an intangible invasion (e.g., pesticide drift, transitory particles) support a trespass claim rather than only a nuisance claim?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intangible/airborne invasion of the plaintiff's land

    2. Substantial damage to the property demonstrating interference with exclusive possession

  • Application: The Vermont Supreme Court in Larkin declined to adopt a hard-line position either way, but held that Larkin's trespass claim failed regardless because it produced no evidence of any physical impact on its property from Marceau's pesticides

  • Exceptions: Bradley further distinguishes transitory particles that quickly dissipate (nuisance only) from particles that accumulate and do not pass away (trespass)

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 218 — Trespass to Chattels

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 218, referenced in textbook narrative

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Trespass to chattels covers the intentional physical interference with or destruction of personal property; the intent required is only intent toward the act affecting the property, and a good faith and reasonable belief that the chattel is one's own is no defense

  • Legal Question: What must a plaintiff show to recover for interference with (short of full conversion of) personal property?

  • Requirements:

    1. Intent to perform the act affecting the chattel

    2. Physical interference with or destruction of the chattel

  • Application: Illustrated by Ranson v. Kitner, where a defendant who reasonably but mistakenly shot a dog believing it a wolf was nonetheless liable

  • Exceptions: If the defendant merely intentionally dispossessed the plaintiff, nominal damages will lie; but if the defendant merely interfered with (without dispossessing) the chattel, the plaintiff must prove actual damages

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 222A — Conversion

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Torts § 222A, referenced in textbook narrative

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Statement: Conversion is the intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel which so seriously interferes with the right of another to control it that the actor may justly be required to pay the other the full value of the chattel

  • Legal Question: What distinguishes conversion from mere trespass to chattels, and what remedy follows from each?

  • Requirements: Exercise of dominion/control (unauthorized use, destruction, possession, or wrongful disposition) that seriously interferes with the plaintiff's right of control

  • Application: Where a chattel is destroyed, remedies under trespass to chattels and conversion converge (fair market value at time of destruction); where the chattel is not destroyed, trespass yields only diminution-in-value damages while conversion, if established, forces a "sale" at full market value — illustrated by Russell-Vaughn Ford v. Rouse (salesman mockingly withholding car keys)

  • Exceptions: In some jurisdictions "conversion" is merely a remedy (forced sale) for trespass to chattels rather than a standalone tort; unauthorized use short of a "serious violation" of the right to control does not rise to conversion

 

2. CASE NARRATIVE

Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Communications: Ahron Leichtman describes himself as a nationally known anti-smoking advocate. On the date of the Great American Smokeout, he was invited onto the WLW radio station in Cincinnati to appear on Bill Cunningham's talk show and discuss the harms of smoking and secondhand smoke. While Leichtman was in the studio for that purpose, a second WLW talk-show host, Andy Furman, lit a cigar and repeatedly blew smoke directly into Leichtman's face, allegedly at Cunningham's urging, and allegedly for the specific purpose of causing Leichtman physical discomfort, humiliation, and distress. Leichtman sued WLW, Cunningham, and Furman for battery, invasion of privacy, and violation of a Cincinnati Board of Health smoking regulation, seeking damages for what he experienced as a deliberate, hostile stunt carried out on-air in front of an audience.

Dickens v. Puryear: For a period of time, plaintiff Dickens, a thirty-one-year-old man, had a sexual and drug-and-alcohol-involved relationship with the seventeen-year-old daughter of defendants (the Puryears). On April 2, 1975, the defendants lured Dickens out to a rural area of Johnston County, North Carolina. When he arrived, defendant Earl Puryear, the girl's father, identified himself and pointed a pistol between Dickens's eyes while shouting for others to come out. Four masked men armed with nightsticks then emerged and beat Dickens into semi-consciousness, handcuffed him to farm machinery, and continued striking him. Earl Puryear brandished a knife, cut Dickens's hair, and threatened to castrate him. During several breaks in the roughly two-hour ordeal, the group discussed — within Dickens's hearing — and voted on whether to kill or castrate him. Eventually they stopped, and Earl Puryear told Dickens to go home, disconnect his phone, pack, and leave North Carolina, warning that he would otherwise be killed. Dickens was then released. He waited nearly three years (filing March 31, 1978) before suing, framing his claim as intentional infliction of mental distress rather than assault and battery, apparently to take advantage of the longer three-year limitations period that applied to the former rather than the one-year period applicable to the latter.

Singer v. Marx: On September 13, 1953, nine-year-old Tim Marx (son of Zeppo and Marion Marx) was on the front lawn of the Singer residence with eight-year-old Denise Singer, while another eight-year-old, Barbara Corcoran, rode her bicycle nearby. The children were not playing any organized game; Tim had been throwing rocks into the street, bragging about how far he could throw. As Barbara rode onto the Singer property, Tim, looking at Denise, said "watch Barbie" and threw a rock — the flight path of which would have passed several feet in front of Denise had it been aimed at Barbara as claimed. Instead, the rock struck Denise in the eye, injuring her. There was conflicting evidence whether Tim deliberately changed his throw's direction, threw at Barbara and missed (hitting Denise instead), or negligently released the rock so that it went astray. Separately, Denise's father and neighbors had, according to trial evidence, observed a pattern of Tim (and his younger brother) habitually throwing rocks and clods at people and cars, including an incident where Tim threw clods at his school principal's car — evidence going to whether Tim's parents knew of and negligently failed to control his dangerous habit.

Halberstam v. Welch: Over a roughly five-year period, Bernard Welch supported himself and his live-in partner, Linda Hamilton, through an extensive campaign of nighttime residential burglaries in the Washington, D.C. area. Hamilton, a young secretary when she met Welch in a parking lot in October 1975, moved in with him weeks later; Welch claimed to be an investor in real estate, coins, and jewelry. In reality, Welch left the house nearly every evening for hours, always alone, while Hamilton stayed home; she claims she never asked exactly what he was doing and assumed it involved checking on investments or dealing with coin/jewelry dealers. Over the following years the couple's wealth exploded — houses in Minnesota and a $1,000,000 home in Great Falls, Virginia, multiple Mercedes, a housekeeper — funded by Welch melting stolen gold and silver into ingots in their home garage and selling them, with the proceeds funneled through Hamilton's own bank accounts, tax returns (falsely reporting fictitious "cost of goods" deductions), and secretarial paperwork. Hamilton typed transmittal letters, kept inventories, and had free access to (and once provided police the key to) a locked basement where, after Welch's eventual arrest in December 1980, police discovered roughly fifty boxes containing some three thousand stolen items. On December 5, 1980, during one such burglary, Welch shot and killed Dr. Michael Halberstam, a prominent physician, in the course of the crime. Halberstam's widow brought a wrongful death/tort suit not just against Welch (the shooter) but also against Hamilton, on the theory that her long-running, knowing financial and logistical support of Welch's criminal enterprise made her legally responsible for the killing that resulted from it.

McCann v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.: On December 11, 1996, Debra McCann and two of her children, 16-year-old Jillian and 12-year-old Jonathan, were shopping at a Wal-Mart in Bangor, Maine, returning a Christmas tree, exchanging a CD player, and browsing for roughly an hour and a half, with a receipt time-stamped 10:10 p.m. As they tried to leave, two Wal-Mart employees, Jean Taylor and Karla Hughes, stepped in front of their shopping cart (Taylor possibly touching the cart) and told Debra McCann that her children were barred from the store because they had previously been caught shoplifting — a claim that was simply mistaken, since the employees had confused the McCanns with a different family whose son had shoplifted two weeks earlier. Despite Debra's protests and attempt to show identification (which Taylor refused to look at), the employees insisted the family "had to go with her," said records existed and police were being called, escorted the McCanns to an area near the exit, and stood over them while supposedly calling police — though they actually called only an internal store security officer who could identify the real prior shoplifter. During roughly an hour of this, Jonathan was accused directly of having stolen before (making him cry and deny it), and when he said he needed to use the bathroom, he was told he could not go. The McCanns were never told they were free to leave during this period. Eventually the security officer arrived, confirmed the McCanns were not the family in question, Hughes acknowledged the mistake, and the family was allowed to leave — at approximately 11:15 p.m., over an hour after they had first tried to exit.

Fojtik v. Charter Medical Corp.: Felix Fojtik, a 45-year-old man who had run several businesses, was the subject of a planned "intervention" arranged with Charter Medical Corporation staff (Dorrill Nabours and Valerie Bullock), during which his family and friends confronted him and told him he needed inpatient treatment for alcoholism at a Charter hospital. Fojtik's own medical records reflect that he told Charter staff he was admitting himself only because those running the intervention had warned that, if he did not go voluntarily, they would have him involuntarily committed and brought in wearing handcuffs. Upon admission Fojtik was angry, refused to be photographed, and refused to consent to being contacted after discharge. During his stay, he repeatedly requested passes to leave, which were initially denied because he was not yet "eligible," and he complained that he felt he was "locked up and couldn't get away." He later received passes for a few hours at a time and always voluntarily returned on schedule, explaining that he had "vowed" to follow all the rules — a vow he later argued on appeal was motivated only by a hope that obedience would speed his release. Charter's evidence, by contrast, was that Fojtik was free to leave at any time and that, although the facility preferred patients follow its pass procedures, it would let a patient leave if he insisted on doing so without following those procedures; Fojtik's own admission paperwork indicated he consented to inpatient treatment.

John Larkin, Inc. v. Marceau: John Larkin, Inc. and the Larkin Family Partnership are real estate developers who, in 2001, purchased undeveloped land adjoining property owned by J. Edward Marceau, Jr., with the intention of building a residential development; the property they bought had previously belonged to Marceau's former spouse. To increase the density permitted for their planned development, Larkin later also obtained a transfer of development rights from Marceau's own property and from another adjoining parcel. Marceau, meanwhile, operated an apple orchard on his land and, as part of that farming operation, sprayed pesticides. Larkin came to believe that winds carried detectable levels of those pesticides onto Larkin's adjoining land, and in March 2005 filed suit against Marceau seeking injunctive relief and compensatory damages, framing the claim specifically as trespass (rather than nuisance) — a strategic choice apparently made because Vermont's right-to-farm statute creates only a rebuttable presumption that agricultural activities are not a nuisance, a protection that would not apply to a straightforward trespass claim.

Case Name: Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Communications, Inc., Court of Appeals of Ohio, First District, 1994, Per Curiam

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • Leichtman, a self-described nationally known anti-smoking advocate, was invited onto WLW's Bill Cunningham radio talk show on the date of the Great American Smokeout to discuss the harms of smoking and secondhand smoke

    • While Leichtman was in the studio, Andy Furman, another WLW host, lit a cigar and repeatedly blew smoke in Leichtman's face

    • Leichtman alleged Furman did so "for the purpose of causing physical discomfort, humiliation and distress"

    • Leichtman's complaint alleged that Furman acted "[a]t Defendant Cunningham's urging"

    • Leichtman sued WLW, Cunningham, and Furman for battery, invasion of privacy, and violation of a Cincinnati Board of Health smoking regulation

  • Procedural Posture

    • Trial court (name not specified in the opinion): dismissed Leichtman's complaint for battery, invasion of privacy, and violation of the Cincinnati Board of Health regulation, on defendants' Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motion

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Leichtman sought review in the Court of Appeals of Ohio, First District, on the grounds that his complaint was sufficient to state a claim upon which relief could be granted and the trial court erred in granting the Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motion

    • Court of Appeals of Ohio, First District: Leichtman is the appellant; WLW Jacor Communications, Cunningham, and Furman are the appellees

  • Issue: Whether Leichtman's complaint sufficiently stated a claim for battery, invasion of privacy, and violation of the health regulation to survive a Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motion to dismiss?

  • Holding: The Court of Appeals of Ohio held that the battery claim was sufficient to survive dismissal as to all three defendants, but affirmed dismissal of the first and third counts (invasion of privacy and the health regulation violation)

  • Rule: Restatement (Second) of Torts Battery Definition — an actor is liable for battery if he intends to cause a harmful or offensive contact with another and such contact results

  • Reasoning: The Ohio Court of Appeals reasoned as follows:

    • Tobacco smoke is "particulate matter" with physical properties capable of making contact

    • Contact offensive to a reasonable sense of personal dignity is offensive contact, and Furman's deliberate blowing of smoke into Leichtman's face met that standard

    • No matter how trivial, a battery is actionable even for only one dollar in damages, quoting Roscoe Pound's rationale that civilized society requires assurance that others will commit no intentional aggressions

    • The court declined to adopt a broader "smoker's battery" theory based on substantial certainty of contact, because Leichtman's claim rested on Furman's deliberate act, not passive/secondhand smoke

    • Cunningham could be liable because one who is present and encourages or incites a battery by words can be equally liable as a principal, and the complaint alleged Furman acted at Cunningham's urging

    • WLW's liability under respondeat superior turned on the factual question whether Furman's conduct was within the scope of his employment, which is ordinarily a jury question

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Leichtman argued that Furman's intentional and deliberate act of blowing smoke in his face, done at Cunningham's urging and for the purpose of causing him discomfort, humiliation, and distress, satisfied all elements of battery under Ohio common law and the Restatement

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: The defendants argued (implicitly, via their successful trial-court motion and the "glass cage" defense they relied on) that passive or secondhand smoke exposure does not constitute an offensive contact and urged application of the McCracken v. Sloan "glass cage" defense; the appellate court found this argument irrelevant because Leichtman's claim rested on Furman's deliberate act, not passive exposure

  • Disposition: The Court of Appeals of Ohio affirmed the trial court's dismissal of the first (invasion of privacy) and third (health regulation) counts, but reversed the dismissal of the battery count and remanded for further proceedings on that claim only

  • Importance: This is the primary battery case for the week — it establishes that even trivial, non-injurious offensive contact (blown smoke) satisfies battery, illustrates the "offensive contact" standard, shows how bystander encouragement and employer liability theories attach to a battery claim, and sets up the single-intent/dual-intent debate addressed in the Notes via the Restatement Third § 1

 

Case Name: Dickens v. Puryear, Supreme Court of North Carolina, 1981, Exum, Justice

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • Dickens, a 31-year-old man, had shared sex, alcohol, and marijuana with the 17-year-old daughter of defendants the Puryears

    • On April 2, 1975, defendants lured Dickens into rural Johnston County, North Carolina

    • Earl Puryear pointed a pistol between Dickens's eyes and shouted for others to come out; four masked, armed men then beat Dickens into semi-consciousness and handcuffed him to farm machinery

    • Earl Puryear brandished a knife, cut Dickens's hair, and threatened castration; the group discussed and voted, within Dickens's hearing, on whether to kill or castrate him

    • After about two hours, Earl Puryear told Dickens to go home, disconnect his phone, pack, and leave North Carolina, or be killed, then released him

    • Dickens filed his complaint March 31, 1978 — more than one year but less than three years after the incident — labeling his claim as intentional infliction of mental distress

  • Procedural Posture

    • North Carolina Superior Court, Johnston County (Judge Braswell presiding): granted summary judgment for defendants before any answer was due or filed, on the ground that plaintiff's claim was actually for assault and battery and thus barred by the one-year statute of limitations, N.C. Gen. Stat. § 1-54(3)

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Dickens sought review in the North Carolina Court of Appeals on the grounds that his claim was properly one for intentional infliction of mental distress, subject to the three-year limitations period, not assault and battery

    • North Carolina Court of Appeals: affirmed, holding that even the final threat (to kill plaintiff if he did not leave the state) was "an immediate threat of harmful and offensive contact," i.e., an assault, and thus that the entire claim was barred by the one-year statute

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Dickens sought review in the Supreme Court of North Carolina on the grounds that the Court of Appeals mischaracterized the final threat, which was a threat of future harm and thus not an assault

    • Supreme Court of North Carolina: Dickens is the appellant; the Puryear defendants are the appellees

  • Issue: Whether the final threat — that Earl Puryear would kill Dickens if he did not leave the state — constituted an assault (barred by the one-year statute of limitations) or was instead actionable, if at all, as intentional infliction of mental distress (governed by the three-year statute)?

  • Holding: The Supreme Court of North Carolina held that the physical beatings, hair-cutting, and threats of immediate castration/death were batteries and assaults barred by the one-year statute, but that the final threat of future death was not an assault because it threatened harm in the future rather than imminent contact, and was therefore actionable, if at all, only as intentional infliction of mental distress under the three-year statute

  • Rule: Common Law Definition of Assault — an assault is an offer to show violence to another without striking him, protecting freedom from apprehension of imminent (not future) harmful or offensive contact

  • Reasoning: The North Carolina Supreme Court reasoned as follows:

    • North Carolina follows common law and Restatement (Second) §§ 29(1) and 31 principles, under which the apprehension created must be of imminent contact, not contact in the future

    • "Imminent" does not mean instantaneous but means there will be no significant delay

    • The physical beatings and hair-cutting were batteries, and the threats of immediate castration/death during the confrontation were assaults, because they created apprehension of immediate harmful or offensive contact

    • The final threat — leave the state or be killed — was conditioned on a future contingency and threatened harm only if plaintiff failed to comply over time, making it a threat for the future rather than an imminent one

    • Threats for the future are "simply not present breaches of the peace" and thus fall outside assault's boundaries, but may be actionable as intentional infliction of mental distress

    • The nature of an action is determined by the facts shown and relief sought, not by the plaintiff's chosen label, so most of Dickens's claim (though labeled IIED) was properly recharacterized as time-barred assault/battery

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Dickens argued his claim was for intentional infliction of mental distress, not assault and battery, and that even if some incidents were assaults/batteries, the final threat of future death was not imminent and thus was independently actionable as IIED within the three-year period

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: The Puryears argued that all of Dickens's factual allegations, including the final threat, described assault and battery, and that the entire suit — regardless of its label — was therefore barred by the one-year statute of limitations

  • Disposition: The Supreme Court of North Carolina reversed in part (as to the final threat of future death, allowing that portion of the claim to proceed as IIED) and affirmed in part (as to the beatings, hair-cutting, and immediate threats, which were time-barred assault/battery)

  • Importance: This is the primary assault case for the week — it draws the critical line between assault (imminent threatened contact) and non-actionable-as-assault threats of future harm, illustrates that courts look past a plaintiff's chosen tort label to the underlying facts, and demonstrates how the choice of tort label can determine which statute of limitations applies

 

Case Name: Singer v. Marx, District Court of Appeal of California, 1956, Ashburn, Justice

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • On September 13, 1953, nine-year-old Tim Marx, eight-year-old Denise Singer, and eight-year-old Barbara Corcoran were on/near the front lawn of the Singer residence

    • Tim had been throwing rocks into the street and talking about how far he could throw; neither girl threw any rocks

    • As Barbara rode her bicycle onto the Singer property, Tim, looking at Denise, said "watch Barbie," raised his arm, and threw a rock that struck Denise in the eye

    • The throw's trajectory toward Barbara would have passed several feet in front of Denise, meaning either Tim changed direction without warning or held/released the rock in a way that caused it to miscarry

    • Evidence at trial suggested Tim had a history ("penchant") for throwing rocks at people, including throwing clods at his school principal's car, of which his mother, Marion Marx, had been informed

    • There was no similar evidence that Tim's father, Zeppo Marx, had personal knowledge of Tim's rock-throwing proclivities

  • Procedural Posture

    • California Superior Court (county not specified in the opinion): granted a nonsuit in favor of defendants Tim Marx, Zeppo Marx, and Marion Marx as to both plaintiffs (Denise Singer and her father) and all causes of action

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiffs-Appellants Denise Singer and her father sought review in the District Court of Appeal of California on the grounds that the evidence was sufficient to support claims for battery/negligence against Tim and negligent supervision against his parents

    • District Court of Appeal of California: Singer (plaintiffs) are the appellants; the Marx defendants are the appellees

  • Issue: Whether the evidence was sufficient to withstand a nonsuit on plaintiff's battery claim against Tim Marx (including via transferred intent) and on the negligent supervision claims against his parents?

  • Holding: The District Court of Appeal of California held that the nonsuit was erroneous as to Tim (battery/transferred intent) and as to Marion Marx (negligent supervision), but that the nonsuit was properly affirmed as to Zeppo Marx (no evidence of personal knowledge of Tim's dangerous proclivities)

  • Rule: Transferred Intent — if a defendant unlawfully aims at one person and hits another, he is liable for battery to the party actually hit, as a direct, natural, and probable consequence of the wrongful act

  • Reasoning: The California District Court of Appeal reasoned as follows:

    • An infant is liable for his torts even lacking capacity to recognize wrongfulness, so long as he has the mental capacity to form the specific intent required for the tort charged (citing Ellis v. D'Angelo); this differs from negligence, which requires capacity to appreciate that heedless conduct might foreseeably injure another

    • It could not be said as a matter of law that Tim lacked capacity to intend a harmful contact; if he told both girls he was throwing at Barbara and instead hit Denise, he was liable to Denise via transferred intent

    • The evidence supported multiple reasonable inferences all pointing toward liability (deliberate throw at Denise, transferred intent from an errant throw at Barbara, or negligent release), and where evidence supports several inferences all favoring the plaintiff, the jury — not the court on nonsuit — should choose among them

    • As to Marion Marx, testimony that she was informed by Tim's school principal that Tim threw clods at her car, plus a neighbor's testimony about Tim's habitual rock-throwing, was sufficient to raise a jury question on her negligent failure to control a child with known dangerous proclivities

    • As to Zeppo Marx, the only evidence was that he had been consulted about punishing Tim for the clod-throwing incident, which was insufficient to show his personal knowledge of the ongoing proclivity

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Plaintiffs argued Tim intentionally struck Denise, or alternatively struck her via a transferred-intent-eligible errant throw at Barbara, and that Tim's parents were negligent in failing to control his known habit of throwing rocks/clods at people

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Defendants argued that the evidence was equally susceptible to inferences favorable and unfavorable to plaintiffs and thus insufficient to go to the jury, and that there was no evidence either parent had the requisite knowledge of Tim's dangerous proclivities to support negligent supervision liability

  • Disposition: The District Court of Appeal of California reversed the nonsuit as to Tim Marx and as to Marion Marx, and affirmed the nonsuit as to Zeppo Marx

  • Importance: This case is the vehicle for two doctrines: (1) that young children can possess sufficient capacity to intend a battery even though incapable of negligence, and (2) the doctrine of transferred intent, under which intent aimed at one victim transfers to satisfy the intent element for an unintended victim actually struck; it also illustrates that parents are not automatically vicariously liable for a child's torts absent proof of their own negligent supervision

 

Case Name: Halberstam v. Welch, United States Court of Appeals, D.C. Circuit, 1983, Wald, Circuit Judge (with Bork and Scalia)

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • Bernard Welch conducted a five-year campaign of residential burglaries in the Washington, D.C. area, accumulating a fortune through stolen goods

    • Linda Hamilton met Welch in October 1975 and moved in with him weeks later; Welch had virtually no legitimate assets or employment

    • Welch left the house four to five evenings a week between roughly 5:00 and 9:30 p.m. throughout the five years they lived together; Hamilton never accompanied him and claimed she never had a full discussion with him about where he went

    • The couple's wealth grew enormously — a $102,000 Minnesota house (Hamilton contributing $20,000, titled in her name), a $1,000,000 Great Falls, Virginia home, two Mercedes, a housekeeper — funded by melting stolen gold/silver into bars in their home garage and selling the ingots

    • Hamilton typed transmittal letters, kept inventories, deposited buyer checks into her own bank accounts, and reported the resulting income (and fictitious "cost of goods" deductions) on her personal tax returns, which in 1978-79 reflected gross earnings exceeding $1,000,000 combined

    • Hamilton had free access to, and once provided police a key to, a locked basement where police later found roughly fifty boxes containing about three thousand stolen items

    • On December 5, 1980, Welch shot and killed Dr. Michael Halberstam during a burglary of the Halberstam home

  • Procedural Posture

    • U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia: entered judgment for plaintiff (Halberstam's estate/widow) against defendant Hamilton, holding her liable for Michael Halberstam's death on theories of civil conspiracy and aiding-abetting/joint venture, following findings based largely on Hamilton's own trial testimony

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Hamilton sought review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit on the grounds that the evidence was insufficient to establish that she agreed to, or knowingly and substantially assisted, Welch's burglary enterprise, and that she could not be held liable for a killing she neither participated in nor knew about in advance

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit: Hamilton is the appellant; Halberstam (plaintiff, representing the decedent's estate) is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether Hamilton, who did not personally commit any burglary or the killing, could be held civilly liable for Welch's killing of Halberstam under theories of civil conspiracy and/or aiding-abetting?

  • Holding: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit held that Hamilton was liable under both a civil conspiracy theory and an aiding-abetting theory, because she knowingly and substantially assisted Welch's ongoing criminal enterprise over five years and the killing, though not specifically planned or known to her, was a reasonably foreseeable act in furtherance of that enterprise

  • Rule: Restatement (Second) § 876 — a person is liable for harm resulting from another's tortious conduct if she acts in concert pursuant to a common design (conspiracy) or knowingly gives substantial assistance/encouragement to a tortfeasor's breach of duty (aiding-abetting)

  • Reasoning: The D.C. Circuit reasoned as follows:

    • Under the "clearly erroneous" standard, the district court's inferences that Hamilton knew of Welch's illegal activity were amply supported: unaccompanied nightly absences for five years, boxes of loot, gold/silver smelting, sudden unexplained wealth, all transactions funneled through Hamilton, and collusive/false tax filings

    • An agreement (even tacit) between Hamilton and Welch to pursue the criminal enterprise could reasonably be inferred from their symbiotic division of labor (Welch stealing, Hamilton laundering/bookkeeping) over a long, continuous period at the same shared location

    • Applying the § 876(b) factors, Hamilton's assistance (bookkeeping, banking, recordkeeping, secretarial services) was substantial given the nature of the burglary/fencing enterprise, notwithstanding her absence from the burglaries themselves and her relationship as housemate (given low weight to avoid over-penalizing normal domestic support)

    • Her knowing, five-year-long, deliberate participation reflected a state of mind and duration supporting substantial assistance

    • Once a conspiracy or substantial-assistance relationship is established, a defendant may be liable for a co-actor's other reasonably foreseeable acts in furtherance of the common enterprise, even without specific knowledge of or participation in those acts (as with Welch's killing during a burglary escape)

    • Violence and killing were a foreseeable risk of a burglary/fencing/property-crime enterprise, regardless whether Hamilton knew specifically that Welch was committing burglaries as opposed to some other form of personal property crime

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Plaintiff (Halberstam's estate) argued that Hamilton's years-long, knowing, and substantial financial/logistical support of Welch's criminal enterprise made her jointly liable for all foreseeable harms flowing from that enterprise, including the killing

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Hamilton argued she was an unknowing, passive homemaker who never personally burglarized anyone, was not present at the killing, did not agree to or know about any specific criminal act, and should not be held responsible for Welch's violent act

  • Disposition: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court's judgment holding Hamilton liable

  • Importance: This is the central case for transferred/participation liability doctrine — it carefully distinguishes civil conspiracy (agreement-based) from aiding-abetting (substantial-assistance-based), lays out the elements and multi-factor "substantial assistance" test for each, and establishes that a participating defendant can be liable for reasonably foreseeable acts of a co-actor even without direct participation in or knowledge of the specific harmful act

 

Case Name: McCann v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., United States Court of Appeals, First Circuit, 2000, Boudin, Circuit Judge

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • On December 11, 1996, Debra McCann and her children Jillian (16) and Jonathan (12) shopped at a Wal-Mart in Bangor, Maine, for about an hour and a half, with a receipt time-stamped 10:10 p.m.

    • As they left, Wal-Mart employees Jean Taylor and Karla Hughes stepped in front of the McCanns' cart (Taylor may have touched the cart) and told Debra McCann her children were not allowed in the store because they had been caught stealing on a prior occasion

    • The employees were in fact mistaken, having confused the McCanns with a different family

    • Taylor told the McCanns they "had to go with her," that Wal-Mart had records, and that police were being called; the McCanns were escorted to an area near the exit and guarded while a store employee supposedly called police (actually calling only an internal store security officer)

    • Debra McCann tried unsuccessfully to show identification; Hughes pointed at Jonathan and accused him directly of the prior theft, making him cry and deny it; when Jonathan said he needed the bathroom, he was told he could not go

    • The McCanns were never told they were free to leave during the roughly hour-long detention; eventually the security officer arrived, confirmed the mistake, and the McCanns left at approximately 11:15 p.m.

    • The jury awarded the McCanns $20,000 in compensatory damages on the false imprisonment claim (and rejected a companion defamation claim)

  • Procedural Posture

    • U.S. District Court for the District of Maine: jury returned a verdict for the McCanns on false imprisonment, awarding $20,000 in compensatory damages, and found for Wal-Mart on the defamation claim, after which the court denied Wal-Mart's post-judgment motions for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b) and for a new trial under Rule 59

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Wal-Mart sought review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit on the grounds that the McCanns had not proved false imprisonment under Maine law because there was no "actual, physical restraint," and that the district court's jury instructions on false imprisonment were erroneous

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit: Wal-Mart is the appellant; the McCanns are the appellees

  • Issue: Whether a reasonable jury could find that the McCanns were "confined" for purposes of false imprisonment under Maine law absent any actual physical restraint, based instead on implicit threats and a false assertion of authority to detain them?

  • Holding: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit held that a reasonable jury could find confinement based on the Wal-Mart employees' conduct, and affirmed the denial of Wal-Mart's post-judgment motions

  • Rule: Confinement Without Actual Physical Restraint (Implicit Threats and False Legal Authority) — confinement can be established by mere threats (explicit or implicit) of physical force, or by a false assertion of legal authority to confine, without requiring actual physical restraint

  • Reasoning: The First Circuit reasoned as follows:

    • Under Restatement (Second) of Torts § 35 and the Maine case law, confinement can be imposed by physical barriers or force, but much less will suffice, including threats (explicit or implicit) and false claims of legal authority to confine, or other forms of duress

    • The evidence, viewed favorably to the McCanns, showed the employees blocked their exit, directed them to come along, invoked the (false) claim that police were being summoned, and continuously stood over/guarded them, including telling Jonathan he could not leave to use the bathroom

    • This combination was enough to induce reasonable people to believe either that they would be physically restrained if they tried to leave, or that the store was claiming lawful authority to confine them until police arrived, or both

    • Wal-Mart's reliance on isolated language in Knowlton requiring "actual, physical restraint" took that phrase out of context: in Knowlton, the plaintiff was never told she couldn't leave, was not physically stopped, and entered the room voluntarily — the phrase there simply meant the plaintiff must be actually confined, not that only physical touching qualifies

    • Reading "actual, physical restraint" literally would put Maine law at odds with the Restatement and the near-uniform body of common law accepting mere threats or false claims of authority as sufficient

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: The McCanns argued that the employees' conduct — blocking their exit, ordering them to come along, invoking the police, and standing guard over them (including denying a child bathroom access) — was sufficient to establish confinement even without physical touching

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Wal-Mart argued that Maine law required "actual, physical restraint" for false imprisonment, that no such restraint occurred, and that the jury instructions improperly allowed the jury to find confinement based on lesser conduct

  • Disposition: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit affirmed the district court's judgment for the McCanns

  • Importance: This case illustrates that false imprisonment does not require physical touching or even explicit verbal threats — implicit threats combined with a false assertion of authority (invoking the police, blocking an exit, standing guard) are sufficient, and shows a plaintiff-favorable application of the "confinement" element, contrasting with the plaintiff-unfavorable result in Fojtik

 

Case Name: Fojtik v. Charter Medical Corp., Court of Appeals of Texas, 1999, Chavez, Justice

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • Fojtik's admission to Charter followed an "intervention" by Charter staff (Nabours and Bullock) and Fojtik's family/friends, who confronted him and told him he needed inpatient alcohol treatment

    • Fojtik's medical records show he told Charter staff he admitted himself only because the intervention group threatened that, if he did not go voluntarily, he would be involuntarily committed and brought in wearing handcuffs

    • Upon admission, Fojtik was angry, refused to be photographed, and refused to consent to post-discharge contact

    • Fojtik repeatedly requested passes to leave, initially denied as he was not yet "eligible"; he complained he was getting a "raw deal" because he felt "locked up and couldn't get away"

    • He later received several-hour passes and always voluntarily returned, having "vowed" to follow all the rules (a vow he argued on appeal was motivated by hope of faster release)

    • Charter's summary judgment evidence showed Fojtik was free to leave at any time; while Charter preferred patients follow pass procedures, it would let an insistent patient leave without following them; Fojtik's admission documents indicated he consented to inpatient treatment

  • Procedural Posture

    • Texas trial court (name and county not specified in the opinion): granted summary judgment for defendant Charter Medical Corporation on Fojtik's false imprisonment claim

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Fojtik sought review in the Court of Appeals of Texas on the grounds that genuine issues of material fact existed regarding whether he was falsely imprisoned during his stay at Charter's alcohol treatment hospital

    • Court of Appeals of Texas: Fojtik is the appellant; Charter Medical Corporation is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether the summary judgment evidence raised a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Fojtik was detained against his will by threats or other means sufficient to constitute false imprisonment?

  • Holding: The Court of Appeals of Texas held that the summary judgment evidence did not raise a fact issue on false imprisonment, and affirmed summary judgment for Charter

  • Rule: Elements of False Imprisonment (Texas) — willful detention, without consent, without authority of law, which may be accomplished by violence, threats, or any other restraining means; where based on threat, the threat must inspire a "just fear of injury," evaluated by factors including relative size, age, experience, sex, and physical demeanor

  • Reasoning: The Texas Court of Appeals reasoned as follows:

    • Comparing prior case law, plaintiffs who recovered for threat-based false imprisonment (Black v. Kroger, Skillern & Sons v. Stewart) were vulnerable, young, inexperienced individuals subjected to intimidating, harsh, or physically coercive interrogation-like circumstances

    • By contrast, in Safeway Stores v. Amburn and Randall's Food Markets v. Johnson, mere threats of police/jail, without other overbearing circumstances, or a plaintiff's ability to leave and voluntarily return, were insufficient

    • None of the relevant factors (relative size, age, experience, sex, physical demeanor) favored Fojtik: he was a 45-year-old man who had run several businesses, not a young or inexperienced person

    • He was not physically restrained; he was threatened only with commitment/police, with no other intimidating circumstances; and he left the facility on passes and voluntarily returned each time, like the plaintiff in Randall's Food Markets

    • The proper inquiry is not Fojtik's subjective belief that he was restrained (his comments about being "locked up"), but whether he had an objectively "just fear" of injury — and the record showed he never actually insisted on leaving

    • Charter's use of the "alcoholic" label and its pass system did not constitute restraint; if anything, the pass system (letting him leave and voluntarily return) undermined rather than supported his claim

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Fojtik argued that the threat of forcible commitment in handcuffs that induced his admission, combined with his felt sense of being "locked up," his repeated requests for passes, and the psychological effect of being constantly treated as an alcoholic, created a fact issue on whether he was falsely imprisoned

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Charter argued it was undisputed that Fojtik was free to leave at any time, that patients who insisted on leaving without following pass procedures were permitted to do so, and that Fojtik's own conduct (never insisting on leaving, voluntarily returning from passes, signing consent to treatment) negated any claim of detention

  • Disposition: The Court of Appeals of Texas affirmed the trial court's summary judgment in favor of Charter

  • Importance: Paired with McCann, this case shows the line-drawing courts perform on threat-based confinement claims — it demonstrates that a plaintiff's subjective feeling of being confined is not enough, that personal characteristics (age, sophistication, size) matter to whether a threat is objectively intimidating, and that a plaintiff's pattern of voluntary compliance and return undercuts a false imprisonment claim

 

Case Name: John Larkin, Inc. v. Marceau, Supreme Court of Vermont, 2008, Johnson, J.

  • Document: 40-88.txt

  • Course: Torts

  • Facts

    • Larkin purchased undeveloped land adjoining Marceau's property in 2001 to build a residential development, and later obtained a transfer of development rights from Marceau's own parcel and another adjoining parcel to increase permitted density

    • Marceau operated an apple orchard on his adjoining land and sprayed pesticides as part of that operation

    • Larkin alleged that winds carried detectable levels of pesticides onto its property, and sued Marceau in March 2005 seeking injunctive and compensatory relief, framing the claim as trespass rather than nuisance

    • Vermont's right-to-farm law, 12 V.S.A. §§ 5751-5754, creates a rebuttable presumption that agricultural activities are not a nuisance, but does not by its terms apply to trespass claims

    • Larkin failed, during discovery, to produce any evidence of the extent of pesticide dispersion onto its property, any physical impact on the property, or any safety/health concerns related to the pesticide use

  • Procedural Posture

    • Vermont Superior Court (county not specified in the opinion): entered final judgment in favor of Marceau on Larkin's trespass claim, on summary judgment, following the parties' stipulated dismissal of any nuisance-based claims

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiffs-Appellants John Larkin, Inc. and Larkin Family Partnership sought review in the Supreme Court of Vermont on the grounds that the superior court erred by recharacterizing their trespass action as a nuisance action subject to the right-to-farm law, and that the right-to-farm law does not insulate farmers from trespass liability

    • Supreme Court of Vermont: Larkin is the appellant; Marceau is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether the dispersion of airborne pesticide particulates onto neighboring land, without any demonstrated physical impact on that land, can support a claim for trespass rather than only nuisance?

  • Holding: The Supreme Court of Vermont held that, absent any demonstrated physical impact on Larkin's property from the pesticides, Larkin failed to make a showing sufficient to survive summary judgment on its trespass claim, and expressly declined to decide whether airborne particulate dispersion could ever constitute trespass

  • Rule: Trespass vs. Nuisance Distinction — trespass is an invasion of the plaintiff's interest in exclusive possession of land, generally requiring a tangible invasion, while nuisance is an interference with use and enjoyment, generally requiring proof of actual and substantial injury; some jurisdictions have adopted a "modern" trespass theory allowing intangible/airborne invasions to qualify only upon proof of substantial damage to the property

  • Reasoning: The Vermont Supreme Court reasoned as follows:

    • Traditionally, trespass requires a direct and tangible invasion of property, while nuisance addresses indirect/intangible interference (citing Maddy, Adams, Dobbs)

    • Some courts have adopted a "modern" trespass theory permitting airborne/intangible invasions to be actionable as trespass, but only upon a showing of substantial damage sufficient to constitute an infringement on exclusive possession (citing Maddy, Borland, Bradley), while at least one court (Michigan's Adams) rejected this modern view to preserve the trespass/nuisance distinction

    • Vermont case law was silent on the issue, and neither the Restatement provisions nor their commentary directly addressed whether particulate dispersion could constitute trespass, though the Restatement notes trespass and nuisance are not mutually exclusive and may overlap

    • A purely technical reading of trespass (any physical entry of any particle, regardless of impact) would subject virtually everyone to automatic trespass liability given the ubiquity of ambient particulate matter — "that cannot be the law"

    • Because Larkin failed to demonstrate any impact on its property whatsoever from the pesticides, the court found it unnecessary to resolve whether or under what circumstances airborne particulate invasions could constitute trespass, and affirmed summary judgment on that narrower ground

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Larkin argued that pesticide drift onto its land constituted a direct, tangible-enough invasion of its exclusive possessory interest to sustain a trespass claim, entitling it to injunctive relief and at least nominal damages without needing to prove substantial actual injury, and that the right-to-farm law's protections for nuisance claims should not extend to bar a properly pled trespass claim

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Marceau argued that Larkin's suit was in substance a nuisance claim dressed up as trespass to circumvent the right-to-farm law's rebuttable presumption against nuisance liability for agricultural activities, and that Larkin had produced no evidence of any impact on its property to sustain even a trespass theory

  • Disposition: The Supreme Court of Vermont affirmed the superior court's judgment in favor of Marceau

  • Importance: This case frames the trespass-to-land unit's central doctrinal tension — the traditional tangible-invasion requirement versus the "modern trend" allowing intangible/airborne invasions upon a showing of substantial damage — and illustrates how the classification (trespass vs. nuisance) can be outcome-determinative where a special statute (like a right-to-farm law) applies to one theory but not the other

 

4. DEFINITIONS

Battery

  • Definition: The intentional infliction of a harmful or offensive contact with the person of another (Restatement Second), or under the Restatement Third, an intentional contact caused by the actor's affirmative conduct that either causes bodily harm or is offensive

  • Significance: Central tort of the Leichtman case; illustrates that even trivial, non-injurious contacts (blown smoke) are actionable, and that damages of even one dollar support liability

  • Distinction: Distinguished from assault, which protects against apprehension of contact rather than the contact itself; many batteries also involve an accompanying assault, giving rise to the common (if technically imprecise) phrase "assault and battery"

 

Single Intent vs. Dual Intent

  • Definition: Single intent requires only that the defendant intend to cause the contact itself; dual intent requires that the defendant additionally intend that the contact be harmful or offensive

  • Significance: Raised in the Notes following Leichtman to question whether the Restatement Third § 1's formulation (intent to cause "a contact") differs meaningfully from the Restatement Second/Leichtman formulation ("intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact")

  • Distinction: For assault, courts have adopted a dual-intent approach even where they use single intent for battery — the actor must intend to cause apprehension specifically of a harmful or offensive contact, not merely apprehension of any contact

 

Assault

  • Definition: An offer to show violence to another without striking him; intentionally causing another to apprehend an imminent harmful or offensive contact with his person

  • Significance: Central tort of Dickens v. Puryear; the case turns on distinguishing assaults (imminent threats) from non-actionable-as-assault threats of future harm

  • Distinction: Distinguished from battery (actual contact) and from intentional infliction of emotional distress (which can capture threats of future, non-imminent harm that assault cannot reach)

 

Imminence

  • Definition: Under Restatement (Second) § 29(1), "imminent" does not mean instantaneous, but means there will be no significant delay between the threat and the potential contact

  • Significance: The dispositive factor in Dickens distinguishing the actionable assaults (pistol, knife, votes on killing) from the future threat (leave the state or be killed) that was not an assault

  • Distinction: Distinguished from mere temporal proximity — a threat can be very soon in absolute time and still not be "imminent" if conditioned on a delayed contingency (though conditional threats can still be imminent, per Note 3)

 

Transferred Intent

  • Definition: A doctrine under which a defendant's intent to commit a tort against one person is legally transferred to satisfy the intent element where the defendant instead, or additionally, harms a different, unintended person (transfer between persons), or commits a different tort than intended (transfer between torts, per Restatement Third § 11)

  • Significance: Central doctrine of Singer v. Marx (transfer between persons) and discussed further in the Notes (transfer between torts, e.g., A's shot frightening B (assault) while striking C (battery))

  • Distinction: Distinguished from ordinary intent, which requires the defendant to have intended harm to the actual plaintiff; transferred intent substitutes intent toward a different target/tort to satisfy the requirement

 

Civil Conspiracy

  • Definition: Liability theory requiring (1) an agreement (explicit or tacit) between two or more persons, (2) to participate in an unlawful act or lawful act unlawfully, (3) an injury caused by an unlawful overt act by a party to the agreement, and (4) that act done in furtherance of the common scheme

  • Significance: One of the two theories applied in Halberstam v. Welch to hold Hamilton liable for Welch's killing of Halberstam

  • Distinction: Distinguished from aiding-abetting by its central requirement of agreement — courts often blur the two by inferring agreement from evidence of substantial assistance, but Halberstam insists on keeping the theories analytically separate

 

Aiding-Abetting (Concerted Action by Substantial Assistance)

  • Definition: Liability theory requiring (1) that the party aided commit a wrongful act causing injury, (2) that the defendant be generally aware of her role in an overall illegal/tortious activity when providing assistance, and (3) that the defendant knowingly and substantially assist the primary violation

  • Significance: The second theory applied in Halberstam; also called "participation liability" or "concerted action" under Restatement Third § 10

  • Distinction: Distinguished from civil conspiracy by not requiring proof of agreement — it focuses instead on whether the defendant's assistance was knowing and substantial, evaluated through the multi-factor Restatement § 876(b) test

 

False Imprisonment

  • Definition: The intentional confinement of another within boundaries fixed by the actor, where the other is either conscious of the confinement or harmed by it

  • Significance: Central tort in both McCann (jury found confinement via implicit threats/false assertion of authority) and Fojtik (court found no confinement given plaintiff's characteristics and voluntary compliance)

  • Distinction: Often colloquially associated with wrongful arrest, but the casebook notes it is more accurately thought of as the tort of "intentional confinement," reaching far beyond arrest scenarios (e.g., store detentions, hospital admissions, locked buses)

 

Confinement by Assertion of Legal Authority

  • Definition: A form of confinement occurring when an actor asserts legal authority to take another into custody or otherwise confine him, and the other submits because he believes he has a duty to comply or fears adverse legal/physical consequences for noncompliance

  • Significance: The theoretical basis for liability in McCann, where Wal-Mart employees' false claim of authority (invoking the police, insisting the family "had to go with her") induced submission

  • Distinction: Distinguished from confinement by physical force/barriers — this category depends on the plaintiff's belief about the actor's authority rather than any physical impossibility of leaving; either the felt duty to comply or fear of physical consequences alone suffices

 

Trespass to Land

  • Definition: An intentional, unauthorized entry (or causing of an entry) onto the land in the possession of another, requiring only intent to perform the act of entry, not intent to trespass specifically on another's land

  • Significance: Addressed in Larkin v. Marceau in the specific context of airborne pesticide drift; also covered extensively in the surrounding textbook narrative on nominal damages, minimal intent, and exceeding a license

  • Distinction: Distinguished from nuisance (interference with use/enjoyment, requiring substantial injury) by protecting the interest in exclusive possession, and traditionally requiring a tangible/direct invasion, versus nuisance's indirect/intangible interference

 

Modern Trend of Trespass (Intangible Invasions)

  • Definition: A doctrinal approach, adopted by some courts, permitting trespass claims based on intangible/airborne invasions (smoke, particulates) where the invasion causes substantial damage sufficient to interfere with the plaintiff's exclusive possession

  • Significance: The central unresolved doctrinal question in Larkin v. Marceau, which the Vermont Supreme Court declined to resolve because Larkin showed no impact on its property regardless of which test applied

  • Distinction: Distinguished from the traditional view (Adams v. Cleveland-Cliffs), which requires a tangible, direct physical intrusion and treats all intangible invasions as sounding only in nuisance

 

Trespass to Chattels

  • Definition: The intentional physical interference with or destruction of another's personal property, requiring only intent to perform the act affecting the chattel (a reasonable, good-faith mistaken belief that the chattel is one's own is no defense)

  • Significance: Introduced in the textbook narrative closing the reading, including the Ranson v. Kitner "wolf/dog" example and internet-era applications (eBay v. Bidder's Edge, Intel v. Hamidi)

  • Distinction: Distinguished from conversion by the degree of interference — trespass to chattels covers lesser interference (actual damages generally required unless dispossession occurred), while conversion covers such serious interference with the right to control that the actor must pay the chattel's full value

 

Conversion

  • Definition: The intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel so seriously interfering with another's right to control it that the actor may justly be required to pay the full value of the chattel

  • Significance: Discussed at the close of the reading as either a standalone tort or, in some jurisdictions, merely a remedy (forced sale at market value) for trespass to chattels; illustrated by Russell-Vaughn Ford v. Rouse (mockingly withheld car keys) and modern applications to domain names (Kremen v. Cohen) and medical research (Moore v. Regents)

  • Distinction: Distinguished from trespass to chattels by the severity of interference and the resulting remedy — conversion forces a full-value "sale," while trespass to chattels yields only diminution-in-value or actual damages (except where dispossession, which supports nominal damages)
