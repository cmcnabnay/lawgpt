# UNIT I — Introduction & Intentional Torts (Weeks 1–2)

## Week 1 (CB 1–40) — Introduction; Intent

### The Aims of Tort Law; The Common Law
**Rule: Aims of tort law.** Tort law aims to (1) prevent self-help (give people a peaceful alternative to vengeance), (2) do corrective justice, (3) express retribution, (4) deter unreasonable conduct, and (5) compensate victims.
- **The common law** is law common to all England, developed **case by case by courts** rather than by statute, and carried into the English-speaking world.
- Tort law adapts to new situations through judicial decisions, but courts also hit limits on how far they will go without the legislature (Roberson).

### Limits of Common-Law Tort-Making
**Rule: Limits of common-law tort-making.** A court will not create a new cause of action (here, a right of privacy) when no precedent supports it; the remedy for a gap like this is with the legislature.

**Case: Roberson v. Rochester Folding Box Co.** (N.Y. 1902)
- **Facts:**
  - The defendants printed and circulated about 25,000 flour advertisements bearing Abigail Roberson's likeness without her consent. They hung in saloons, stores, and warehouses.
  - She alleged humiliation and emotional distress and sought an injunction plus $15,000 in damages.
  - The defendants **demurred**: even if every allegation is true, no recognized legal right supports the claim.
- **Issue:** Does the unauthorized commercial use of a person's likeness state a cause of action when no right of privacy has been recognized?
- **Holding:** No. The Court of Appeals reversed and sustained the demurrer.
  - Earlier English cases (Prince Albert v. Strange, Pollard, Duke of Queensberry) rested on **property or contract**, not a free-standing personal right to be left alone.
  - Recognizing the right would open a flood of litigation and was a job for the legislature. New York then passed a statute protecting against commercial use of a person's name or likeness.
- **Takeaway:** Courts won't create a new tort without precedent. Floodgates, line-drawing, and deference to the legislature are the policy reasons against recognizing a novel harm, and the gap here was later filled by statute.

### The Forms of Action
**Rule: Trespass vs. case.** Under the old writ system, trespass (vi et armis) lies for a direct and immediate forcible injury; an action on the case lies for an injury that is only consequential. Personal contact isn't required for trespass if the defendant set the injuring force in motion.

**Case: Scott v. Shepherd** (K.B. 1773)
- **Facts:**
  - On a fair day, Shepherd threw a lighted gunpowder squib into a crowded market-house.
  - It landed on Yates's stall. Willis instantly threw it away to protect himself and the wares; it landed on Ryal's stall, and Ryal instantly threw it on.
  - It exploded in Scott's face and put out his eye. Scott sued Shepherd in trespass.
- **Issue:** Does trespass (which requires a direct injury) lie against Shepherd when two other people handled the squib before it hurt Scott?
- **Holding:** Yes, 3–1. Trespass was "well maintainable."
  - The throws by Willis and Ryal were a **continuation of Shepherd's original force**. They acted out of **necessity** that Shepherd imposed on them, not as free agents.
  - Nares and Gould also relied on the **unlawful act rule**: whoever does an unlawful act answers for all its consequences, mediate or immediate.
  - **Blackstone (dissent):** Willis and Ryal were **free agents** who gave the squib a new impetus, so Scott's injury was only consequential and he had to sue in case. "We must keep up the boundaries of actions."
- **Takeaway:** An original actor stays liable when intermediaries react instinctively to protect themselves; their forced reactions don't break the causal chain. The case is an early version of later intervening-cause, necessity, and proximate-cause doctrine.

### Intent (Substantial Certainty)
**Rule: Intent (substantial certainty).** An actor intends a consequence if they act with the purpose of producing it or knowing that it is substantially certain to result.

**Case: Beauchamp v. Dow Chemical Co.** (Mich. 1986)
- **Facts:** Beauchamp, a Dow research chemist, was exposed to Agent Orange on the job. He sued Dow in tort for intentionally misrepresenting and hiding the chemical's dangers.
- **Issue:** Can an employee sue the employer for an intentional tort outside the **workers' compensation exclusivity** bar, and what counts as "intent"?
- **Holding:** Yes. The intentional tort exception exists, and the employee can meet it under the **substantial certainty** test. The court remanded.
  - The court rejected the narrower "true intent" (purpose only) test.
  - Michigan's legislature later narrowed the exception by statute (actual knowledge that injury was certain plus willful disregard).
- **Takeaway:** Intent means purpose or knowledge that the result is substantially certain. Knowing of a risk, even a high one, is only negligence or recklessness, which matters when intent unlocks a remedy (here, getting around workers' comp exclusivity).

### Single Intent for Battery
**Rule: Single intent for battery.** Battery requires only the intent to make contact; the actor need not intend the contact to be harmful or offensive. Mental illness doesn't negate this intent if the actor meant to make the contact.

**Case: Wagner v. State** (Utah 2005)
- **Facts:** A mentally disabled man in a state-supervised program, while shopping at a Kmart with a state employee, attacked Wagner from behind for no apparent reason. Wagner sued the state for negligent supervision.
- **Issue:** Was the patient's act a **battery** (which would trigger the state's immunity for claims "arising out of" battery), even though he may have lacked the capacity to appreciate that it was harmful or offensive?
- **Holding:** Yes. It was a battery, so the state was **immune** and dismissal was affirmed.
  - The actor need only intend the contact. Requiring an intent to harm or offend (dual intent) would let mentally ill actors escape liability for intentional torts.
- **Takeaway:** Under single intent, D only needs to intend the contact, so a mentally ill or confused defendant can still commit battery. Jurisdictions using dual intent (intent to harm or offend) would come out differently. Insanity is not a defense.

### Notes on Sudden Incapacity (CB 40–41)
**Rule: Sudden incapacity.** An actor who suddenly and unexpectedly loses the capacity for voluntary action (heart attack, fainting, a sudden delusion) cannot form intent. But the actor may still be negligent if they knew or had reason to know they were subject to such episodes and engaged in risky conduct anyway.

## Week 2 (CB 40–88, 114–142) — Battery, Assault, Participation, False Imprisonment, Trespass; Consent & Self-Defense

### Battery
**Rule: Battery.** An actor is liable for battery if they intend to cause a harmful or offensive contact with another, and a harmful or offensive contact directly or indirectly results (Restatement (Second)). The Restatement (Third) § 1 requires only intent to cause a contact that turns out harmful or offensive.
- **Offensive contact:** offends a **reasonable sense of personal dignity** (objective standard, not the plaintiff's own sensitivity).
- **Contact with things closely attached** to the person counts (a hat, a cane, a plate in hand, an occupied car).
- **Incitement:** someone present who **encourages or incites** a battery with words is liable as a principal.

**Case: Leichtman v. WLW Jacor Communications, Inc.** (Ohio Ct. App. 1994)
- **Facts:**
  - Leichtman, an anti-smoking advocate, appeared on a WLW radio talk show on the Great American Smokeout.
  - Another host, Furman, lit a cigar and repeatedly blew smoke in his face, allegedly at host Cunningham's urging, to cause discomfort and humiliation.
  - Leichtman sued WLW, Cunningham, and Furman for battery, invasion of privacy, and violation of a smoking regulation. The trial court dismissed under Rule 12(B)(6).
- **Issue:** Does deliberately blowing smoke into someone's face state a claim for battery?
- **Holding:** Yes, as to all three defendants. Dismissal of the privacy and regulation counts was affirmed.
  - Tobacco smoke has **physical properties**, so intentionally directing it at someone is a contact. It is offensive to a reasonable sense of dignity.
  - Cunningham can be liable for **inciting** the battery. WLW's liability under **respondeat superior** depends on whether the battery was within the scope of employment (a fact question).
- **Takeaway:** Any intentional contact that offends a reasonable sense of dignity is battery, even trivial contact with no physical harm, and contact can be indirect (smoke, thrown objects). Anyone who incites the battery is also liable, and employer liability turns on scope of employment.

### Assault
**Rule: Assault.** An actor is liable for assault if they intend to cause another to anticipate an imminent harmful or offensive contact, and their conduct causes that apprehension.
- **Imminent** means no significant delay, not instantaneous. A threat of **future** harm is not assault.
- **Words alone** aren't enough unless, combined with acts or circumstances, they create reasonable apprehension of imminent contact (Restatement (Second) § 31).
- **Conditional threats** ("leave or I'll hit you") can still be assaults.
- Courts look past the plaintiff's **label** to the facts and relief sought.

**Case: Dickens v. Puryear** (N.C. 1981)
- **Facts:**
  - Dickens, 31, had a relationship involving sex and drugs with the defendants' 17-year-old daughter.
  - The defendants lured him to a rural area, held a pistol between his eyes, and had masked men beat him and handcuff him to farm equipment. They cut his hair, threatened castration, and voted within his hearing on whether to kill him.
  - At the end, Earl Puryear told him to leave the state or be killed.
  - Dickens sued almost three years later for **intentional infliction of emotional distress** (3-year statute) because assault and battery had a **1-year** statute.
- **Issue:** Was the final threat of death if he didn't leave the state an assault (time-barred) or IIED (timely)?
- **Holding:** The beatings, haircut, and immediate threats were batteries and assaults, so they were time-barred. But the final threat was of **future** harm, not imminent contact, so it was **not an assault**. That part could go forward as IIED under the 3-year statute.
- **Takeaway:** Assault requires a threat of imminent contact; a threat of future harm is not assault, though it may support IIED. Because each act is classified separately, the label can decide the statute of limitations and the remedy.

### Transferred Intent; Child Liability
**Rule: Transferred intent.** If the defendant intends a tort against one person but instead harms another, the intent transfers to the actual victim. Under the Restatement (Third) § 11, intent transfers between persons and between torts (an intended assault can support a battery claim).

**Rule: Children.** A child is liable for an intentional tort if they had the capacity to form the intent required for that tort, even if too young to understand the act was wrong.

**Case: Singer v. Marx** (Cal. Ct. App. 1956)
- **Facts:**
  - Nine-year-old Tim Marx said "watch Barbie" and threw a rock at Barbara, a girl on a bicycle. The rock hit 8-year-old Denise Singer in the eye instead.
  - Tim had a known habit of throwing rocks and clods at people and cars. Denise sued Tim for battery and his parents (Zeppo and Marion Marx) for negligent supervision. The trial court granted nonsuits.
- **Issue:** Was there enough evidence to send the battery claim against Tim and the supervision claims against his parents to the jury?
- **Holding:**
  - **Tim:** nonsuit reversed. A 9-year-old can intend a battery, and if he aimed at Barbara and hit Denise, his intent transfers.
  - **Marion Marx:** nonsuit reversed. There was evidence she knew of Tim's dangerous habit and failed to control it.
  - **Zeppo Marx:** nonsuit affirmed. There was no evidence of his personal knowledge.
- **Takeaway:** If D aims at one person and hits another, intent transfers (between persons and, under the Restatement (Third), between torts). A child is liable if they could form the required intent. Parents aren't vicariously liable but can be negligent for failing to supervise a child with a known dangerous tendency.

### Participation Liability
**Rule: Civil conspiracy.** (1) An agreement between two or more persons (it can be tacit), (2) to participate in an unlawful act or a lawful act in an unlawful manner, (3) an injury caused by an unlawful overt act by one party, (4) done in furtherance of the common scheme.

**Rule: Aiding and abetting.** (1) The person aided commits a wrongful act causing injury, (2) the defendant is generally aware of their role in the overall illegal activity when they help, and (3) the defendant knowingly and substantially assists the principal.
- **Substantial assistance factors:** nature of the act encouraged, amount and kind of assistance, presence at the time of the tort, relation to the tortfeasor, state of mind, and (added by Halberstam) **duration** of the assistance.
- A participant is liable for **reasonably foreseeable** acts in furtherance of the activity, even ones they didn't know about or plan (Restatement (Second) § 876).

**Case: Halberstam v. Welch** (D.C. Cir. 1983)
- **Facts:**
  - For five years, Welch committed nighttime burglaries, leaving home most evenings while his live-in partner Hamilton stayed home.
  - Their wealth exploded (a $1 million house, Mercedes cars). Hamilton ran the money side: proceeds went through her accounts, she kept records, did the taxes, and typed transmittal letters for the sale of melted-down gold and silver.
  - During a burglary, Welch shot and killed Dr. Halberstam. His widow sued Hamilton.
- **Issue:** Is Hamilton liable for a killing she didn't commit, know about, or plan?
- **Holding:** Yes, under both **conspiracy** (a tacit agreement to run a stolen-goods enterprise) and **aiding and abetting** (knowing, substantial assistance as Welch's banker, bookkeeper, and secretary). The killing was a **foreseeable** risk of violent burglary.
- **Takeaway:** Two theories reach someone who didn't act: conspiracy (agreement plus an overt act in furtherance) and aiding and abetting (general awareness plus knowing, substantial assistance). Either one makes D liable for foreseeable acts within the scope of the joint enterprise.

### False Imprisonment
**Rule: False imprisonment.** The actor (1) intends to confine another within a limited area, (2) their conduct causes a confinement, and (3) the plaintiff is aware of the confinement or is harmed by it (Restatement (Third) § 7).
- **Means of confinement:** physical barriers, force, express or **implicit threats** of force, duress, or a (false) **assertion of legal authority** that the plaintiff submits to.
- **Threats to call police** aren't usually enough on their own.
- Barriers must leave no **reasonable means of escape**.

**Case: McCann v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.** (1st Cir. 2000)
- **Facts:**
  - Wal-Mart employees stopped Debra McCann and her two children at the exit, mistakenly believing the children had shoplifted before.
  - They said the family "had to go with" them, that police were being called, and stood over them near the exit for about an hour.
  - They refused to look at her ID and refused to let 12-year-old Jonathan use the bathroom. In fact they only called store security. The McCanns were never told they could leave.
- **Issue:** Could a jury find confinement without any physical restraint?
- **Holding:** Yes. The jury verdict for the McCanns was affirmed. The employees' conduct implied a threat of physical restraint and asserted (falsely) legal authority to hold the family until police arrived.
- **Takeaway:** Confinement doesn't require physical force. Implicit threats and a false assertion of authority can be enough, such as refusing a bathroom request and saying "you have to come with me," when P reasonably believes they can't leave.

### False Imprisonment (contrast)
**Rule: False imprisonment (Texas).** False imprisonment is (1) a willful detention, (2) without consent, (3) without authority of law. A threat-based detention requires a threat that would inspire a just fear of injury to person, reputation, or property, judged by the parties' relative size, age, experience, sex, and demeanor.

**Case: Fojtik v. Charter Medical Corp.** (Tex. App. 1999)
- **Facts:**
  - After a family "intervention," Fojtik, a 45-year-old businessman, entered Charter's alcohol treatment program. He said he agreed only because he was told he would otherwise be involuntarily committed and brought in handcuffs.
  - He was denied passes at first and felt "locked up," but later received passes and always came back on his own. Charter's evidence was that he could have left at any time.
- **Issue:** Did the evidence raise a fact issue that he was detained against his will?
- **Holding:** No. Summary judgment for Charter was affirmed. He consented, came back voluntarily, and an experienced adult businessman would not have a just fear from these circumstances.
- **Takeaway:** P's subjective feeling of being confined isn't enough. The threat or restraint must be objectively coercive for a person with P's characteristics (Texas: a just fear of injury), which is the contrast with McCann.

### Trespass to Land
**Rule: Trespass to land.** An intentional, unauthorized entry onto land in another's possession. Only the act of entry must be intended; a reasonable mistake about ownership is no defense. Nominal damages are available without actual harm. Staying after permission is revoked, or going beyond the scope of a license, is also trespass.

**Rule: Trespass vs. nuisance.** Trespass protects exclusive possession and traditionally requires a direct, tangible invasion. Nuisance protects use and enjoyment and requires substantial harm.
- **Modern trend:** intangible invasions (smoke, gas, particulates) are trespass **only if they cause substantial physical damage** to the property.

**Case: John Larkin, Inc. v. Marceau** (Vt. 2008)
- **Facts:** A developer bought land next to Marceau's apple orchard. It alleged that pesticide spray drifted onto its land and sued in **trespass** (not nuisance), to avoid Vermont's **right-to-farm** presumption that farming isn't a nuisance.
- **Issue:** Can airborne pesticide drift with no shown physical impact support a trespass claim?
- **Holding:** No. Summary judgment for Marceau was affirmed. Without evidence of **physical impact** on the land, there was no trespass. The court didn't decide whether particulate drift could ever be trespass.
- **Takeaway:** Trespass needs a direct, tangible entry; intangible invasions (drift, noise, light, odors) usually belong in nuisance unless they cause physical damage. The label matters: trespass gives nominal damages without harm, while nuisance requires substantial harm.

### Trespass to Chattels and Conversion (CB 80–88)
**Rule: Trespass to chattels (Restatement (Second) § 218).** Intentional physical interference with or dispossession of another's personal property. Only intent to do the act is required; a good-faith belief that the chattel is one's own is no defense. Damages = harm to the chattel or loss of use.

**Rule: Conversion (§ 222A).** An intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with the owner's right to control it that the actor may justly be required to pay its full value (a forced sale).
- **Seriousness factors:** extent and duration of control, intent to assert a right, good faith, harm done, inconvenience and expense.
- Example: *Russell-Vaughn Ford v. Rouse* (car salesman holding a customer's keys as a joke for an extended time was conversion).

### Consent
**Rule: Actual consent.** The plaintiff (1) had capacity, (2) was free of duress or substantial mistake, and (3) was subjectively willing for the conduct to occur. It may be express or inferred from conduct. Consent covers conduct that isn't substantially different from what the plaintiff agreed to, including the risk of accidental injury inherent in the activity.

**Case: Hellriegel v. Tholl** (Wash. 1966)
- **Facts:** Teenage friends engaged in horseplay. Dicka told the others, "You couldn't throw me in [the lake] even if you tried." They tried; in the struggle one boy lost his balance and fell on Dicka's head, breaking his neck.
- **Issue:** Did the plaintiff consent to the contact that injured him?
- **Holding:** Yes. Dicka invited the horseplay, and his consent covered accidental injury arising from it. Dismissal was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Participants consent to the ordinary, accidental risks of an activity, but not to intentional conduct beyond the scope of the game. The question is whether D's conduct fell within what P agreed to.

### Apparent Consent; Effectiveness
**Rule: Apparent consent.** If the plaintiff's words or conduct are reasonably understood as consent, that is as effective as actual consent.

**Rule: Effective consent.** An adult can give effective consent unless they have an abnormality that substantially impairs their capacity to weigh the conduct and the defendant knows or has reason to know of it.
- **Duress** voids consent only if drastic: threats of force against the person or family, or of arrest. Economic pressure usually doesn't count.
- **Substantial mistake** about the nature of the invasion, extent of harm, or the actor's purpose voids consent if induced by fraud or known to the actor.
- **Consent to a crime** is effective unless the statute protects a class regardless of consent (statutory rape).

**Case: Reavis v. Slominski** (Neb. 1996)
- **Facts:** Reavis, a dental assistant, testified that her employer Slominski repeatedly pressured her into sexual contact over years and that she said no but felt she had no real choice. No physical force was used, and she sometimes successfully refused. She described a psychological inability to refuse him.
- **Issue:** Should the court have directed a verdict for Slominski on the ground that Reavis consented as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** No (plurality). Conflicting evidence created jury questions on actual and apparent consent and on whether her consent was effective given her alleged incapacity and his knowledge of it. The jury verdict for Reavis stood.
- **Takeaway:** Consent has two parts: whether there was consent (actual, or apparent from words or conduct), and whether it was effective (capacity, duress, mistake, and whether D knew of P's impairment).

### Other Consent Doctrines (CB 125–30)
- **Presumed consent:** the conduct is commonly accepted without asking, and the actor has no reason to think the plaintiff would refuse.
- **Emergency doctrine** (Restatement (Third) § 17): no reason to think the plaintiff would refuse, and the risk to life or health makes waiting for consent unreasonable.
- **Ordinary contacts of life** are consented to; no one can "erect a glass cage" around themselves.
- **Proposed "no means no" rule:** if the plaintiff expresses unwillingness to sexual contact and the actor immediately proceeds, there is no consent as a matter of law.

### Self-Defense
**Rule: Self-defense.** A person may use reasonable force to defend against a threatened harmful or offensive contact.
- **Non-deadly force:** no duty to retreat.
- **Deadly force:** only against a reasonable apprehension of **death or serious bodily harm**, and only if **retreat** isn't safely possible, **except** in one's own dwelling (and, for some courts, place of business).
- **Mere words** never justify force.
- An owner may use reasonable non-deadly force to **eject a trespasser** who refuses to leave after a request.

**Rule: Defense of others.** Same privilege as self-defense if the actor reasonably believes the third person has a self-defense privilege and that intervention is necessary.

**Case: Silas v. Bowen** (D.S.C. 1967)
- **Facts:** Silas, a professional basketball player who had been drinking, came to Bowen's lot angry about a car repair. He refused to leave, grabbed Bowen, and threatened him. Bowen got a shotgun from his office and fired it toward the ground to scare him off, and Silas was hit.
- **Issue:** Was Bowen privileged in self-defense?
- **Holding:** Yes. Judgment for the defendant. Silas was a much larger, aggressive trespasser who attacked Bowen on his own business premises, which created a reasonable fear of serious bodily harm. Bowen had no duty to retreat at his place of business.
- **Takeaway:** Self-defense checklist: a reasonable belief in a threat, force proportional to it, deadly force only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm, and the jurisdiction's retreat rule and its exceptions (no duty to retreat at home).

# UNIT II — Defenses (cont.) & Negligence: Breach (Weeks 3–5)

## Week 3 (CB 142–206) — Defense of Property, Discipline, Necessity; the Standard of Care

### Defense of Property
**Rule: Defense of property.** An owner may use reasonable, nondeadly force to prevent or end an intrusion on land or chattels if (1) the intrusion isn't privileged, (2) the owner first asks the intruder to stop (unless that would be useless or dangerous), and (3) the owner reasonably believes force is needed. Deadly force may never be used to protect property alone. It is allowed only if the intruder also threatens the owner's personal safety (then it's self-defense).
- **Deadly force to prevent a felony:** only for felonies threatening death or serious bodily harm, or breaking into a **dwelling** (Restatement (Second) § 143).
- **Mechanical devices** (spring guns, traps): an owner can't do with a device what they couldn't do in person. Deadly devices are never privileged to protect property.
- **Recapture of chattels:** reasonable nondeadly force is allowed to recapture property **tortiously taken**, if done **promptly** (fresh pursuit) and after a demand when practical. There is no privilege to use force to retake **land**.
- **Repossession (UCC § 9-503):** a secured party may repossess collateral on default without court process if it can be done **without a breach of the peace**.

**Case: Brown v. Martinez** (N.M. 1961)
- **Facts:** Teenage boys came onto Martinez's land at night to steal watermelons. Martinez fired a rifle in a different direction from where he saw two boys running, meaning only to scare them. The bullet hit Brown, a 15-year-old standing outside the fence, in the leg.
- **Issue:** May a landowner use a gun to stop a misdemeanor trespass and theft of watermelons?
- **Holding:** No, as a matter of law. The defendant was liable even though he intended only to frighten. Because he intended to cause apprehension, his intent **transferred** to Brown.
- **Takeaway:** Deadly force to protect property alone is never privileged, including spring guns and shooting at fleeing thieves. It's justified only if the intruder also threatens someone's safety, which makes it self-defense.

### Discipline (CB 160–62)
**Rule: Parental discipline.** Parents (and to a lesser extent teachers) have a privilege to use reasonable physical force to discipline a child. Courts weigh the child's age and condition, the misconduct, the force used, and whether it was likely to cause serious harm.

### Public Necessity
**Rule: Public necessity.** A complete defense when the defendant's intrusion was reasonably necessary to avert a public disaster (e.g., destroying houses to stop a fire from spreading). Traditionally, the actor owes no compensation (though some takings law may require the government to pay).

### Private Necessity
**Rule: Private necessity.** A person may enter or use another's property when reasonably necessary to avoid serious harm to people or property, especially to save human life. The owner may not resist or expel the entrant during the emergency.

**Case: Ploof v. Putnam** (Vt. 1908)
- **Facts:** During a sudden violent storm on Lake Champlain, Ploof moored his sloop, with his wife and children aboard, to Putnam's dock. Putnam's servant untied it. The sloop was wrecked and the family was thrown into the lake and injured.
- **Issue:** Was the mooring privileged, such that the owner's agent could not cut the boat loose?
- **Holding:** Yes. Necessity justified the entry, so unmooring the sloop was actionable. The complaint stated a claim (the demurrer was overruled).
- **Takeaway:** In an emergency, a person may enter or use another's property to protect life or property. An owner who resists or expels them is liable for the resulting harm.

### Incomplete Privilege
**Rule: Incomplete privilege.** A person who acts under private necessity is privileged to stay (the owner can't eject them), but must pay for actual damage caused when they deliberately protect their own property at someone else's expense.

**Case: Vincent v. Lake Erie Transportation Co.** (Minn. 1910)
- **Facts:** A steamship was unloading at Vincent's dock when a severe storm hit. No tug was available, so the crew kept the ship tied up all night, replacing lines as they broke. Waves slammed the ship against the dock and damaged it by about $500.
- **Issue:** Is a shipowner who reasonably keeps its vessel tied to a dock during a storm liable for the damage to the dock?
- **Holding:** Yes. The shipowner **deliberately** chose to save its more valuable ship at the expense of the dock, so it must compensate the dock owner. Had the storm simply thrown the ship against the dock, there would be no liability.
- **Dissent:** The ship lawfully moored, acted with due care, and the damage was an act of God; the dock owner should bear the loss.
- **Takeaway:** Private necessity is an incomplete privilege: the actor can't be expelled (Ploof) but must pay for any actual damage. Public necessity, by contrast, is a complete defense.

### Rise of Negligence
**Rule: Fault requirement for unintentional injury.** For a direct but unintentional injury, the plaintiff must prove the defendant acted without ordinary care (fault), or that the act was unlawful. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving the defendant's lack of care. Pure accident with no fault means no liability.

**Case: Brown v. Kendall** (Mass. 1850)
- **Facts:** Kendall was beating two fighting dogs (his and Brown's) with a stick to separate them. Backing up, he raised the stick over his shoulder and accidentally hit Brown in the eye.
- **Issue:** Is a defendant liable in trespass for a direct injury caused unintentionally while doing a lawful act, and who has the burden on care?
- **Holding:** Not without fault. The defendant is liable only if he failed to use **ordinary care** under the circumstances, and the plaintiff bears that burden. The trial court erred by requiring extraordinary care and placing the burden on the defendant. New trial ordered.
- **Takeaway:** For unintentional injury, P must prove D failed to use ordinary care, and P bears that burden. A pure accident without fault means no liability.

### The Prima Facie Case of Negligence
**Rule: Negligence elements.** The plaintiff must prove (1) duty, (2) breach, (3) cause in fact, (4) scope of liability (proximate cause), and (5) actual damages (physical injury or property damage in most cases).
- **Baseline duty:** an actor ordinarily owes **reasonable care** when their conduct creates a risk of physical harm (Restatement (Third) § 7(a)).

### The Objective Standard
**Rule: Reasonable person.** Negligence is measured by what a person of ordinary prudence would do under the same circumstances, not by the defendant's own best judgment.

**Case: Vaughan v. Menlove** (C.P. 1837)
- **Facts:** Menlove stacked hay near his property line, next to the plaintiff's cottages. He was warned for five weeks that it could spontaneously ignite. He said he "would chance it" (the hay was insured). It caught fire and burned the cottages.
- **Issue:** Is negligence measured by whether the defendant acted honestly to the best of his own judgment, or by an objective standard?
- **Holding:** Objective standard. A test based on each person's own judgment would be "as variable as the length of the foot of each individual." The verdict for the plaintiff stood.
- **Takeaway:** The standard is objective: what would a reasonable person of ordinary prudence have done? D's good faith or "best judgment" is no defense.

### Common Knowledge
**Rule: Common knowledge.** The reasonable person is charged with knowledge of matters of common experience. A driver must know the condition of parts (like tires) that a reasonable inspection would reveal as dangerous. No expert testimony is needed for risks within common knowledge.

**Case: Delair v. McAdoo** (Pa. 1936)
- **Facts:** McAdoo's tire blew out while he was passing Delair, and his car swerved into Delair's. Witnesses testified the tire was worn through to the fabric.
- **Issue:** Can a jury find negligence from a visibly worn tire without expert testimony?
- **Holding:** Yes. Every driver is charged with knowing that badly worn tires are dangerous. Judgment for the plaintiff was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** D is charged with knowing what ordinary experience teaches (e.g., worn tires are dangerous), so ignorance of a common risk is no excuse, and P needs no expert to prove it.

### Children
**Rule: Child standard.** A minor is held to the standard of a reasonable person of like age, intelligence, and experience under like circumstances.
- **Adult-activity exception:** a minor engaged in an activity **normally undertaken only by adults** (Restatement (Second)), or "characteristically undertaken by adults" (Restatement (Third)), is held to the **adult** standard. Driving cars, boats, and planes are the classic examples (dangerous to third parties who can't tell the operator is a child). Licensing supports the adult standard.
- **Young children:** many states hold children under **7** (Restatement (Third): under **5**) incapable of negligence. Some use rebuttable presumptions for ages 7–14.

**Case: Goss v. Allen** (N.J. 1976)
- **Facts:** Allen, 17, a beginning skier on his first downhill run, walked a quarter of the way up the beginners' slope and skied down. He lost control at a sharp turn at the bottom and hit Goss, a ski patrol member standing about 60 feet past the end of the slope.
- **Issue:** Should a 17-year-old skier be held to an adult standard of care?
- **Holding:** No. Recreational skiing isn't an activity limited to adults, so the jury was correctly told to apply the child standard. The verdict for Allen stood.
- **Dissent:** A 17-year-old should be held to the adult standard; the plaintiff shouldn't bear the loss of a minor's inexperience.
- **Takeaway:** Children are held to the standard of a reasonable child of like age, intelligence, and experience, except in adult activities (driving, motorized vehicles), where the adult standard applies. Whether an activity is adult-only, inherently dangerous, or licensed decides which standard governs.

### Mental Disability
**Rule: Mental disability.** Adults with mental disabilities are held to the same objective standard as everyone else (Restatement (Second) § 283B; (Third) § 11(c)). No allowance is made for lack of intelligence or mental illness.
- **Exception (Creasy):** a patient with no capacity to control their conduct owes **no duty** to a **professional caregiver** who is employed to deal with that very risk.

**Case: Creasy v. Rusk** (Ind. 2000)
- **Facts:** Rusk, an Alzheimer's patient in a nursing home, was known to be combative. While Creasy, a nursing assistant who knew his history, was putting him to bed, he kicked her and injured her back.
- **Issue:** (1) Are mentally disabled adults held to the ordinary objective standard? (2) Did Rusk owe Creasy a duty?
- **Holding:** (1) Yes, the general rule applies. (2) But on these facts Rusk owed **no duty** to Creasy, given the caregiver relationship and public policy. Summary judgment for Rusk.
- **Policy for the objective rule:** allocating loss to the one who caused it, encouraging caretakers to supervise, avoiding false claims of disability, and promoting integration of disabled people.
- **Takeaway:** Mental disability doesn't lower the standard; D is held to the reasonable-person standard. A caregiver injured by an institutionalized patient may still be owed no duty, given their relationship and public policy. Physical disability is treated differently (Haley).

### Physical Disability
**Rule: Physical disability.** A physically disabled person must act as a reasonable person with that disability. Defendants must take reasonable precautions for foreseeable disabled members of the public.

**Case: Haley v. London Electricity Board** (H.L. 1964)
- **Facts:** Haley, a blind man, walked his usual route to work. The Board's workers had dug a trench in the sidewalk and guarded it with only a long-handled hammer laid across the path. A sighted person would have seen it; Haley's cane missed it, and he tripped and fell, becoming deaf.
- **Issue:** Must a party excavating a sidewalk take precautions adequate for blind pedestrians?
- **Holding:** Yes. Blind pedestrians walking alone are foreseeable, and the precautions were inadequate for them. The appeal was allowed.
- **Takeaway:** A physically disabled person is held to the standard of a reasonable person with that disability. D must take precautions for foreseeable disabled people, such as blind pedestrians on a public sidewalk.

### Superior Skill; Emergency; Sudden Incapacity
- **Superior skill (Restatement (Third) § 12):** an actor's above-average skills or knowledge are **circumstances** considered in judging reasonable care. It's not a separate higher standard.
- **Emergency doctrine (§ 9):** a sudden emergency, **not of the actor's own making**, is taken into account in judging what was reasonable. The actor must still respond as a reasonable person would in that emergency.
- **Sudden incapacitation (§ 11(b)):** conduct during a sudden loss of consciousness from physical illness is negligent only if the incapacity was **reasonably foreseeable**.

### Foreseeability
**Rule: Foreseeability of injury.** A defendant is negligent only if a reasonable person would have foreseen some injury from the conduct. The precise manner or extent of harm need not be foreseeable.

**Case: Barker v. City of Philadelphia** (E.D. Pa. 1955)
- **Facts:** A city trash-truck driver, familiar with a neighborhood full of children, swerved to pass a double-parked truck. He saw a large pile of wrapping paper by the curb and tried to avoid it because it might contain glass. He misjudged and ran over it, killing a child hiding underneath.
- **Issue:** Could the jury find that a reasonable driver should have foreseen injury from driving over the paper?
- **Holding:** Yes. The driver knew children played there and suspected something might be under the paper. The verdict for the plaintiff was upheld.
- **Takeaway:** Breach requires foreseeability of the general kind of harm, not the precise manner. D's knowledge of the setting (e.g., children nearby) raises what a reasonable person would anticipate.

## Week 4 (CB 206–277) — The Calculus of Risk; Judge and Jury; Negligence Per Se; Custom; Malpractice

### The Hand Formula
**Rule: Hand formula.** The defendant is negligent if B < P × L: the burden of adequate precautions is less than the probability of harm times the gravity of the resulting injury.

**Case: United States v. Carroll Towing Co.** (2d Cir. 1947)
- **Facts:** The barge *Anna C* broke loose from a pier in New York Harbor while a tug was shifting other barges. It hit a tanker's propeller and sank. The barge owner's bargee had been ashore for about 21 hours, so no one was aboard to notice the leak and call for help.
- **Issue:** Was the barge owner negligent (contributorily) for having no bargee aboard?
- **Holding:** Yes. In wartime, in a crowded harbor during working daylight hours, the risk of breakaway was high and the burden of keeping a bargee aboard was low, so the owner's absence was negligent. The **sinking** damages were reduced by the barge's share of fault.
- **Takeaway:** Breach exists if the burden of the untaken precaution (B) is less than the probability (P) times the gravity (L) of the harm. The Hand formula is the course's main cost-benefit test for breach.

### Unreasonable Risk
**Rule: Unreasonable risk.** Negligence requires a risk that is both foreseeable and unreasonable. A defendant need not guard against every conceivable risk.
- **Subsequent remedial measures** are generally **inadmissible** to prove negligence (they would discourage safety improvements).

**Case: Pitre v. Employers Liability Assurance Corp.** (La. Ct. App. 1970)
- **Facts:** At a fire department fair, a 17-year-old winding up to throw a baseball at a game concession hit a 9-year-old boy behind him in the temple, killing him. There were no barriers separating throwers from people standing around.
- **Issue:** Were the fair operators negligent for failing to warn or to separate throwers from spectators?
- **Holding:** No. The risk of someone being hit by a pitcher's **backswing** was too remote to be an unreasonable risk; the operators guarded against the foreseeable risk (balls flying toward the target area). The trial court also erred by admitting post-accident changes. The judgment for the plaintiffs was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Foreseeability alone doesn't establish breach. A low-probability risk combined with a heavy burden of prevention isn't unreasonable, so D need not guard against every conceivable risk.

### Judge-Made Standards
**Rule: Judge-made standards of conduct.** When the standard of conduct is clear, the court may lay it down as a matter of law rather than leave it to the jury.

**Case: Baltimore & Ohio R.R. v. Goodman** (U.S. 1927)
- **Facts:** Goodman drove his truck slowly toward a railroad crossing where his view was blocked by a section house. He was killed by a train going at least 60 mph.
- **Issue:** Was Goodman contributorily negligent as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** Yes. A driver at a crossing must stop and look, and if he can't be sure otherwise, he must **get out of the vehicle and look**. Judgment for the widow reversed.
- **Takeaway:** A court can sometimes set the standard of care as a matter of law ("stop, look, and get out"), but Pokora later rejected this approach in favor of leaving the question to the jury.

### Back to the Jury
**Rule: Negligence as a jury question.** Fixed rules of conduct are poorly suited to the variety of real situations. Whether reasonable care required more is usually a question for the jury.

**Case: Pokora v. Wabash Railway Co.** (U.S. 1934)
- **Facts:** Pokora stopped his truck at a crossing where boxcars blocked his view, listened, heard nothing, and drove forward. A train hit him.
- **Issue:** Was Pokora negligent as a matter of law for not getting out to look, under Goodman?
- **Holding:** No. Getting out to look could be useless or even more dangerous. Whether he acted reasonably was a jury question. Goodman's "get out and look" rule was **limited**.
- **Takeaway:** Breach is usually a jury question. The court decides it only when reasonable minds could not differ.

### Negligence Per Se
**Rule: Negligence per se.** The unexcused violation of a statute designed to protect against the harm that occurred is negligence in itself. The jury has no power to treat it as merely evidence. The plaintiff must still prove the violation caused the harm.
- **Elements:** (1) a statute (or regulation) sets a standard of conduct; (2) the plaintiff is in the **class of persons** it protects; (3) the harm is the **type of harm** it aims to prevent; (4) the violation was **unexcused**; (5) the violation **caused** the injury.

**Case: Martin v. Herzog** (N.Y. 1920)
- **Facts:** At night, Herzog's car rounded a curve and hit the plaintiff's husband's buggy, killing him. The buggy was driving without lights, in violation of a statute.
- **Issue:** Is the unexcused failure to use statutorily required lights negligence as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** Yes. It's negligence per se, and the jury erred in being allowed to treat it as mere evidence. The plaintiff's negligence bars recovery only if it **contributed** to the accident (causation is a separate question). The judgment for the defendant was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** An unexcused violation of a safety statute is negligence per se if P is in the protected class and the harm is the type the statute aims to prevent. P must still prove causation.

### Excused Violations
**Rule: Excused statutory violations.** A statutory violation is excused when obeying the statute would create greater danger than violating it. Statutes that codify general rules of the road (rather than specific safety precautions) aren't applied inflexibly.
- Restatement (Third) § 15 excuses include: incapacity, reasonable lack of knowledge, reasonable diligence trying to comply, emergency, and compliance would be more dangerous.

**Case: Tedla v. Ellman** (N.Y. 1939)
- **Facts:** Two pedestrians pushing baby carriages of junk walked on the right side of a highway, facing away from traffic, at night. The statute required pedestrians to walk on the left, facing traffic. But traffic on the left that evening was much heavier. A car hit them from behind.
- **Issue:** Was walking on the wrong side contributory negligence as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** No. The statute was meant to promote safety; the legislature couldn't have intended it to be obeyed when obeying it would increase danger. Judgment for the plaintiffs was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** A statutory violation is excused when complying would create greater danger than violating. Other recognized excuses include incapacity, emergency, and lack of knowledge of the occasion.

### Rebutting Negligence Per Se
**Rule: Rebutting negligence per se (Oregon).** A party who violates a motor-vehicle statute is negligent as a matter of law unless they introduce evidence that they were nonetheless acting as a reasonably prudent person under the circumstances. This is broader than the classic emergency excuse.

**Case: Barnum v. Williams** (Or. 1972)
- **Facts:** On a rainy day, Williams, driving downhill, saw Barnum's motorcycle near the center line on a sharp curve. She braked, slid over the center line, and hit him.
- **Issue:** Was the instruction allowing the jury to excuse the statutory violation if she acted reasonably correct?
- **Holding:** Yes. The jury could find she acted reasonably even though she crossed the line. Verdict for the defendant affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Jurisdictions treat a statutory violation in one of three ways: negligence per se, a rebuttable presumption (Oregon: D may show they acted as a reasonably prudent person), or mere evidence of negligence.

### When Not to Adopt a Statute
**Rule: When not to adopt a statute.** Courts won't use a criminal statute as the tort standard of care if doing so isn't appropriate. Factors: (1) whether the statute is the sole source of a duty (no common-law duty exists) or merely defines a preexisting duty; (2) whether it clearly defines the prohibited conduct; (3) whether it would impose liability without fault; (4) whether it would create liability for indirect harm (harm caused by third parties); (5) whether it would impose ruinous liability for a minor offense.

**Case: Perry v. S.N.** (Tex. 1998)
- **Facts:** The defendants allegedly saw a day-care operator sexually abusing children and failed to report it, in violation of the Texas Family Code. Parents of abused children sued for negligence per se.
- **Issue:** Does violating the mandatory child-abuse reporting statute support negligence per se?
- **Holding:** No. The statute would create a duty where none existed at common law, impose liability for indirect harm by a third party, and create vast liability for a misdemeanor. The negligence per se claim failed.
- **Takeaway:** Whether a court adopts a statute as the standard of care depends on whether it creates a new duty, how clearly it defines the conduct, whether it imposes liability without fault, whether it reaches harm caused by third parties, and whether liability would be ruinous for a minor offense.

### Custom
**Rule: Custom.** Evidence of industry custom is admissible but not conclusive. Following custom doesn't prove due care, and departing from it doesn't prove negligence. A whole industry can lag behind reasonable care (*The T.J. Hooper*).

**Case: Dempsey v. Addison Crane Co.** (D.D.C. 1965)
- **Facts:** A crane's auxiliary jib, held up by slings on open hooks (the common industry method), came loose and fell on pile drivers, killing one and injuring the plaintiff. A safer closed-shackle method was available.
- **Issue:** Does following the customary method of suspending the jib defeat negligence?
- **Holding:** No. The open-hook method was unsafe, and a reasonable operator would have used a safer attachment regardless of custom. The defendant was negligent.
- **Takeaway:** Industry custom is relevant evidence but not conclusive. D can be negligent while following custom if a reasonable person would have done more.

### Malpractice Standard
**Rule: Medical malpractice.** A physician must use the degree of care and skill expected of a reasonably competent practitioner in the same class (general practitioner or specialist), acting in the same or similar circumstances, considering advances in the profession and available facilities. Here, professional custom does set the standard (usually proven by expert testimony).
- The strict **locality rule** (standard of the same community) is rejected. The "same or similar locality" or national standard replaces it.

**Case: Shilkret v. Annapolis Emergency Hospital Ass'n** (Md. 1975)
- **Facts:** A baby suffered brain damage allegedly from negligence during delivery and after. The trial court applied the strict locality rule and directed verdicts when the plaintiffs' experts weren't familiar with local standards.
- **Issue:** What standard of care governs physicians and hospitals in Maryland?
- **Holding:** The strict locality rule is abandoned. The standard is that of a reasonably competent practitioner in the same class under similar circumstances, and hospitals similarly. The plaintiffs' expert evidence was enough to go to the jury.
- **Takeaway:** Doctors are held to the standard of a reasonably competent practitioner in the same field, under a national standard (no locality rule). P generally needs expert testimony.

### Overriding Medical Custom
**Rule: Overriding professional custom.** In an unusual case, a court may hold that reasonable prudence requires a precaution even when the profession's standard doesn't, if the test is simple, cheap, harmless, and would prevent grave, irreversible harm.

**Case: Helling v. Carey** (Wash. 1974)
- **Facts:** Helling, under 40, saw the defendant ophthalmologists for years. They never gave her a simple glaucoma pressure test, because the professional standard didn't call for routine testing of patients under 40. She lost much of her vision to undetected glaucoma.
- **Issue:** Does compliance with the professional standard protect the doctors from liability?
- **Holding:** No. As a matter of law, reasonable prudence required giving the test. The case was remanded for a damages trial.
- **Takeaway:** A court may find professional custom itself unreasonable when the precaution is cheap, simple, and harmless and prevents grave harm. The case is an outlier, and the legislature reversed it.

## Week 5 (CB 278–349) — Informed Consent; Res Ipsa Loquitur; Introduction to Duty

### Informed Consent
**Rule: Informed consent (negligence, not battery).** A physician must disclose the material risks of a treatment, feasible alternatives (including no treatment), and their risks. Materiality is measured by what a reasonable patient would want to know, not by medical custom.
- **Plaintiff must prove:** (1) the doctor failed to disclose a material risk; (2) the patient consented without knowing it; (3) a **reasonable patient** would not have consented if informed (objective causation); (4) the treatment caused the injury.
- **Defenses (doctor's burden):** **emergency** (the patient can't consent and delay would be harmful) and **therapeutic privilege** (disclosure itself would seriously harm the patient).
- Expert testimony is needed to establish the risks and alternatives, but **not** to establish what a reasonable patient would find material.
- Operating with **no consent at all**, or exceeding the scope of consent, is still **battery**.

**Case: Miller v. Kennedy** (Wash. Ct. App. 1974)
- **Facts:** Dr. Kennedy performed a kidney biopsy on Miller, which caused the loss of the kidney. Both sides agreed the biopsy itself wasn't malpractice. Miller testified he was never told of the risk of losing the kidney or of alternative biopsy methods.
- **Issue:** Did the jury instruction correctly state the duty to disclose?
- **Holding:** No. The instruction was misleading, so the court reversed and remanded for a new trial, adopting the **patient-centered** materiality standard.
- **Takeaway:** Informed consent uses either a patient-centered standard (what a reasonable patient would want to know about material risks) or a physician-centered one (what doctors customarily disclose). P must also prove that a properly informed patient would have declined the treatment.

### Origin of Res Ipsa Loquitur
**Rule: Res ipsa loquitur.** When an accident is of a kind that ordinarily doesn't happen without negligence and the instrumentality was under the defendant's control, the accident itself is evidence of negligence.

**Case: Byrne v. Boadle** (Exch. 1863)
- **Facts:** Byrne was walking past Boadle's flour shop when a barrel of flour fell from an upstairs window and hit him. No one saw why it fell.
- **Issue:** Is the fall of the barrel, without more, evidence of negligence?
- **Holding:** Yes. Barrels don't fall out of warehouse windows without negligence, and the defendant controlled the premises. The verdict for the plaintiff was entered. "The thing speaks for itself."
- **Takeaway:** Res ipsa lets P reach the jury on circumstantial evidence when the accident ordinarily doesn't happen without negligence and the instrumentality was under D's control.

### Modern Elements and Effect
**Rule: Res ipsa elements.** (1) The event ordinarily doesn't occur without someone's negligence; (2) it was caused by an instrumentality within the defendant's exclusive control; (3) it wasn't due to any voluntary action or contribution by the plaintiff.
- **Effect:** res ipsa creates a **permissive inference**, not a mandatory presumption. The jury **may**, but need not, infer negligence.
- **Summary judgment or directed verdict for the plaintiff** is possible only in the **rare** case where the inference is so strong that no reasonable jury could reject it.

**Case: Morejon v. Rais Construction Co.** (N.Y. 2006)
- **Facts:** The plaintiff's estate alleged that a roll of roofing material fell from a roof onto Fabio Pardo while he made a delivery. The defendant disputed whether the accident happened at all. The trial court granted the plaintiff summary judgment based on res ipsa.
- **Issue:** Can res ipsa ever support summary judgment for a plaintiff?
- **Holding:** Yes in principle, but only in exceptional cases. Not here, because material facts (including whether the accident occurred) were disputed. The reversal of summary judgment was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Res ipsa elements: (1) the event ordinarily doesn't occur without negligence, (2) D had exclusive control, and (3) P didn't contribute. In most states it creates a permissible inference, so P gets to the jury but doesn't win automatically.

### Rebutting the Inference
**Rule: Rebutting the res ipsa inference.** To get a directed verdict against a res ipsa inference, the defendant must produce evidence that destroys the inference or so completely contradicts it that reasonable people could no longer accept it. Evidence of the defendant's general care (inspections, precautions) usually isn't enough.

**Case: Swiney v. Malone Freight Lines** (Tenn. Ct. App. 1976)
- **Facts:** Two wheels came off a loaded tractor-trailer. The second rolled into Swiney's oncoming car. The defendants offered unrebutted evidence that the wheels had been properly inspected and maintained.
- **Issue:** Does res ipsa apply, and did the defendants' proof of due care entitle them to a directed verdict?
- **Holding:** Res ipsa applies to wheels coming off a moving truck. The defendants' evidence of inspection didn't destroy the inference, so the case was properly given to the jury. The verdict for the plaintiffs was affirmed.
- **Dissent:** The defendants' uncontradicted evidence of care should have won.
- **Takeaway:** Evidence of D's general care (inspections, precautions) doesn't defeat res ipsa. D gets a directed verdict only with evidence that destroys the inference.

### Multiple Defendants
**Rule: Res ipsa with multiple defendants.** When an unconscious patient suffers an unusual injury during medical treatment, all defendants who had control of the patient's body or the instruments may be required to explain their conduct. The "exclusive control" requirement is relaxed to a right of control.

**Case: Ybarra v. Spangard** (Cal. 1944)
- **Facts:** Ybarra went in for an appendectomy and woke up with a paralyzing injury to his right shoulder, an area unrelated to the surgery. He sued the surgeon, the anesthesiologist, the hospital owner, and the nurses. He couldn't say which one caused the injury.
- **Issue:** Can res ipsa apply against multiple defendants when the plaintiff can't identify which one or which instrument caused the harm?
- **Holding:** Yes. Each defendant had some control over the unconscious patient and owed him a duty of care. The nonsuit was reversed as to all defendants. (On retrial all were found liable.)
- **Takeaway:** When an unconscious patient is injured during treatment, res ipsa applies to every defendant who had control, shifting the burden to them to explain. Exclusive control is relaxed to a right of control.

### What Is Duty? (CB 315–20)
**Rule: Duty factors (common law).** Courts weigh (1) the relationship between the parties, (2) the foreseeability of harm, and (3) public policy. These are considerations to balance, not separate required elements.

**Rule: Restatement (Third) § 7.** An actor ordinarily owes a duty of reasonable care when their conduct creates a risk of physical harm. In exceptional cases, a court may find no duty (or a limited duty) only on an articulated policy ground that applies to a whole class of cases. Foreseeability belongs to breach and scope of liability, not duty.
- **Negligent entrustment:** a defendant who supplies a dangerous instrumentality (a car, a gun) to someone they know or should know is likely to misuse it can be liable when that person harms the plaintiff.

### Restatement (Third) Duty
**Rule: Duty under the Restatement (Third).** Following Restatement (Third) § 7, a duty of reasonable care exists whenever the defendant's conduct creates a risk of physical harm. Foreseeability is removed from the duty analysis; it is considered at breach and scope of liability.

**Case: Thompson v. Kaczinski** (Iowa 2009)
- **Facts:** The defendants took apart a trampoline and left its pieces unsecured in their yard, about 38 feet from a road. A storm blew the top onto the road. Thompson swerved to avoid it, lost control, and was injured.
- **Issue:** Did the landowners owe motorists a duty of care?
- **Holding:** Yes. Their conduct created a risk of harm, and no policy reason justified a no-duty rule. Summary judgment for the defendants was reversed and remanded.
- **Takeaway:** Under the Restatement (Third), a duty exists whenever D's conduct creates a risk of physical harm, so duty is the default. Foreseeability goes to breach and scope of liability, which are jury questions.

### Foreseeability-Based Duty
**Rule: Foreseeability-based duty (majority approach).** A duty exists only if the injury is foreseeable, judged by how the facts reasonably appeared to the defendant at the time, not in hindsight.

**Case: Brown v. Kerr** (Ky. Ct. App. 2010)
- **Facts:** The Kerrs kept their guns in a locked steel cabinet. Their nearly 20-year-old son secretly made a key, stole a gun, and later, after a day of heavy drug and alcohol use, killed his friend Roy.
- **Issue:** Did the parents owe Roy a duty to prevent their son from stealing the gun and using it?
- **Holding:** No. The son's theft and the killing weren't reasonably foreseeable to the parents, who had secured the guns and didn't know of his break-in. Summary judgment for the Kerrs was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Under the majority approach, foreseeability is part of duty and the judge decides it, often on summary judgment. This is the opposite of Thompson, where foreseeability goes to the jury.
- **From class:** folding case-specific foreseeability into duty is really breach analysis under a duty label. It lets the judge take the case from the jury. Contrast Delair: there the driver is charged with knowing the condition of his own tires (easy to inspect), while here the harm required predicting a third party's covert criminal act, and the Kerrs had taken precautions.

### The Foreseeable Plaintiff
**Rule: Foreseeable plaintiff (majority).** Negligence is relational. "The risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed." A defendant owes a duty only to plaintiffs within the zone of foreseeable danger. A plaintiff outside it can't recover, even if the defendant was negligent toward someone else.

**Rule: Duty to the world at large (dissent).** Everyone owes a duty to the world at large to refrain from unreasonably risky acts. If the act is negligent, anyone injured can recover, subject to proximate cause limits.

**Case: Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R.** (N.Y. 1928)
- **Facts:** Railroad guards helped a man carrying a newspaper-wrapped package jump onto a moving train. The package, which contained fireworks, fell and exploded. The shock knocked over scales at the other end of the platform, which fell on Mrs. Palsgraf.
- **Issue:** Did the railroad owe Palsgraf a duty when nothing suggested the package was dangerous to someone far away?
- **Holding:** No. The guards' conduct, if negligent at all, was negligent only toward the man and his package. Palsgraf was outside the range of apparent danger. The judgment for her was reversed and the complaint dismissed.
- **Takeaway:** Palsgraf governs unforeseeable plaintiffs. Cardozo (the majority view): a duty runs only to plaintiffs in the zone of foreseeable danger, decided by the court. Andrews: a duty runs to everyone, limited by proximate cause, often for the jury.

# UNIT III — Negligence: Duty (Weeks 6–7)

## Week 6 (CB 349–424) — Affirmative Duties; No Duty Based on Public Policy

### The No-Duty-to-Rescue Rule (CB 349–55)
**Rule: No duty to rescue.** A person has no general duty to aid or protect another, even when they know action is needed (Restatement (Second) § 314). Exceptions arise from:
1. a special relationship with the plaintiff,
2. a voluntary undertaking (taking charge or beginning a rescue),
3. the defendant's creating the risk or injuring the plaintiff (even innocently),
4. a special relationship with the dangerous third person, or
5. a statute imposing the duty.
- Misfeasance vs. nonfeasance: misfeasance (affirmative conduct creating an unreasonable risk) carries an automatic duty of reasonable care. Nonfeasance (failing to act to protect someone) creates a duty only if an exception applies.
- Risk creation (Restatement (Third) § 7 cmt. o): conduct creates a risk if it leaves the plaintiff worse off than if the defendant had never acted.
- A few states (e.g., Vermont) require reasonable assistance to someone exposed to grave harm when it can be given without danger. Negligently interfering with a third person's rescue is actionable (§ 327).
- Good Samaritan statutes immunize good-faith emergency rescuers (often only medical professionals) from liability, except for gross negligence or willful misconduct.

### Misfeasance vs. Nonfeasance
**Rule: Misfeasance vs. nonfeasance.** The court, not the plaintiff's label, characterizes the defendant's conduct. Failing to prevent harm or to warn is nonfeasance and requires a special relationship.

**Case: Price v. E.I. DuPont de Nemours & Co.** (Del. 2011)
- **Facts:** Bobby Price worked around asbestos at DuPont for decades. DuPont gave him no locker room, uniforms, or warnings, so he brought fibers home on his clothes. His wife Patricia washed his clothes for years and developed lung disease.
- **Issue:** Was DuPont's conduct misfeasance (duty automatic) or nonfeasance (special relationship required)?
- **Holding:** Nonfeasance. Patricia's claim was really a failure to warn or protect, and she had no special relationship with DuPont, so there was **no duty**. The denial of her motion to amend (as futile) was affirmed.
- **Dissent:** Using asbestos in the workplace is affirmative conduct that created the risk; that's misfeasance.
- **Takeaway:** Misfeasance (creating a risk) carries a duty, while nonfeasance (failing to prevent harm or warn) needs a special relationship. The court decides which label fits, and take-home exposure cases split on it.

### Special Relationship
**Rule: Special relationships (Restatement (Second) § 314A; (Third) § 40).** Recognized relationships: common carrier–passenger, innkeeper–guest, possessor of land open to the public–visitor, custodian–person in custody, employer–employee, school–student, landlord–tenant. Courts may recognize new ones by analogy. The duty covers risks arising within the scope of the relationship.

**Case: Grimes v. Kennedy Krieger Institute, Inc.** (Md. 2001)
- **Facts:** KKI, a Johns Hopkins–affiliated research institute, studied cheaper, partial lead-paint abatement methods by having families with young children live in partially abated houses and testing the children's blood-lead levels. It didn't promptly tell parents about dangerous dust readings. Children developed elevated lead levels.
- **Issue:** Can a special relationship arise between a nontherapeutic researcher and child research subjects?
- **Holding:** Yes. The consent agreements, the researcher–subject relationship, and federal research regulations could all support a duty. Summary judgment for KKI was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** A special relationship, and so a duty, can arise when one party has superior knowledge and control and the other depends on it, as with researchers and child subjects.

### Companions & Voluntary Rescue
**Rule: Voluntary rescuer (Restatement (Third) § 44).** Someone with no duty who takes charge of a person who appears imperiled and helpless must use reasonable care while the person is in their charge. They may stop helping, but not leave the person worse off than before.

**Rule: Gratuitous undertaking (§§ 323, 324A).** A person who undertakes to help is liable if their failure to use reasonable care increases the risk of harm or the harm results from reliance on the undertaking.

**Case: Farwell v. Keaton** (Mich. 1976)
- **Facts:** Siegrist and Farwell, both 18, were out together when a group of boys beat Farwell. Siegrist put ice on his head and drove him around for two hours while Farwell fell asleep in the back seat. Siegrist left him in the car in Farwell's grandparents' driveway without telling anyone. Farwell died three days later.
- **Issue:** Did Siegrist owe Farwell a duty to get help?
- **Holding:** Yes. Siegrist **voluntarily undertook** to help, and the two were **companions on a social venture**, a special relationship that implies each will help the other in an emergency. The jury verdict for the plaintiff was reinstated.
- **Takeaway:** A volunteer who starts to help must act reasonably and can't leave the victim worse off. Companions in a joint social venture may owe each other a duty to aid.

### Duty After Causing Harm
**Rule: Duty after causing harm (Restatement (Second) § 322; (Third) § 39).** If the actor's conduct, tortious or innocent, causes bodily harm that leaves another helpless and in danger of further harm, the actor must use reasonable care to prevent further harm.

**Case: Maldonado v. Southern Pacific Transportation Co.** (Ariz. Ct. App. 1981)
- **Facts:** Maldonado was trying to board a moving freight train when the crew jerked the cars, and he fell under the wheels. His arm was severed. As the caboose passed, he called to two employees on the platform for help; they did nothing.
- **Issue:** Did the railroad owe a separate duty to help him after its train injured him?
- **Holding:** Yes. Under § 322, once its instrumentality left him helpless, the railroad had a duty to render reasonable aid, regardless of whether the original injury was tortious. Dismissal of that count was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** If D's conduct, even innocent conduct, leaves P helpless, D must take reasonable steps to prevent further harm.

### Duty to Control Third Persons
**Rule: No duty to control others (Restatement (Second) § 315; (Third) § 41).** No duty to control a third person to protect others unless (a) a special relationship exists with the dangerous person (therapist–patient, custodian–person in custody, parent–child, employer–employee), or (b) a special relationship exists with the victim.
- **Tarasoff:** a therapist whose patient presents a serious danger of violence to a **readily identifiable victim** must take reasonable steps to protect that victim.
- **Identifiable victim:** the duty to **warn** runs only to specific, identifiable victims, not the public at large.

**Case: Thompson v. County of Alameda** (Cal. 1980)
- **Facts:** The county released a juvenile offender, James, to his mother's home. It knew he had violent tendencies toward young children and had said he would kill a child in the neighborhood if released, but he named no one. Within 24 hours he killed the Thompsons' son, who lived a few doors away.
- **Issue:** Did the county owe a duty to warn the police, the neighborhood parents, or James's mother?
- **Holding:** No. The victim was not **identifiable**; he was one of a large, amorphous group. Warnings to the public would be of little value and could undermine the rehabilitation program. Dismissal was affirmed.
- **Dissent (Tobriner):** The threat was specific enough, and a warning to the mother at least would have been easy.
- **Takeaway:** A duty to control or warn about a dangerous person requires a special relationship and a specific, identifiable victim (Tarasoff). A general threat to the community creates no duty.

### Statutory Duty
**Rule: Implied private right of action.** When a statute doesn't expressly provide one, the court asks: (1) is the plaintiff in the class the statute was enacted to benefit; (2) would a private right promote the legislative purpose; and (3) is a private right consistent with the legislative scheme? The third factor is usually decisive.

**Case: Uhr v. East Greenbush Central School District** (N.Y. 1999)
- **Facts:** A New York statute required schools to screen students for scoliosis each year. The school allegedly skipped the plaintiff's screening one year, and by the time her scoliosis was detected, it had worsened and required surgery.
- **Issue:** Does the statute create a private right of action, or did the school assume a common-law duty?
- **Holding:** No to both. The statute expressly **immunized** schools from liability for screening and was enforced by the Commissioner of Education, so a private action would conflict with the scheme. There was no special relationship giving rise to a common-law duty. Summary judgment for the school was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** A statute creates a private tort duty only if P is in the protected class, a private right would advance the statute's purpose, and a private right fits the legislative scheme. This is distinct from negligence per se, which borrows a statute to set the standard for an existing duty.

### No Duty: Crushing Liability
**Rule: No duty: crushing liability.** Even when harm is foreseeable, courts may limit duty as a matter of public policy to keep liability within a controllable degree and avoid crushing liability to an indeterminate class. A contractual relationship can mark the boundary of the "orbit of duty."

**Case: Strauss v. Belle Realty Co.** (N.Y. 1985)
- **Facts:** Con Edison's **gross negligence** caused a 25-hour blackout across New York City. Strauss, 77, fell on the dark basement stairs of his apartment building while getting water. Con Ed supplied his apartment under his own contract, but supplied the **common areas** under a contract with the landlord.
- **Issue:** Did Con Ed owe a duty to a tenant injured in a common area it served only under the landlord's contract?
- **Holding:** No. In a citywide blackout, liability is limited to those in **privity** with the utility for the area where the injury occurred. Otherwise liability would be limitless. Dismissal was affirmed.
- **Dissent:** Foreseeable plaintiffs should be able to recover; the majority leaves seriously injured people without a remedy against a grossly negligent defendant.
- **Takeaway:** Courts may deny a duty even to foreseeable plaintiffs to avoid crushing liability to an indeterminate class (a citywide blackout). A contractual relationship can mark where the duty ends.

### No Duty: Social Hosts
**Rule: Social host liability (Texas).** A social host owes no common-law duty to third parties injured by an intoxicated adult guest who drives. The guest, not the host, controls the decision to drink and drive. Texas's dram shop statute covers only commercial providers.
- **Texas duty factors:** foreseeability and likelihood of injury weighed against the **social utility** of the conduct, the **burden** of guarding against injury, the consequences of placing the burden on the defendant, and whether the defendant had a **right to control** the actor or **superior knowledge** of the danger.

**Case: Graff v. Beard** (Tex. 1993)
- **Facts:** Moos drank at a party hosted by the Graffs and left intoxicated. His car hit Beard's motorcycle. Beard sued the hosts.
- **Issue:** Should Texas impose a common-law duty on social hosts who serve alcohol to an adult guest they know will drive?
- **Holding:** No. A host has no legal right to control an adult guest; the guest is primarily responsible; and the legislature chose to cover only commercial providers. The court of appeals' recognition of a duty was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Most courts refuse to make social hosts liable to third parties injured by adult guests who drive drunk; dram shop statutes cover only commercial sellers. The court relied on deference to the legislature and on the guest's personal responsibility.

## Week 7 (CB 424–463, 505–540, 560–577) — Landowner Liability; Immunities; Emotional Harm; Economic Harm

### The Traditional Trichotomy (CB 424–30)
**Rule: Entrant status (Restatement (Second) § 332).** The duty a land possessor owes depends on the entrant's status at the time of injury:
- Invitee: enters with permission either (1) for a purpose in which the possessor has a material (business) interest, or (2) as a member of the public on land held open to the public. Duty: reasonable care to inspect, repair, and warn of dangers the possessor knew or would discover by reasonable inspection.
- Licensee: enters with permission but for their own purposes, including social guests and people using a shortcut with implied permission. Duty: warn of or make safe known dangers that the licensee is unlikely to discover.
- Trespasser: no permission, or exceeds the scope of permission. Duty: only to refrain from willful or wanton injury. The trespasser takes the land as they find it.
- Activities vs. conditions: the limited duties apply to conditions on the land. For the possessor's active conduct, ordinary reasonable care is owed to invitees and licensees (and known trespassers).
- Known or foreseeable trespassers: most courts owe known trespassers reasonable care regarding known dangers; Restatement (Second) § 335 extends this to constant trespassers on a limited area.
- Open and obvious dangers: a minority treat them as no duty; the majority consider obviousness at breach and comparative fault.
- Related rules: recreational-use statutes (no liability without a fee unless willful or malicious); the firefighter's rule (professional rescuers can't recover for the negligence that created the emergency); duty to people outside the land for artificial conditions near a highway; a duty to aid a visitor injured on the land, whatever their status (Restatement (Third) § 52).

### Exceeding the License
**Rule: Exceeding the license.** Status is fixed at the moment of injury. An entrant who goes beyond the scope of an implied license (in area or purpose) becomes a trespasser. Whether a duty exists is a question of law; without a duty, even reckless conduct by the possessor is immaterial.

**Case: Cochran v. Burger King Corp.** (Mo. Ct. App. 1996)
- **Facts:** At 2 a.m., an intoxicated Cochran cut across a closed Burger King's dark lot as a shortcut. He went into a dumpster enclosure and tried to climb its 8-foot wall. Burger King's manager knew the wall had been damaged by a trash truck a month earlier and was dangerous, but never fixed it. It collapsed and Cochran lost his leg.
- **Issue:** Did Burger King owe Cochran a duty regarding the unstable wall?
- **Holding:** No. As a shortcut user he may have been a licensee, but by **climbing the wall** he exceeded any implied license and became a trespasser. Burger King didn't have to anticipate the climb. The jury verdict (including punitive damages) was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** P's status is fixed at the moment of injury. Going beyond the scope of an invitation, in area or purpose, makes an invitee a trespasser, owed only freedom from willful or wanton injury.

### Abolishing the Licensee/Invitee Distinction
**Rule: Duty to lawful visitors (N.C.).** Owners owe reasonable care to all lawful visitors (former licensees and invitees). Trespassers remain a separate category, owed only freedom from willful injury.

**Rule: Reasonable care to all entrants (Rowland v. Christian, Cal.).** The question is whether the possessor acted as a reasonable person given the probability of injury. Status is a factor, not determinative. (Most states that abolished the categories still give some protection against trespasser suits, e.g., Cal. Civ. Code § 847 for trespassers committing felonies.)

**Case: Nelson v. Freeland** (N.C. 1998)
- **Facts:** Freeland asked his friend and business partner Nelson to pick him up for a business meeting. Nelson tripped over a stick Freeland had left on his porch. It was unclear whether Nelson was a licensee (social guest) or an invitee (mutual business).
- **Issue:** Should North Carolina keep the common-law trichotomy?
- **Holding:** No, in part. The court **merged licensees and invitees** into one category of lawful visitors owed reasonable care, kept trespassers separate, and gave Nelson a jury trial under the new rule.
- **Policy:** The categories are confusing, arbitrary, and outdated; they protect landowners' status more than human safety. Abolition lets juries apply ordinary negligence principles. Against: predictability, landowner autonomy, and insurance costs.
- **Takeaway:** There are three regimes for land entrants: the traditional trichotomy; partial abolition (reasonable care to all lawful visitors, with trespassers separate; Nelson); and full abolition (reasonable care to all, with status only a factor; Rowland).

### Attractive Nuisance
**Rule: Attractive nuisance (Restatement (Second) § 339).** A possessor is liable to trespassing children for physical harm caused by an artificial condition if:
- (a) the possessor knows or has reason to know children are likely to trespass there;
- (b) the possessor knows or has reason to know of the condition and that it poses an unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily harm to children;
- (c) the children, because of their youth, don't discover the condition or realize the risk;
- (d) the utility of maintaining the condition and the burden of eliminating it are slight compared with the risk to children; and
- (e) the possessor fails to use reasonable care to eliminate the danger or protect the children.
- An adult who tries to rescue a child from an attractive nuisance takes on the child's status and is owed ordinary care.
- Rescue doctrine: a rescuer who doesn't act rashly may recover from the person whose negligence created the peril.

**Case: Bennett v. Stanley** (Ohio 2001)
- **Facts:** The Stanleys let their unused swimming pool fill with rainwater, removed its cover and part of the fencing, and let it become a pond with frogs and algae-slick sides. They knew the young Bennett children next door played outside unsupervised. Five-year-old Chance went to look at the frogs and fell in; his mother Cher drowned trying to save him, and both died.
- **Issue:** Does Ohio recognize attractive nuisance, and what duty is owed to an adult who tries to rescue a child?
- **Holding:** Ohio adopted § 339. A child trespasser can recover if its elements are met, and the mother, as a rescuer, took on the child's status. Summary judgment for the Stanleys was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** A child trespasser can recover under attractive nuisance (§ 339) if (a) D knows children are likely to trespass, (b) D knows the condition poses an unreasonable risk to them, (c) the children don't appreciate the risk because of their youth, (d) the burden of eliminating it is slight compared to the risk, and (e) D failed to use reasonable care.

### Duty to Protect from Crime
**Rule: Duty to protect from third-party crime.** Businesses aren't insurers of patrons' safety, but they must take reasonable measures against foreseeable third-party crime. Four tests for foreseeability:
1. Specific harm: the owner knew of specific, imminent harm (narrowest).
2. Prior similar incidents: previous crimes on or near the premises, considering recency, frequency, and similarity.
3. Totality of the circumstances: all factors, including the area's crime level (broadest).
4. Balancing (Posecai, adopted): weigh the foreseeability and gravity of the harm against the burden of the precaution. The more foreseeable the crime, the more is required. A very high degree of foreseeability is needed to require guards.

**Case: Posecai v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.** (La. 1999)
- **Facts:** Posecai was robbed at gunpoint in a Sam's Club parking lot in daylight by a man hiding under her car. Sam's had no parking-lot security. There had been only three predatory crimes on the premises in six years, though the neighborhood nearby had high crime.
- **Issue:** Did Sam's owe a duty to provide parking-lot security?
- **Holding:** Businesses do owe a duty against foreseeable crime, measured by the **balancing test**. Here the crime wasn't foreseeable enough to require guards or other security. The judgment for Posecai was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Businesses must take reasonable precautions against foreseeable crime, measured by one of four tests: specific harm, prior similar incidents, totality of the circumstances, or balancing (adopted here). Landlords may also owe tenants a duty when they have notice (Kline).

### Emotional Harm — Overview (CB 505–10)
**Rule: Parasitic damages.** Emotional distress that accompanies a physical injury (pain and suffering) is always recoverable.

**Rule: Negligent infliction of emotional distress (NIED).** For stand-alone emotional harm, courts impose a duty only in discrete categories: zone of danger, bystanders, mishandled corpses, death notices, false diagnosis of a dreaded disease, and similar special relationships.
- The old **impact rule** (Ward) required physical contact; most states have abandoned it.
- Most courts require **physical manifestation** or an objectively **diagnosable** psychological injury.
- **Fear of disease:** mere exposure isn't enough; most courts require current physical symptoms. HIV-exposure claims are more often allowed, limited to the window before a negative test.
- **Eggshell psyche:** if an ordinarily sensitive person would have been harmed, the defendant pays for the plaintiff's full harm, however sensitive.

### Zone of Danger
**Rule: Zone of danger.** A plaintiff who suffers fright from a reasonable fear of immediate personal injury, which causes substantial bodily injury or sickness, may recover without any impact.

**Case: Falzone v. Busch** (N.J. 1965)
- **Facts:** The defendant's car hit Charles Falzone, then veered toward his wife Mabel, who was sitting in their parked car. It came close enough to make her fear for her own safety, but didn't hit her. She became ill from the fright.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff placed in danger, but not struck, recover for physical illness caused by fear for her own safety?
- **Holding:** Yes. The **impact rule** (Ward) was overruled. The court rejected the policy objections (fraud, floodgates, difficulty proving causation) as manageable.
- **Takeaway:** Under the zone-of-danger test, P recovers for emotional harm only if P was in the zone of physical danger, reasonably feared for their own safety, and suffered substantial bodily injury or illness as a result.

### Bystanders
**Rule: Bystander recovery (N.J.).** A plaintiff may recover for emotional distress from witnessing another's injury by proving (1) the death or serious physical injury of another caused by the defendant's negligence; (2) a marital or intimate family relationship with the victim; (3) observation of the death or injury at the scene; and (4) resulting severe emotional distress.
- **Dillon v. Legg** (Cal.) factors: closeness to the scene, **sensory and contemporaneous** observation, close relationship. (California later made these strict requirements in Thing v. La Chusa.)
- Jurisdictions vary on each element: arriving shortly after the accident, mistaken beliefs of injury, unmarried partners (Dunphy factors: duration, mutual dependence, shared life), and harm to property or pets.

**Case: Portee v. Jaffee** (N.J. 1980)
- **Facts:** Seven-year-old Guy Portee was trapped between an elevator door and the shaft wall in the defendants' building and dragged up to the third floor. His mother watched for four and a half hours as police tried to free him. He died in the shaft.
- **Issue:** Can a parent who was never in danger recover for watching her child suffer and die?
- **Holding:** Yes. The defendant's duty extends to the emotional harm of a close family member who **observes** at the scene the death or serious injury caused by its negligence. Summary judgment for the defendants was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Bystander recovery requires (1) D negligently caused death or serious injury, (2) a close family relationship with the victim, (3) observation of the death or injury at the scene, and (4) resulting severe emotional distress.

### Duty Through Ordinary Negligence
**Rule: NIED as ordinary negligence (Cal.).** NIED is not an independent tort; it's ordinary negligence, so the plaintiff must show a duty. Duty is decided under the Rowland factors: foreseeability of harm, certainty of injury, closeness of connection, moral blame, policy of preventing harm, burden on the defendant, and insurance. (Public agencies add factors such as the impact on public resources.)
- **Mishandled remains** (Restatement (Second) § 868) and **death communications** are recognized NIED categories.

**Case: Catsouras v. Department of the California Highway Patrol** (Cal. Ct. App. 2010)
- **Facts:** An 18-year-old was decapitated in a car crash. CHP officers emailed photos of the scene to friends and family "for shock value on Halloween." The photos spread to thousands of websites, and the family was taunted with them.
- **Issue:** Did the CHP owe the family a duty of care?
- **Holding:** Yes. Under the Rowland factors, the officers owed the family a duty not to put the death images on the internet for the "lurid titillation" of people with no official purpose. Dismissal was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** In California, NIED is ordinary negligence, so P must show a duty under the Rowland factors. Outrageous conduct and moral blame (e.g., leaking accident-scene photos) support finding one.

### No General Duty (Texas)
**Rule: No general duty to avoid emotional distress (Texas).** There is no general duty not to negligently inflict emotional distress. Mental anguish damages are recoverable only for the breach of some other legal duty (physical injury, wrongful death, battery, defamation, privacy invasion, mishandled corpse, a special relationship, etc.). Where emotional distress is a recognized element of damages for that duty, no physical manifestation is required.

**Case: Boyles v. Kerr** (Tex. 1993)
- **Facts:** Boyles, 17, secretly videotaped himself having sex with Kerr, 19, and showed the tape to friends. Word spread at both of their universities. Kerr suffered humiliation and serious distress. She sued only for **negligent** infliction of emotional distress.
- **Issue:** Does Texas recognize an independent cause of action for NIED?
- **Holding:** No. The court overruled earlier language recognizing an independent NIED tort and remanded in the interest of justice so Kerr could pursue other theories (such as intentional infliction or invasion of privacy).
- **Takeaway:** In restrictive states (Texas), there's no freestanding NIED claim; emotional damages require breach of some other duty. Plaintiffs plead negligence rather than an intentional tort to reach insurance coverage, which excludes intentional acts.

### Pure Economic Loss
**Rule: Economic loss rule.** As a default, tort law doesn't allow recovery for purely economic loss (no personal injury or property damage). Under Aikens, a plaintiff with purely economic loss from an interruption in commerce can't recover without (1) physical harm to their person or property, (2) a contractual relationship with the defendant, or (3) a special relationship showing the defendant had a duty to that particular plaintiff and the harm was clearly foreseeable to it.
- **Special relationship:** the plaintiff is affected **differently from society in general**, and the defendant knew or had specific reason to know of the consequences, the people likely to be hurt, and the damages likely to result.
- **Robins Dry Dock:** no recovery for economic loss from negligent damage to **someone else's** property.
- **Minority approaches:** *People Express* (N.J.) allows recovery by **particular, identifiable** plaintiffs whose economic loss the defendant had reason to know of; *J'Aire* (Cal.) allows recovery with a special relationship even without privity.
- **Exception:** commercial fishermen can recover lost profits from oil spills (*Union Oil v. Oppen*). The federal Oil Pollution Act allows broad economic recovery for oil spills.
- In **product** cases, the economic loss rule bars tort recovery for damage to the product itself (*East River*); contract and warranty govern.

**Case: Aikens v. Debow** (W. Va. 2000)
- **Facts:** A truck carrying an oversized trackhoe hit a highway overpass, closing it for 19 days. Aikens's motel and restaurant, reachable by detour, lost about $9,000 in income. He had no injury, no property damage, and no contract with the defendants.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff with purely economic loss from negligent damage to someone else's property recover?
- **Holding:** No. Without physical harm, a contract, or a special relationship, there is no duty. The plaintiff was affected the same as many other businesses.
- **Takeaway:** In most courts, P can't recover purely economic loss in negligence without physical injury or property damage, to avoid indeterminate liability. A special relationship is the main exception.

# UNIT IV — Causation & Scope of Liability (Weeks 8–9)

## Week 8 (CB 579–653) — Cause in Fact; Multiple Causes and Defendants; Scope of Liability Foundations

### General Principles of Cause in Fact (CB 579–82)
**Rule: But-for causation.** The defendant's negligence is a factual cause if the harm would not have occurred without it. The plaintiff must prove this more likely than not (preponderance). A mere possibility isn't enough, and "post hoc ergo propter hoc" (it happened after, so it was caused by) isn't proof.
- **All-or-nothing rule:** if the plaintiff proves causation by a preponderance, they recover 100%; if not, nothing.
- **Relaxed causation:** courts are sometimes lenient when the defendant omitted a precaution aimed at the **very risk** that came about, and harm of that exact type happened (Zuchowicz; Haft).

### Circumstantial Proof
**Rule: Circumstantial proof of causation.** When several causes are possible and the defendant is responsible for only one, the plaintiff need not eliminate every other cause. It's enough to prove facts showing with reasonable certainty that the defendant's cause was the direct cause.

**Case: Stubbs v. City of Rochester** (N.Y. 1919)
- **Facts:** The city negligently let sewage-polluted river water (meant for firefighting) mix into its drinking water system. Stubbs drank the city water at work and home and caught typhoid. About 58 people in the affected area got typhoid, and citywide cases spiked. The city argued typhoid has many possible causes (food, flies, milk, other carriers).
- **Issue:** Must a plaintiff rule out every other possible cause of the disease?
- **Holding:** No. The plaintiff's evidence (his drinking habits, the outbreak cluster, medical testimony) gave a reasonable basis for finding the water was the cause. The nonsuit was reversed and a new trial granted.
- **Takeaway:** P need not rule out every other possible cause. Proof with reasonable certainty that D's breach was the likely cause, including through statistics and inference, is enough to reach the jury.

### Conflicting Experts
**Rule: Conflicting expert testimony.** When qualified experts disagree, causation is a jury question. Rational inferences from a sequence of proven events can establish causation, and an expert's opinion need not be free from doubt.

**Case: Daly v. Bergstedt** (Minn. 1964)
- **Facts:** Daly tripped over molding left in a store aisle and bruised her breast. Fourteen months later, she was diagnosed with breast cancer at the exact site of the bruise. Six doctors testified trauma can't cause cancer; one testified it did.
- **Issue:** Can a verdict that the fall caused the cancer stand on that evidence?
- **Holding:** Yes. The conflicting expert testimony and the sequence of events created a fact question for the jury.
- **Takeaway:** When qualified experts disagree, causation goes to the jury. Modern courts now screen out weak science like this under Daubert and Joiner before it reaches the jury.

### Gatekeeping Expert Testimony
**Rule: FRE 702 / Daubert.** The trial judge is the gatekeeper and must ensure expert testimony is relevant and reliable: the expert is qualified, the testimony helps the jury, it rests on sufficient facts or data, uses reliable methods, and applies them reliably to the case. (*Kumho Tire* applies this to all experts, not just scientists.)
- **Analytical gap (Joiner):** a court may exclude an opinion connected to the data "only by the **ipse dixit** of the expert."
- Review of admissibility rulings is for **abuse of discretion**.
- Toxic tort plaintiffs must show **general causation** (the substance can cause this disease) and **specific causation** (it caused this plaintiff's disease).

**Case: General Electric Co. v. Joiner** (U.S. 1997)
- **Facts:** Joiner, an electrician exposed to PCB-laced transformer fluid, developed lung cancer. He was a smoker with a family history of lung cancer. His experts relied on studies of mice injected with massive PCB doses and on epidemiological studies that didn't find a link.
- **Issue:** What's the standard of review for excluding expert testimony, and was exclusion proper?
- **Holding:** Abuse of discretion; and the trial court didn't abuse its discretion. The studies didn't support the experts' conclusions, leaving too great an analytical gap. The Eleventh Circuit's reversal was itself reversed and the case remanded.
- **Takeaway:** Trial judges act as gatekeepers for expert testimony and are reviewed only for abuse of discretion. A judge may exclude an opinion when there's "too great an analytical gap" between the data and the conclusion, which often decides toxic tort causation.

### Loss of Chance
**Rule: Loss of chance.** In medical malpractice, a patient's chance of survival or a better outcome is valuable even if under 50%. Loss of chance is treated as the injury itself, not a relaxed causation rule. The plaintiff must prove by a preponderance that the negligence reduced that chance.
- **Proportional damages:** full wrongful-death damages × (chance before negligence − chance after). In Matsuyama: $875,000 × 37.5% = $328,125.
- Largely limited to **medical malpractice**; courts refuse to extend it to legal malpractice or lost business opportunities. Some states reject the doctrine entirely.
- **Future harm:** most courts let a plaintiff sue later for a disease if and when it develops, an exception to the single-judgment rule.

**Case: Matsuyama v. Birnbaum** (Mass. 2008)
- **Facts:** Dr. Birnbaum treated Matsuyama, a patient with known risk factors for gastric cancer, for years without testing for it. By the time cancer was diagnosed, it was too advanced, and he died. Had it been caught earlier, his chance of survival would have been less than 50%.
- **Issue:** Does Massachusetts allow recovery for a lost chance of survival that was below 50%?
- **Holding:** Yes. Loss of chance is recognized as a theory of injury in malpractice and wrongful-death cases, with damages measured proportionally. The verdict for the plaintiff was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** In loss-of-chance jurisdictions, a patient who was already more likely than not to die can still recover for the lost chance of survival. Damages are proportional: full damages times the percentage of chance lost.

### Multiple Sufficient Causes
**Rule: Concurrent sufficient causes.** When two causes, each attributable to a responsible person and each sufficient alone to cause the harm, combine, each wrongdoer is liable for the entire loss (Restatement (Third) § 27). The but-for test fails here, so courts use the substantial factor test.
- **Exceptions:** the other cause had a **natural origin** (lightning), or it was so much larger that it **swallowed up** the defendant's cause.

**Case: Kingston v. Chicago & Northwest Railway Co.** (Wis. 1927)
- **Facts:** Two fires merged and destroyed Kingston's property. The railroad's locomotive negligently set one. The other's origin was unknown but apparently human. Either fire alone would have destroyed the property.
- **Issue:** Is the railroad liable for the entire loss when the other fire alone would have caused it?
- **Holding:** Yes. Each wrongdoer is liable for the whole. The railroad had the burden to show the other fire was of natural origin, and it didn't.
- **Takeaway:** When two independent forces would each have caused the harm alone, but-for causation fails, and the substantial factor test applies instead. Each wrongdoer is a cause and is liable.

### Alternative Liability
**Rule: Alternative liability.** When all defendants acted negligently toward the plaintiff, but only one caused the harm, and the plaintiff can't tell which through no fault of their own, the burden shifts to each defendant to prove they didn't cause it (Restatement (Second) § 433B(3); (Third) § 28(b)). All possible wrongdoers must be before the court.

**Case: Summers v. Tice** (Cal. 1948)
- **Facts:** Two hunters both negligently shot at a quail in the plaintiff's direction. A pellet hit him in the eye. It came from one gun, but no one could tell which.
- **Issue:** Can both be held liable when only one caused the injury?
- **Holding:** Yes. Both negligently created the risk, and they are in a better position than the innocent plaintiff to show who caused it. Each must exonerate himself, and neither could, so both were jointly and severally liable.
- **Takeaway:** When two or more negligent defendants exposed P to harm and P can't tell which caused it, the burden shifts to each defendant to prove they didn't. All the possible wrongdoers must be before the court.

### Concert of Action; Joint and Several Liability
**Rule: Concert of action (Restatement § 876).** A defendant is liable for another's tortious conduct if they ordered or induced it, knowingly gave substantial assistance or encouragement to a known breach of duty, or substantially assisted a tortious result while their own conduct was itself a breach.

**Rule: Joint and several liability.** Defendants who act in concert, or whose independent negligence causes an indivisible injury, are each liable for the entire harm. The plaintiff may collect from any of them but gets only one satisfaction. Defendants can seek contribution from each other. (See Week 10 for modern reforms.)
- **Enterprise liability (Hall v. du Pont):** in a small industry that **jointly controlled the risk** through common safety standards, the burden of causation shifts to all members.

### Market Share Liability
**Rule: Market share liability.** When (1) the product is fungible (made from an identical formula), (2) the plaintiff can't identify the maker through no fault of her own, and (3) she joins manufacturers of a substantial share of the market, the burden shifts to each defendant to prove it couldn't have made the product. Each non-exonerated defendant pays its share of the market.
- **Brown v. Superior Court:** market-share liability is **several only**; the plaintiff bears the shares of absent or bankrupt makers.
- **Risk contribution (Collins, Wis.):** each maker that contributed to the risk can be liable, even without a substantial market share.

**Case: Sindell v. Abbott Laboratories** (Cal. 1980)
- **Facts:** Sindell's mother took DES during pregnancy to prevent miscarriage. Years later, Sindell developed cancer caused by the drug. About 200 companies made DES from the same formula, and after decades she couldn't identify which one made her mother's pills.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff who can't identify the maker of the injuring product recover from manufacturers of the identical drug?
- **Holding:** Yes, under the new **market share** theory. Alternative liability (not all possible makers were joined), concert of action, and industry-wide liability didn't fit, but the policies behind Summers support shifting the burden. The dismissal was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Four theories address unidentifiable manufacturers of fungible products (DES): alternative liability, concert of action, enterprise liability, and market share. Under market share, each defendant pays its share of the market.

### Introduction to Scope of Liability (CB 634–37)
**Rule: Scope of liability.** Scope of liability (proximate cause) presupposes cause in fact. It asks whether, even though the defendant was negligent and caused the harm, the harm is among the results for which the defendant should be held responsible. Under the Restatement (Third), liability is limited to harms that result from the risks that made the conduct negligent (the risk rule). It's usually a question for the factfinder.

### Direct Consequences
**Rule: Direct consequences test.** Foreseeability determines whether the act is negligent. Once it is, the defendant is liable for all damage directly traceable to it, even if the type of damage was unforeseeable, unless independent intervening causes break the chain.

**Case: In re Polemis & Furness, Withy & Co.** (K.B. 1921)
- **Facts:** Stevedores (the charterers' servants) negligently let a plank fall into a ship's hold, which was filled with petrol vapor. The plank caused a spark, which ignited the vapor, and the ship burned. Arbitrators found some damage from a falling plank was foreseeable, but a fire was not.
- **Issue:** Is the defendant liable for an unforeseeable fire caused directly by its negligence?
- **Holding:** Yes. Because some damage was foreseeable, the act was negligent, and the defendant is liable for all **direct** consequences, including the unforeseeable fire.
- **Takeaway:** Under the direct consequences test, D is liable for all direct consequences of negligence, even unforeseeable ones. Wagon Mound I later rejected it, and Andrews's Palsgraf dissent endorses it.

### Duty vs. Proximate Cause
**Rule: Duty before proximate cause.** The question of liability comes before the measure of consequences. If the plaintiff was outside the range of foreseeable danger, there's no duty to her, so proximate cause is never reached.

**Rule: Proximate cause.** Everyone owes a duty to the world at large. Liability is limited by proximate cause, which is a matter of "convenience, public policy, [and] a rough sense of justice." Factors: a natural and continuous sequence between cause and effect; whether the act was a substantial factor; a direct connection without too many intervening causes; whether the result was foreseeable; and remoteness in time and space.
- **Takeaway:** Cardozo: no duty to an unforeseeable plaintiff, so proximate cause is never reached (the majority view). Andrews: a duty to everyone, with liability limited by proximate cause factors (natural and continuous sequence, substantial factor, directness, foreseeability, and remoteness in time and space).

## Week 9 (CB 653–725) — Scope of Liability (cont.); Intervening Causes; Contributory Negligence

### Foreseeability of the Kind of Harm
**Rule: Reasonable foreseeability test.** A negligent defendant is liable only for damage of a kind a reasonable person should have foreseen. Polemis's direct consequences test is rejected.
- **Restatement (Third) § 29 (risk rule):** liability is limited to harms that result from **the risks that made the conduct tortious**.
- **Restatement (Second) § 435(2):** not a legal cause if, looking back, it appears **highly extraordinary** that the conduct brought about the harm.

**Case: Overseas Tankship (U.K.) Ltd. v. Morts Dock & Engineering Co. (The Wagon Mound No. 1)** (P.C. 1961)
- **Facts:** The defendant's crew carelessly spilled a large amount of furnace oil into Sydney Harbour. It drifted under the plaintiff's wharf, where welding was going on. The plaintiff's manager was told furnace oil on water wouldn't burn and resumed welding. Two days later, molten metal ignited a rag floating on the oil, and the fire destroyed the wharf. The trial court found the defendant couldn't reasonably have known the oil would burn on water.
- **Issue:** Is a negligent defendant liable for direct damage of a kind that wasn't foreseeable?
- **Holding:** No. Polemis "should no longer be regarded as good law." Fire damage wasn't foreseeable, so the defendant wasn't liable for it (only the foreseeable fouling damage).
- **Takeaway:** D is liable only for harm of a foreseeable kind, not for all direct consequences. Wagon Mound I replaced the Polemis direct consequences test in England.

### Type vs. Manner of Harm
**Rule: Type vs. manner of harm.** Only the type (genus) of harm must be foreseeable, not the precise manner in which it happens or the exact sequence of events.

**Case: Hughes v. Lord Advocate** (H.L. 1963)
- **Facts:** Post Office workers left an open manhole under a tent, surrounded by lit paraffin lamps, while they went on break. An 8-year-old took a lamp down the manhole; coming out, he knocked it into the hole. Paraffin vaporized and **exploded** (an unlikely event), and he fell in and was badly burned.
- **Issue:** Is the defendant liable for burns caused by an unforeseeable explosion when burns from the lamp were foreseeable?
- **Holding:** Yes. The accident was of a foreseeable **type** (a burning injury from a lamp), and the explosion was an immaterial detail in the chain of causation.
- **Takeaway:** Only the general type of harm must be foreseeable, not the precise manner. Plaintiffs should describe the risk broadly ("burns"); defendants narrowly ("explosion").

### The Counterpoint
**Rule: Unforeseeable kind of harm.** An unforeseeable kind of harm isn't within the scope of liability just because a different foreseeable risk accompanied the same act. An act that would create no foreseeable risk of this harm if done deliberately doesn't become negligent because it was inadvertent.

**Case: Doughty v. Turner Manufacturing Co.** (C.A. 1964)
- **Facts:** A worker accidentally knocked an asbestos-cement cover into a vat of molten liquid at 800°C. A **splash** was foreseeable, but the cover slid in without splashing. A minute or two later, an unknown chemical reaction in the cover caused an **eruption** that burned the plaintiff.
- **Issue:** Is the employer liable for an unforeseeable eruption when only a splash was foreseeable?
- **Holding:** No. The eruption wasn't foreseeable and wasn't a variant of the splash risk. Judgment for the defendant.
- **Takeaway:** When the actual harm differs in kind from the foreseeable risk (an eruption rather than a splash), D isn't liable. Read with Hughes, it shows the outcome turns on how generally the type of harm is described.

### Thin Skull Rule
**Rule: Eggshell plaintiff.** If the type of harm was foreseeable, the defendant is liable for its full extent, even if a latent condition made it much worse than expected. "The defendant takes his victim as he finds him."

### Same Forces, Same General Harm
**Rule: Same forces, same general harm.** When damages result from the same physical forces whose existence required more care, and are of the same general sort that was expectable, the unforeseeability of the exact sequence and the extent of the loss doesn't limit liability. But liability ends where the link becomes "too tenuous," a matter of fair judgment.
- **Very duty rule:** when the actor is negligent precisely for failing to guard against a third party's act or a force of nature, that act doesn't cut off liability.

**Case: Petition of Kinsman Transit Co.** (2d Cir. 1964)
- **Facts:** A negligently moored ship broke loose on the ice-filled Buffalo River at night and hit a second ship, which also broke free. The city failed to raise a drawbridge in time, despite warnings and a federal regulation. The ships crashed into the bridge and formed a **dam** of ice and debris, which **flooded** property upstream.
- **Issue:** Are the shipowners and the city liable for flood damage from this unforeseeable chain of events?
- **Holding:** Yes. The claimants were within the "area of hazard" (Palsgraf), and the damage came from the same forces (current, ice, a drifting ship) that made the conduct negligent and was of the same general sort expected. (In Kinsman II, purely economic losses to businesses that couldn't move grain because of the blocked river were too tenuous.)
- **Dissent:** Foreseeability was applied with hindsight; the flooding was not foreseeable.
- **Takeaway:** Liability extends to harm from the same forces and of the same general sort as the risk, even if the sequence and extent were unforeseeable. It ends where the connection becomes "too tenuous."

### Real but Remote Risks
**Rule: Real but remote risks.** A risk is reasonably foreseeable if a reasonable person would see it as real, not far-fetched, even if remote, especially if it could be eliminated at no cost or disadvantage. Foreseeability also limits damages in nuisance.

**Case: Overseas Tankship (U.K.) Ltd. v. Miller Steamship Co. (The Wagon Mound No. 2)** (P.C. 1966)
- **Facts:** The same spill and fire as Wagon Mound No. 1. Here the plaintiffs owned ships being repaired at the wharf. The trial court found that a reasonable ship's engineer would know fire was possible, though only in very exceptional circumstances.
- **Issue:** Is damage from a real but remote risk reasonably foreseeable?
- **Holding:** Yes. A real risk that a reasonable chief engineer wouldn't brush aside is foreseeable, even if remote, when stopping the spill cost nothing (it was illegal and wasted oil). The defendant was liable in negligence.
- **Takeaway:** A small risk counts as foreseeable if a reasonable person would see it as real rather than far-fetched, and D must avoid it if doing so costs little. Foreseeability here merges with Hand-formula reasoning.

### Intervening Causes (CB 694–720)
**Rule: Intervening and superseding causes.** An intervening act (by a third party or nature) supersedes the defendant's negligence, cutting off liability, only if it was unforeseeable. A foreseeable intervening act doesn't break the chain.
- **Restatement (Third) § 34:** when an intervening act or force of nature is also a cause, liability is still limited to harms within the **risks that made the conduct tortious**. Acts that are **unforeseeable, unusual, or highly culpable** may fall outside.
- **Intervening negligence:** foreseeable types of negligence by others usually don't supersede. **Intentional or criminal** acts supersede unless the defendant's negligence created the very risk of that crime (Restatement (Second) § 442B).
- **Rescuers:** "Danger invites rescue." A tortfeasor is liable to rescuers (unless barred by the firefighter's rule) and for **enhanced harm from medical treatment** or other reasonable aid (§ 35).
- **Suicide:** the original tortfeasor is liable if the injury caused a mental disturbance that led to the suicide, not if it was a free and deliberate choice.
- **Acts of God:** foreseeable natural events don't cut off liability.

### Efficient Intervening Cause
**Rule: Efficient intervening cause (Nebraska).** Proximate cause requires (1) but-for causation, (2) that the injury was a natural and probable result of the negligence, and (3) no efficient intervening cause. An efficient intervening cause is new, independent conduct of a third person that the defendant could not have anticipated and that breaks the causal connection.

**Case: Latzel v. Bartek** (Neb. 2014)
- **Facts:** Two pickups collided at an unmarked rural intersection. The landowners had planted 7-foot-tall corn right up to the roadside, blocking drivers' view. A passenger was killed. Both drivers said they could have seen with a setback.
- **Issue:** Was the drivers' negligence in entering a blind intersection an efficient intervening cause as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** Yes. Landowners aren't bound to anticipate that drivers will disregard the obvious danger of a blind intersection. Summary judgment for the landowners was affirmed. The court declined to apply Restatement (Third) § 34.
- **Takeaway:** Under the traditional test, a third party's new, independent, unforeseeable act is an efficient intervening cause that cuts off D's liability, and the court can decide it on summary judgment. The Restatement (Third) would leave foreseeability to the jury.

### Foreseeable Criminal Acts
**Rule: Foreseeable intervening acts.** A third person's act that contributes to the harm doesn't excuse the original wrongdoer if it ought to have been foreseen. If the negligence and the third party's act are effectively concurrent, it's a joint tort rather than an intervening cause.

**Case: Brauer v. New York Central & Hudson River R.R.** (N.J. 1918)
- **Facts:** The railroad's train negligently hit the plaintiff's wagon at a crossing, stunning the driver and scattering the cargo (barrels, cider, a blanket). Bystanders stole the cargo. The railroad's own detectives on the train guarded its cargo but did nothing for the plaintiff's.
- **Issue:** Is the railroad liable for the stolen cargo, or was the theft a superseding cause?
- **Holding:** Liable. Theft was a natural and probable result of disabling the driver and scattering goods. The railroad's own guards show theft was foreseeable.
- **Takeaway:** A third party's crime doesn't break the causal chain if it was foreseeable, especially when D's negligence created the opportunity for that kind of crime.

### Contributory Negligence
**Rule: Contributory negligence (total bar).** A plaintiff whose own failure to use ordinary care contributed to the injury cannot recover anything, even if the defendant was also at fault.
- Contributory negligence is an **affirmative defense**; the defendant bears the burden.
- The plaintiff is held to the **same objective standard** as the defendant.
- The plaintiff's statutory violation can be contributory negligence per se.
- **No bar** if the defendant's conduct was **intentional, willful, or wanton** (sometimes gross negligence).
- **Last clear chance:** a plaintiff could still recover if the defendant had the last clear opportunity to avoid the accident.

**Case: Butterfield v. Forrester** (K.B. 1809)
- **Facts:** Forrester put a pole across part of a road while repairing his house. At dusk, Butterfield, riding very hard, hit the pole and was thrown. A witness said he could have seen it from 100 yards if he hadn't been riding so fast.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff recover for an obstruction negligently placed by the defendant if he failed to use ordinary care himself?
- **Holding:** No. Recovery requires both the defendant's fault **and** no lack of ordinary care by the plaintiff. Verdict for the defendant.
- **Takeaway:** Under contributory negligence, any negligence by P bars recovery entirely. Last clear chance developed as a way around the bar, and only a handful of jurisdictions still follow the rule.

# UNIT V — Plaintiff's Conduct, Apportionment & Multiple Parties (Week 10)

## Week 10 (CB 725–822) — Comparative Negligence; Assumption of Risk; Mitigation; Vicarious Liability; Apportionment; Animals

### Pure Comparative Negligence
**Rule: Pure comparative negligence.** The plaintiff's negligence does not bar recovery; it reduces damages by the plaintiff's percentage of fault, however large (a plaintiff 90% at fault recovers 10%).
- **Last clear chance** is abolished (no longer needed).
- **Set-off:** when both parties have claims, one judgment is entered for the difference.
- **Procedure:** the jury finds total damages and each party's percentage of fault by special verdict; the court computes the award.

**Case: Hoffman v. Jones** (Fla. 1973)
- **Facts:** A Florida negligence case in which the plaintiff's contributory negligence was at issue. Florida had followed the total bar since 1886, and the legislature had failed to change it.
- **Issue:** Should the court replace contributory negligence with comparative negligence, and which form?
- **Holding:** Yes. Contributory negligence is no longer a total bar. The court adopted **pure** comparative negligence, apportioning damages by relative fault.
- **Policy:** The total bar is unjust: it puts the whole loss on a plaintiff who may be only slightly at fault. Courts can change judge-made rules when conditions change.
- **Takeaway:** In a pure comparative negligence state, P's damages are reduced by P's percentage of fault, and P recovers even if P was more at fault than D.

### Modified Comparative Negligence
**Rule: Modified comparative negligence.** The plaintiff recovers reduced damages only if their fault is below a threshold:
- "Less than" (49%) form (Bradley): recovery is barred if the plaintiff's fault equals or exceeds the combined fault of the other parties.
- "Not greater than" (50%) form (Wisconsin): recovery is barred only if the plaintiff's fault is greater than the defendant's.
- Most states use some modified form. A plaintiff's fault is compared against the combined fault of all defendants in Bradley; some states compare against each defendant separately.
- Restatement (Third) Apportionment § 8: shares of responsibility depend on the nature of each party's conduct (awareness, intent, how unreasonable) and the strength of the causal connection.
- Comparative negligence is not a defense to intentional torts. Jurisdictions split on comparing a plaintiff's negligence with a defendant's reckless conduct.

**Case: Bradley v. Appalachian Power Co.** (W. Va. 1979)
- **Facts:** Two consolidated cases asking West Virginia to soften its contributory negligence rule.
- **Issue:** Should West Virginia adopt comparative negligence, and in what form?
- **Holding:** Yes, a **modified** form. A plaintiff recovers as long as their fault doesn't equal or exceed the combined fault of the other parties, with damages reduced by their percentage. Pure comparative fault was rejected.
- **Takeaway:** In modified comparative negligence, P recovers reduced damages only if P's fault stays below the threshold: "not as great as" D's (P must be under 50%) or "not greater than" D's (P can be at 50%).

### Express Assumption of Risk
**Rule: Exculpatory agreements.** A pre-injury release of liability for negligence is generally enforceable if it's clear, conspicuous, and made knowingly and willingly. It's void if:
- there's an obvious disparity in bargaining power;
- the transaction affects the public interest (the Tunkl factors: a business suitable for public regulation; an essential service; held out to the public; superior bargaining power; a standardized adhesion contract; the plaintiff placed under the defendant's control);
- it purports to cover gross negligence, recklessness, or intentional conduct; or
- it would waive a safety statute.
- Express assumption of risk remains a complete defense under comparative fault. States split on whether a parent's release binds a minor.

**Case: LaFrenz v. Lake County Fair Board** (Ind. Ct. App. 1977)
- **Facts:** Before entering the restricted pit area of a demolition derby, LaFrenz signed a release covering injury or death "whether caused by the negligence of Releasees or otherwise." "THIS IS A RELEASE" was printed in bold on every signature line. A derby car jumped the barrier and killed her.
- **Issue:** Is the release enforceable to bar the wrongful death claim?
- **Holding:** Yes, as a matter of law. No public policy violation, no unequal bargaining power, not a public-interest activity, and the conspicuous form showed she signed knowingly and willingly. Summary judgment for the defendants was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** An express release bars a negligence claim if it's clear and unambiguous and doesn't violate public policy (e.g., essential services or unequal bargaining power). Releases usually don't cover gross negligence or willful conduct.

### Implied Assumption of Risk — Categories (CB 760–75)
**Rule: Traditional elements.** The plaintiff (1) knew of the risk, (2) appreciated its danger, and (3) voluntarily chose to encounter it. The test is subjective, but a court may find knowledge as a matter of law when the risk is obvious to anyone.
- **Primary implied:** the defendant owes **no duty** (or a limited duty) because the risk is **inherent** in the activity (spectator sports, recreation). This survives comparative fault as a no-duty rule.
- **Secondary implied:** the plaintiff knowingly encounters a risk created by the defendant's **negligence**. Most comparative-fault states **merge** it into comparative fault.
- **Co-participants in sports:** liable only for **intentional or reckless** conduct totally outside the range of ordinary activity in the sport (the California *Knight* approach, followed by many states).

### Limited Duty for Inherent Risks
**Rule: Limited duty for inherent risks.** An amusement operator owes no duty to protect patrons from risks that are common, frequent, and expected in the activity itself (batted balls into the stands), but owes ordinary care for risks not inherent in the activity, like features of the particular facility.
- In inherent-risk cases, a plaintiff can get to the jury only with evidence that the facility **deviated from established custom** (e.g., screening behind home plate).

**Case: Jones v. Three Rivers Management Corp.** (Pa. 1978)
- **Facts:** Jones, standing in an interior concourse of a new stadium, looked out through an opening onto the field during batting practice. A batted ball hit her in the eye.
- **Issue:** Does the no-duty rule for batted balls bar recovery by a spectator hit while using an interior concourse?
- **Holding:** No. The openings in the concourse were a design feature of this stadium, not an inherent part of baseball, so ordinary negligence rules applied. The verdict for Jones stood.
- **Takeaway:** Primary assumption of risk means no duty for risks inherent in the activity, regardless of P's knowledge. D still owes ordinary care for risks that aren't inherent, like the features of a particular facility.

### Secondary Assumption of Risk
**Rule: Secondary assumption of risk.** A plaintiff who knowingly and voluntarily encounters an obvious risk created by the defendant's conduct assumes that risk.

**Case: Herod v. Grant** (Miss. 1972)
- **Facts:** At night, Grant sat on a toolbox in the bed of Herod's pickup while they hunted deer in a plowed field. Herod chased a deer at 15–20 mph, sped up and turned, and Grant was thrown out and seriously injured.
- **Issue:** Did Grant assume the risk of being thrown as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** Yes. A mature adult knew the truck might cross rough ground or swerve and accelerate in the excitement of the chase. A directed verdict for Herod should have been granted.
- **Takeaway:** Secondary assumption of risk applies when D breached a duty but P knowingly and voluntarily encountered the risk. Most comparative fault states fold it into comparative fault instead of treating it as a complete bar.

### Abolishing Implied Assumption of Risk
**Rule: Abolishing implied assumption of risk (Nevada, minority).** All forms of implied assumption of risk, including primary, are abolished and folded into comparative negligence. Recreational injury cases are decided under ordinary negligence: did the defendant act reasonably, considering the nature of the activity?

**Case: Auckenthaler v. Grundmeyer** (Nev. 1994)
- **Facts:** During a recreational trail ride to watch dog training, White rode Grundmeyer's nervous, recently gelded horse, which was threatening to kick. When Auckenthaler's horse came close, the horse kicked her leg.
- **Issue:** Do recreational participants owe each other ordinary care, or only a duty to avoid reckless or intentional harm?
- **Holding:** Ordinary care. Nevada had abolished implied assumption of risk, so the California reckless-only standard didn't apply. Summary judgment was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Implied assumption of risk comes in two forms, primary and secondary, distinct from express assumption. Abolishing it moves the decision from the judge (no duty) to the jury (breach and comparative fault).

### Avoidable Consequences; Mitigation (CB 776–80)
- **Avoidable consequences / seat belts:** with comparative fault, courts increasingly let juries reduce damages when the plaintiff's **pre-accident** conduct (no seat belt or helmet) increased the injuries. Some statutes exclude the evidence or cap the reduction.
- **Mitigation:** after injury, the plaintiff must act **reasonably** to minimize damages (getting treatment, following medical advice). The Restatement (Third) folds unreasonable failure to mitigate into comparative responsibility.
- A plaintiff's **egregious** conduct can be a superseding cause.

### Respondeat Superior
**Rule: Respondeat superior.** An employer is vicariously liable for an employee's negligence within the scope of employment. Under Restatement (Second) of Agency § 228, conduct is within scope if (a) it's the kind of work the employee is hired to do, (b) it occurs substantially within authorized time and space, and (c) it's motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer.
- **Enterprise benefit test (Hawaii):** whether the conduct was **related to the employment enterprise** or benefited the employer (even indirectly, like morale), and whether the risk is incident to the enterprise.
- **Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07:** outside scope if part of an **independent course of conduct** not meant to serve the employer.
- **Frolic vs. detour:** the employer isn't liable for a **frolic** of the employee's own, but is for a minor **detour**.
- **Intentional torts:** jurisdictions split; liability is more likely when the tort arose out of or was made possible by the job.
- **Indemnity:** a merely vicariously liable employer may recover the full amount from the employee.

**Case: Wong-Leong v. Hawaiian Independent Refinery, Inc.** (Haw. 1994)
- **Facts:** Rellamas, a refinery employee, paid for beer at an on-premises party celebrating his promotion (part of a company tradition, with drinking tolerated on site). He drove home drunk and crashed, killing three people and himself.
- **Issue:** Can the employer be vicariously liable for a crash that happened off duty?
- **Holding:** Yes, potentially. The negligent act was **drinking while knowing he had to drive**, and that act may have occurred within the scope of employment, even though the crash didn't. Fact issues precluded summary judgment.
- **Takeaway:** An employer is vicariously liable for torts within the scope of employment. Scope is measured at the time of the negligent act, not the moment of injury.

### Apparent Agency
**Rule: Independent contractors.** A principal is generally not liable for an independent contractor's negligence. Employee vs. contractor status turns on the right to control the manner and means of the work.

**Rule: Apparent agency (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 2.03).** A principal is liable for a contractor's negligence if (1) the principal held the actor out as its agent, and (2) the plaintiff accepted the services in the reasonable belief that the principal (or its employees) was providing them. Reliance need not be proven.
- **Nondelegable duties:** a hirer is liable for a contractor's negligence in **inherently dangerous** activities (blasting) and for duties to keep land or chattels safe.

**Case: Jones v. HealthSouth Treasure Valley Hospital** (Idaho 2009)
- **Facts:** Jones had spine surgery at the hospital. A blood-salvage technician, an independent contractor wearing the same scrubs as hospital staff, allegedly made an error that caused her harm. The hospital's consent forms didn't disclose that the technicians were contractors.
- **Issue:** Can the hospital be vicariously liable under apparent agency for an independent contractor's support staff?
- **Holding:** Yes. Apparent agency applies to tort claims and extends beyond physicians to support personnel the hospital assigns. The plaintiff needs only a reasonable belief traceable to the hospital, not reliance. Summary judgment was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Hospitals generally aren't liable for independent contractor doctors, except under apparent agency: the hospital held the doctor out as its own, and P reasonably relied on that.

### Other Vicarious Liability; Imputed Contributory Negligence (CB 793–800)
- **Parents** aren't vicariously liable for their children's torts by status alone (only for their own negligent supervision).
- **Joint enterprise:** members are vicariously liable for each other's torts in furtherance of the enterprise. Requires an agreement, a common purpose, a **community of pecuniary interest**, and an **equal right of control**.
- **Car owners:** liable for permissive users by statute in some states, or under the **family purpose doctrine**. The federal Graves Amendment shields rental and leasing companies.
- **Imputed contributory negligence (both-ways rule):** negligence that would be imputed to a party as a defendant is also imputed to them as a plaintiff. The Restatement (Third) § 5 excepts car accident cases.

### Joint and Several Liability
**Rule: Joint and several liability.** When independent tortfeasors cause an indivisible injury, the plaintiff may collect the entire judgment from any one of them. That defendant bears the risk that a co-defendant can't pay, subject to contribution.
- **Contribution:** a defendant who pays more than its share may recover the excess from the others, now usually by percentage of fault.
- **Settlements:** a non-settling defendant gets a credit either for the **dollar amount** (pro tanto) or for the **settling party's percentage** of fault (Restatement (Third) Apportionment § 16). A good-faith settlement cuts off contribution claims against the settler.
- **Reforms:** (1) pure **several** liability (each pays only its share; the plaintiff bears insolvency risk); (2) several liability only when the plaintiff is partly at fault; (3) **threshold** tests (joint liability only above a set percentage, e.g., Texas more than 50%); (4) joint liability only for **economic** damages; (5) **reallocation** of uncollectible shares among all parties, including the plaintiff.
- **Indivisible injury:** defendants bear the burden to prove the harm can be divided.

**Case: Walt Disney World Co. v. Wood** (Fla. 1987)
- **Facts:** Aloysia Wood was hurt on Disney's grand prix ride when her fiancé (later husband) rammed her car from behind. The jury found her 14% at fault, her husband 85%, and Disney 1%. Her husband's share was uncollectible because of spousal immunity, so under joint and several liability Disney would pay 86% of the damages.
- **Issue:** Should Florida replace joint and several liability with liability based on each defendant's percentage of fault?
- **Holding:** No. The doctrine isn't clearly unjust and doesn't automatically fall with comparative negligence; the change is for the legislature (which later largely did it).
- **Takeaway:** Under joint and several liability, P can collect the whole judgment from any defendant, even one barely at fault. The main reforms are several liability only, fault thresholds, and joint liability limited to economic damages.

### Strict Liability for Animals
**Rule: Domestic animals (Restatement (Second) § 509).** A possessor of a domestic animal known (or with reason to be known) to have dangerous propensities abnormal to its class is strictly liable for harm resulting from that propensity (the "one bite" rule). Otherwise, liability requires negligence (§ 518), which includes taking reasonable steps against the animal's normal dangers.
- **Wild animals (Restatement (Third) § 22):** strict liability for harm from a species not generally domesticated and likely to cause injury unless restrained.
- **Exceptions:** no strict liability for animals kept under a public duty (zoos for some courts), or where the plaintiff came near the animal to get a benefit from it (e.g., a vet).
- **Defenses:** traditionally only assumption of risk; under the Restatement (Third), comparative responsibility applies.
- **Livestock trespass:** strict liability, modified by "fencing in" or "fencing out" statutes. **Dog bite statutes** often impose strict liability regardless of prior viciousness.

**Case: Duren v. Kunkel** (Mo. 1991)
- **Facts:** Kunkel bought an 1,800-pound bull cheaply because it "acted up" in the sale ring. A year later, he told his neighbor Duren, who was helping on the farm, to drive the bull out of the corral alone, past fresh blood from castrating calves. The bull attacked Duren.
- **Issue:** Is the owner strictly liable without knowing of a dangerous propensity abnormal to bulls? If not, is he negligent?
- **Holding:** No strict liability, since there was no evidence the bull was more dangerous than other bulls of its class. But an owner who knows bulls' **normal** dangers must take reasonable precautions, and a negligence case was made. Remanded for a new trial on negligence.
- **Takeaway:** Owners of wild animals, or of domestic animals with known dangerous tendencies, are strictly liable. Otherwise, P must prove negligence.

# UNIT VI — Strict Liability & Products Liability (Weeks 11–13)

## Week 11 (CB 822–905) — Abnormally Dangerous Activities; Coase; Origins of Products Liability

### Defnition
**Strict Liability:** Liability without proof of intent or fault, also called absolute liability, though causation must still be shown

### Origins
**Rule: Rylands strict liability (Exchequer Chamber).** A person who for his own purposes brings onto his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes must keep it in at his peril, and is prima facie liable for all damage that is the natural consequence of its escape.

**Rule: Non-natural use (House of Lords).** Liability attaches to a non-natural use of land (introducing something not naturally there). A natural use (water collecting naturally) isn't actionable.
- Modern English law treats Rylands as part of nuisance: an **escape** onto another's land, **foreseeable** damage, and an **extraordinary and unusual** use.
- Defenses suggested by Blackburn: the plaintiff's own default, or an act of God.

**Case: Rylands v. Fletcher** (Exch. Ch. 1866; H.L. 1868)
- **Facts:** The defendants hired competent contractors to build a reservoir on their land. Unknown to anyone, it sat over old mine shafts connected to the plaintiff's coal mine. When the reservoir filled, water broke through the shafts and flooded the mine. The defendants personally weren't negligent.
- **Issue:** Is a landowner who, without negligence, accumulates water that escapes and floods a neighbor's mine liable?
- **Holding:** Yes. The duty is absolute: the defendants brought the water onto their land for their own purposes (a non-natural use) and are liable for the damage its escape caused.
- **Takeaway:** One who brings onto land something likely to cause harm if it escapes (a non-natural use) is strictly liable for damage from its escape. This is the root of strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities.

### The American Rejection
**Rule: Fault requirement in American law.** In the 19th-century U.S. view, no one is liable for injuries to another's person or property without fault or negligence. Rylands conflicts with American law.

**Case: Losee v. Buchanan** (N.Y. 1873)
- **Facts:** A paper company's steam boiler exploded and was thrown onto the plaintiff's land, damaging his buildings. There was no proof of negligence.
- **Issue:** Is the owner of a lawfully kept steam boiler liable without fault when it explodes and damages a neighbor's property?
- **Holding:** No. Liability requires fault. People in civilized society accept the risks of their neighbors' lawful industrial activities in return for the benefits of industry (reciprocity).
- **Takeaway:** Some early American courts rejected Rylands and required fault, reflecting a policy of encouraging industrial development. Most states now impose strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities anyway.

### Restatement Tests for Abnormally Dangerous Activities
**Rule: Abnormally dangerous activities (Restatement (Second) § 519).** One who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is strictly liable for harm to persons, land, or chattels resulting from it, even with utmost care, but only for harm of the kind whose possibility makes the activity abnormally dangerous.

**Rule: Abnormally dangerous activity factors (§ 520).**
- (a) a high degree of risk of some harm;
- (b) the likelihood that resulting harm will be great;
- (c) inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care;
- (d) the extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage;
- (e) inappropriateness of the activity to the place where it's carried on; and
- (f) the extent to which its value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes.
- No factor is required or sufficient alone; usually several must be present. The question is for the court.

**Rule: Abnormally dangerous activities (Restatement (Third) § 20).** An activity is abnormally dangerous if (1) it creates a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised, and (2) it is not a matter of common usage.
- **Blasting** is the classic example. Others are mixed (storing or transporting gasoline, crop dusting, fireworks). Aircraft ground damage is strictly liable (§ 520A).
- **Common carriers** that must accept dangerous cargo aren't strictly liable (§ 521).
- **Defenses:** assumption of risk under the Second Restatement; comparative fault in many modern courts. Act of God is not a defense under the Restatements.
- **Rationales:** loss spreading, a defendant who profits from the risk should bear its costs, destruction of evidence makes negligence hard to prove, and **nonreciprocal risk** (Fletcher): the defendant imposes a risk greater than the community imposes on itself.
- **Statutes:** CERCLA imposes strict liability for hazardous-waste cleanup; the Price-Anderson Act caps liability for nuclear accidents.

### Fireworks
**Case: Klein v. Pyrodyne Corp.** (Wash. 1991)
- **Facts:** At a July 4th fireworks show run by Pyrodyne, a mortar tipped over and a shell flew 500 feet horizontally into the crowd, burning the Kleins. The evidence of what caused the malfunction was destroyed in the explosion.
- **Issue:** Is a public fireworks display an abnormally dangerous activity?
- **Holding:** Yes. Under § 520: the risk is high and the harm serious even with reasonable care (factors a–c); public displays are not a matter of common usage, since few people **set off** professional displays (d); and the location factor doesn't weigh against it. Alternatively, a Washington statute imposed strict liability. Pyrodyne was strictly liable.
- **Concurrence:** strict liability should rest on the statute, not § 520.
- **Takeaway:** The § 520 factors are weighed together; no single factor decides, and several usually must be present. Fireworks displays are abnormally dangerous, and "common usage" means how many people do the activity, not how many watch it.

### The Economic View
**Rule: Economic view of strict liability.** Strict liability is appropriate when due care can't prevent the accident, so that the only way to reduce accidents is to change the location or reduce the level of the activity. If the accident could have been prevented by reasonable care, negligence is adequate. Abnormal danger is a property of activities, not substances.

**Case: Indiana Harbor Belt R.R. Co. v. American Cyanamid Co.** (7th Cir. 1990)
- **Facts:** Cyanamid shipped 20,000 gallons of acrylonitrile, a toxic and flammable chemical, in a leased rail tank car. At a rail yard near Chicago, the car leaked because of a broken lid. Nearby homes were evacuated. The railroad paid for cleanup and sued Cyanamid, the shipper.
- **Issue:** Is a shipper of a hazardous chemical strictly liable for a spill in transit through a metropolitan area?
- **Holding:** No. The leak was caused by carelessness (a broken lid) that ordinary care could prevent, so negligence suffices. Rerouting rail traffic around cities isn't feasible, and the shipper isn't the one transporting. The claim was sent back for trial on negligence.
- **Takeaway:** Strict liability fits only where due care can't prevent the accident. If ordinary care would have prevented it, negligence is enough, so factor (c) and the location and level of the activity carry the most weight.

### Harm Within the Risk
**Rule: Harm within the risk.** Strict liability is limited to harm within the extraordinary risk that makes the activity abnormally dangerous (Restatement (Second) § 519(2); (Third) §§ 24, 29). There's no strict liability for harm that would not have happened but for the abnormally sensitive character of the plaintiff's activity (§ 524A).

**Case: Foster v. Preston Mill Co.** (Wash. 1954)
- **Facts:** The defendant blasted for a logging road more than two miles from Foster's mink ranch. The noise and vibration frightened mother mink during whelping, causing them to kill their young.
- **Issue:** Is the blaster strictly liable for the mink losses?
- **Holding:** No. The risk that makes blasting ultrahazardous is flying debris and heavy concussion, not minor vibration upsetting nervous mink. The harm was outside that risk. Judgment for Foster was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Strict liability covers only harm within the risk that makes the activity abnormally dangerous. It doesn't cover harm caused by the abnormal sensitivity of P's own activity.

### The Coase Theorem
**Rule: Coase Theorem.** When transaction costs are low, the liability rule doesn't affect the efficient allocation of resources, because the parties will bargain to the efficient result regardless of who bears liability. The liability rule affects only who pays (distribution). When transaction costs are high, the legal rule matters, so courts should put liability on the party who can avoid the harm most cheaply.
- Example (Foster): if blasting is worth more than the mink, the blaster continues either way; liability only determines whether the blaster pays the rancher, or the rancher pays the blaster to stop.

### Ending Privity in Negligence
**Rule: Manufacturer's duty without privity.** A manufacturer of a product that is reasonably certain to endanger life and limb if negligently made (a "thing of danger"), which it knows will be used by people other than the buyer without new tests, owes those people a duty of care regardless of contract. A manufacturer must inspect components it buys from others.
- Before MacPherson, under **Winterbottom v. Wright**, a negligence duty ran only to those in **privity** of contract, with narrow exceptions for "imminently dangerous" items.

**Case: MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.** (N.Y. 1916)
- **Facts:** Buick sold a car to a dealer, who resold it to MacPherson. A wooden wheel, made by another company, collapsed because of defective spokes, and MacPherson was thrown out and injured. Reasonable inspection would have revealed the defect.
- **Issue:** Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to someone other than its immediate buyer?
- **Holding:** Yes. A car is a thing of danger when negligently made, and Buick knew it would be used by people other than the dealer. Buick owed MacPherson a duty, including a duty to inspect the wheel.
- **Takeaway:** A manufacturer of a product that is dangerous if negligently made owes a duty of care to all foreseeable users, regardless of privity. Privity is no longer a defense to a negligence claim.

### Breach of Warranty (CB 878–90)
- **Express warranty (UCC § 2-313):** any affirmation of fact, promise, description, or sample that's part of the basis of the bargain.
- **Implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314):** goods sold by a merchant must be fit for their **ordinary purpose**.
- **Fitness for a particular purpose (§ 2-315):** when the seller knows the buyer's particular purpose and that the buyer is relying on the seller's judgment.
- **Disclaimers (§ 2-316):** must mention merchantability and be conspicuous; "as is" excludes implied warranties.
- **Third-party beneficiaries (§ 2-318):** Alternative A extends warranties to the buyer's family, household, and guests.
- **Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors:** implied warranties run to foreseeable users despite lack of privity, and disclaimers in standard adhesion contracts are void.
- **Problems with warranty:** notice requirements, privity, disclaimers. These pushed courts toward tort.
- **Economic loss rule:** strict products liability covers personal injury and damage to **other** property, not damage to the product itself or purely economic loss (warranty governs those).

### Strict Products Liability
**Rule: Strict products liability.** A manufacturer is strictly liable in tort when a product it places on the market, knowing it will be used without inspection for defects, proves to have a defect that causes injury to a human being. The plaintiff need not satisfy warranty rules (notice, privity, reliance) and need not prove negligence.

**Rule: Strict products liability (Restatement (Second) § 402A).** One who sells a product in a defective condition unreasonably dangerous to the user or consumer is liable for physical harm caused, if (a) the seller is engaged in the business of selling such products, and (b) the product reaches the user without substantial change. This applies even if the seller used all possible care and had no contract with the user.
- **Who is liable:** everyone in the **chain of distribution** (manufacturers, wholesalers, retailers). Middlemen can seek indemnity from the manufacturer. Courts extended protection to foreseeable **bystanders**.
- **Comment g:** the defect must exist when the product leaves the seller's hands.
- **Comment i (consumer expectations):** the product must be dangerous beyond what the **ordinary consumer** would expect.
- California rejects the "unreasonably dangerous" element as sounding in negligence (Cronin).
- **Three types of defect:** manufacturing, design, warning (Week 12).

**Case: Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.** (Cal. 1963)
- **Facts:** Greenman's wife bought him a Shopsmith combination power tool, relying on its brochure. While he was using it as a lathe, a piece of wood flew out and hit his head, seriously injuring him. Experts testified the set screws were inadequate to hold the wood. He gave notice of breach of warranty 10 months later.
- **Issue:** Does failure to give timely notice of a warranty breach bar recovery, or is the manufacturer strictly liable in tort?
- **Holding:** The notice rule didn't apply between a consumer and a remote manufacturer, and in any event the manufacturer was **strictly liable in tort**. The verdict for Greenman was affirmed.
- **Policy:** The costs of injuries from defective products should be borne by the **manufacturers** who put them on the market (they can spread the cost), not by injured people who are powerless to protect themselves.
- **Takeaway:** A manufacturer is strictly liable in tort when a defective product causes injury. P need not prove negligence or satisfy warranty rules (notice, privity, reliance).

## Week 12 (CB 905–995) — Defect Today: Manufacturing, Design, and Warning Defects; Who Is Covered; Defenses

### The Concept of Defect Today (CB 905–07)
**Rule: Products liability (Restatement (Third) Products § 1).** One in the business of selling or distributing products who sells a defective product is liable for harm to persons or property caused by the defect.

**Rule: Three types of defect (§ 2).** A product is defective if, at the time of sale or distribution, it has:
- a manufacturing defect: it departs from its intended design, even if all possible care was used (true strict liability);
- a design defect: the foreseeable risks could have been reduced or avoided by a reasonable alternative design, and leaving it out makes the product not reasonably safe; or
- a warning defect: the foreseeable risks could have been reduced or avoided by reasonable instructions or warnings, and leaving them out makes the product not reasonably safe.
- Common elements (any theory): the plaintiff (1) was injured by the product, (2) the product was in essentially the same condition as when it left the defendant, and (3) the injury occurred because the product was defective. There's no separate "failure to test" claim.
- Food (§ 7): an ingredient is a defect if a reasonable consumer wouldn't expect it in the food.

### Manufacturing Defects and Circumstantial Proof
**Rule: Malfunction doctrine (§ 3).** A defect may be inferred without proof of the specific flaw when the incident (a) is of a kind that ordinarily occurs as a result of a product defect, and (b) was not solely the result of other causes existing at the time of sale. It works like res ipsa loquitur, which creates a permissible inference, not a presumption.
- **Factors for inferring a defect:** expert testimony on possible causes; time between sale and accident (strongest for new products); whether similar accidents happened; whether other causes are eliminated; whether the accident is the kind that doesn't happen without a defect.
- Expert testimony isn't needed when lay jurors can see the accident rarely happens without a defect, but it **is** needed when they can't. Experts must satisfy **FRE 702** (sufficient facts, reliable methods, reliably applied).
- **Spoliation:** destroying the product can lead to an adverse inference instruction, a separate claim, or other remedies.

**Case: Smoot v. Mazda Motors of America, Inc.** (7th Cir. 2006)
- **Facts:** Mrs. Smoot's one-year-old Mazda hit a large pothole (or a chunk of asphalt) at 35–40 mph. The airbags deployed and injured her. The day before, Mazda had sent a recall notice about a risk of airbag deployment in minor impacts. The windshield and front wheel were damaged, suggesting a hard jolt. The car, including the airbag control unit, was repaired and sold before suit.
- **Issue:** Could the plaintiffs reach a jury on res ipsa loquitur without admissible expert testimony?
- **Holding:** No. Res ipsa can apply in products cases and sometimes needs no expert, but airbags deploy based on the rate of **deceleration**, and lay jurors can't say a pothole impact couldn't properly trigger one. The plaintiffs' expert was correctly excluded under Rule 702, and the recall notice alone wasn't enough. Dismissal was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** For a manufacturing defect, P must show the defect existed when the product left D's hands. The malfunction inference works only if other plausible causes are ruled out, so the plaintiff must preserve the evidence.

### Design Defect Tests
**Rule: Two tests for design defect.**
- Consumer expectations: the product is unreasonably dangerous to the ordinary consumer given the conditions that foreseeably attend its use.
- Risk-utility: the product's risks outweigh its benefits, shown by a feasible (reasonable) alternative design that would have reduced the risk without unduly impairing utility or costing too much. Risk-utility is essentially the Hand formula applied to products.
- Branham (S.C.): risk-utility with a feasible alternative design is the exclusive test in design cases. Consumer expectations still fits manufacturing defects. Many states (e.g., California's Barker two-prong test) let the plaintiff use either test, with the burden on the defendant on risk-utility.
- A product isn't defective merely because it could be made safer.
- Timing: design is judged by what was known or reasonably attainable at the time of manufacture (foresight), so post-manufacture evidence is generally inadmissible. (Some courts impute later-discovered knowledge, e.g., Keeton's test in Phillips.)
- Other incidents are admissible only if substantially similar.
- Foreseeable careless use (like careless driving) must be part of the design evaluation.
- Obvious dangers don't bar recovery as a matter of law; they're a factor in the risk-utility balance (Campo overruled by Micallef).
- Crashworthiness: car makers are liable for design defects that enhance injuries in foreseeable accidents, even if they didn't cause the crash (§ 16 apportions divisible enhanced injuries).
- Product category liability (O'Brien: above-ground pools too dangerous even without an alternative design) has been rejected by reform statutes requiring a feasible alternative design.
- Submitting factually identical risk-utility claims under several labels (strict liability, negligence, warranty) risks confusing the jury.

**Case: Branham v. Ford Motor Co.** (S.C. 2010)
- **Facts:** The driver of a 1987 Ford Bronco II looked back at the children, drifted off the road, overcorrected, and rolled over. Branham, unbelted in the back seat, was ejected and injured. He claimed a defective seatbelt sleeve (on a failure-to-test theory) and a handling and stability design defect. A former Ford vice president testified that engineers favored a MacPherson suspension and opposed the Twin I-Beam suspension because it raised the center of gravity, but management chose the Twin I-Beam.
- **Issue:** What is the proper test for design defects, was there enough evidence of an alternative design, and did trial errors require a new trial?
- **Holding:**
  - The seatbelt negligence claim failed along with the strict liability claim, since no defect was shown.
  - The MacPherson suspension was enough evidence of a feasible alternative design to send the handling claim to the jury.
  - Risk-utility with a feasible alternative design is the **exclusive** design test.
  - But post-distribution evidence, an inflammatory closing argument, and requiring the jury to apportion fault between joint tortfeasors were errors. The verdict was **reversed and remanded** for a new trial.
- **Takeaway:** Design defect is measured by one of two tests: consumer expectations (§ 402A) and risk-utility (Restatement (Third), which requires a reasonable alternative design). Defect is judged at the time of sale, not with hindsight.

### Risk-Utility Applied
**Rule: Risk-utility factors (Wade factors).** (1) usefulness of the product; (2) likelihood and seriousness of injury; (3) availability of a safer substitute; (4) ability to eliminate the danger without impairing usefulness or making it too expensive; (5) the user's ability to avoid the danger; (6) users' awareness of the danger; (7) feasibility of spreading the loss through pricing or insurance. In Pennsylvania (at the time), the judge decided "initially and as a matter of law" whether the product was unreasonably dangerous.

**Case: Riley v. Becton Dickinson Vascular Access, Inc.** (E.D. Pa. 1995)
- **Facts:** Riley, a 23-year-old ICU nurse, contracted HIV from a stick by the exposed, blood-contaminated needle of an IV catheter. Retractable-sheath designs existed. The court calculated roughly 1–2 infections a year from this kind of stick, about 17 per billion catheters. The safer design cost much more, couldn't eliminate sticks, and created other problems (blood backflow, extensive training).
- **Issue:** Was the catheter "unreasonably dangerous" under Pennsylvania's risk-utility analysis?
- **Holding:** No. The catheter was "dangerous because it is capable of causing serious injury," but **not unreasonably dangerous**, so it wasn't defective as a matter of law. Summary judgment for the defendant.
- **Takeaway:** Under risk-utility, P must show a feasible alternative design that is safer overall and not too costly. An alternative that creates new risks or costs too much doesn't prove a defect.

### Prescription Drugs and Comment k (CB 940–47)
- **Comment k (§ 402A):** "unavoidably unsafe" products (some drugs and vaccines), properly made and accompanied by proper warnings, are **not defective**. Courts split: some apply it **case by case**, some give **blanket** protection to all prescription drugs (Brown v. Superior Court (Cal.); Grundberg (Utah)), and a few reject it. Most apply it to implanted medical devices.
- **Restatement (Third) Products § 6(c):** a drug or device is defectively designed only if its risks are so great relative to its benefits that reasonable health-care providers wouldn't prescribe it for **any** class of patients. It has gained little acceptance.

### Adequacy of Warnings
**Rule: Adequate warning (Pavlides).** A warning must (1) be designed to catch the attention of the consumer, (2) be comprehensible and give a fair indication of the specific risks, and (3) be of an intensity proportional to the magnitude of the risk.
- A warning can be **substantively** inadequate (doesn't convey the severity of the risk) or **procedurally** inadequate (not conspicuous enough to reach the user).
- **Warning of one risk doesn't satisfy the duty to warn of other material risks**, and failing altogether to warn of a material, hidden risk is a breach.
- A party is entitled to a **specific** jury instruction on its theory when it's supported by the evidence and properly requested; stock instructions aren't enough.

**Case: Lewis v. Sea Ray Boats, Inc.** (Nev. 2003)
- **Facts:** At Lake Mead, Gasse and Lewis slept in the cabin of a Sea Ray boat with the gasoline generator running the air conditioner. Carbon monoxide from the generator was blown back into the cabin by the wind. Unlike engine exhaust, it had no distinctive odor. Gasse died and Lewis was catastrophically injured. The warnings addressed **engine** exhaust, addressed CO generally, and only "inferentially" covered generator fumes. The jury, twice asking what "adequate warning" meant, returned a verdict for Sea Ray.
- **Issue:** Were the plaintiffs entitled to a specific instruction defining an adequate warning?
- **Holding:** Yes. The general "common sense" instruction was insufficient. Reversed and remanded for a new trial.
- **Takeaway:** A warning must address the specific risk. A warning about one risk (exhaust) doesn't cover a different hidden risk (odorless generator CO), so adequacy depends on the warning's content, specificity, and prominence.

### Obvious Dangers; Duty vs. Causation
**Rule: Open and obvious exception.** There's no duty to warn of dangers that are patently obvious to the class of foreseeable users. Whether the particular plaintiff already knew the danger goes to causation, not duty. Courts must keep these separate so one unusually knowledgeable plaintiff doesn't erase the duty for everyone else.
- **Read-and-heed presumption:** most jurisdictions presume the plaintiff would have read and heeded an adequate warning.
- Warnings can also inform users of **safer alternatives** (Liriano), so a duty may exist even for an obvious danger if a warning would tell users how to avoid it.
- **Employers:** workers' compensation bars suits against employers, which pushes injured workers to sue manufacturers.

**Case: Burke v. Spartanics Ltd.** (2d Cir. 2001) (Calabresi, J.)
- **Facts:** Burke reached behind a metal-shearing machine to clear cut metal, bracing his right hand on the cutting surface, as he had been trained to do. His employer had installed a ramp that made this necessary. His supervisor, not seeing him, made a cut and severed his fingers. Burke had used the machine for seven months and fully understood the danger. He argued there should have been a warning at the back of the machine.
- **Issue:** Did the jury instruction misstate New York law by letting Burke's own knowledge negate the duty to warn, and if so, was the error harmless?
- **Holding:**
  - The instruction that there is no duty to warn of **obvious, generally known** dangers was correct.
  - The instruction that **Burke's own knowledge** negated the duty was wrong; individual knowledge goes to causation.
  - The error was **harmless**: Burke's awareness (and the shop's routine practice) negated causation as a matter of law. Verdict for the defendant affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** There's no duty to warn of dangers obvious to users generally. P's own knowledge of the danger goes to causation: a warning wouldn't have changed P's behavior.

### State of the Art in Warning Cases
**Rule: State of the art in warning cases.** In warning cases, liability depends on what the manufacturer knew or should have known from information reasonably available when it distributed the product (Restatement (Second) § 402A cmt. j). It is not liable for unknowable dangers. The manufacturer is held to the knowledge of an expert in the field and must keep up with scientific advances. In strict liability warning cases, the defendant bears the burden of proving the danger wasn't reasonably knowable.
- Reliance on **FDA** approval doesn't excuse a warning the manufacturer knew or should have known was needed.
- **Post-sale duty to warn:** a manufacturer that later learns of a danger must take reasonable steps to warn those who already have the product (Restatement (Third) Products § 10).
- **Learned intermediary doctrine:** a drug maker's duty runs to the patient but is fulfilled by warning the **prescribing physician**.
- **Bulk supplier doctrine:** a supplier of raw materials that can't practically warn end users may rely on a sophisticated intermediary buyer to warn.
- The **National Childhood Vaccine Injury Compensation Act** provides no-fault compensation for vaccine injuries, with limits on pain and suffering.

**Case: Feldman v. Lederle Laboratories** (N.J. 1984)
- **Facts:** Carol Ann Feldman's father, a doctor and pharmacist, gave her the tetracycline Declomycin several times as a young child from 1960 to 1963. It turned her teeth gray. The drug reference guide didn't mention tooth discoloration until 1965–66. Experts disagreed about whether the earlier medical literature should have alerted Lederle.
- **Issue:** Is a drug maker strictly liable for failing to warn of a danger that may have been unknowable, and who has the burden on knowability?
- **Holding:** Liability turns on whether the danger was **reasonably knowable** at the time of distribution; the manufacturer isn't liable for truly unknowable dangers. **Beshada** (no state-of-the-art defense in asbestos cases) is limited to its facts. The **defendant** bears the burden of proving the information wasn't reasonably available. Judgment for Lederle was reversed and remanded for a new trial.
- **Takeaway:** Manufacturers must warn only of risks an expert in the field knew or could have known at the time of distribution. In strict liability warning cases, D bears the burden of proving the danger was unknowable.

### Defendants and Interests Covered (CB 975–85)
- **Component makers:** liable if the component itself was defective, or if they **substantially participated** in integrating it into the final product's defective design.
- **Successor corporations:** a merger passes on liability. After an **asset sale**, liability passes only if the buyer (1) agreed to assume it, (2) the deal is a **de facto merger**, (3) the buyer is a **mere continuation** of the seller, or (4) the deal was a **fraud** to escape liability. California's "**product line**" exception is rejected by most states.
- **Apparent manufacturers:** one who sells another's product **as its own** is liable as if it made it (§ 14).
- **Used goods sellers** generally aren't liable (they keep the used market cheap and can't influence design), unless they rebuild or modify the product.
- **Products vs. services:** commercial **lessors** may be liable; financers, repairers, installers, and service providers generally aren't. **Blood shield statutes** treat blood and tissue as a service, not a sale. Doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies are rarely strictly liable.
- **Intangibles:** products liability is limited to **tangible** personal property. Claims based on bad information in books generally fail, and electricity isn't a product until it reaches the customer.
- **Real estate:** not goods, but some courts recognize an **implied warranty of habitability** for new homes.

### Defenses; Plaintiff's Behavior (CB 985–95)
- **Comment n (§ 402A):** ordinary contributory negligence (failing to **discover** a defect or guard against it) is **not** a defense; assumption of risk (knowingly using a product despite a known defect) is.
- **Comparative fault** now applies to strict products liability in most states, usually by statute. The jury assigns percentages of responsibility to each party, whether based on negligence or a defective product. Restatement (Third) Products § 17 reduces recovery when the plaintiff's own conduct combined with the defect to cause the harm.
- Some states keep **assumption of risk** or **misuse** as complete defenses. Misuse is often treated not as a defense but as a question of whether the product was defective or the misuse was **foreseeable** (foreseeable misuse must be designed or warned against).

## Week 13 (CB 996–1058) — Pre-emption; Regulatory Compliance; Nuisance

### Pre-emption
**Rule: Types of pre-emption.** Federal law displaces state tort law through (1) express pre-emption (a statute's pre-emption clause), (2) field pre-emption (Congress occupied the field), or (3) implied conflict pre-emption (it's impossible to comply with both, or state law is an obstacle to federal objectives). Congressional intent is the touchstone.

**Rule: FIFRA § 136v(b).** A state may not impose labeling or packaging "requirements" "in addition to or different from" FIFRA's.
- "Requirements" include **common-law duties** enforced through damages, but only **rules of law that must be obeyed**, not verdicts that merely **induce** a manufacturer to change its label.
- Two steps: (1) is it a requirement **for labeling or packaging**; (2) is it **in addition to or different from** FIFRA's?
- **Parallel requirements:** a state labeling rule that is **equivalent to and fully consistent with** FIFRA's misbranding standard is **not** pre-empted.

**Case: Bates v. Dow Agrosciences LLC** (U.S. 2005)
- **Facts:** Dow's weed killer Strongarm's label said it was recommended "in all areas where peanuts are grown." It badly stunted peanut crops in the high-pH soils of West Texas, where 29 farmers used it. The EPA never reviewed the efficacy claim.
- **Issue:** Does FIFRA pre-empt the farmers' state-law claims for defective design, defective manufacture, negligent testing, breach of express warranty, fraud, and failure to warn?
- **Holding:** The design, manufacturing, testing, and express warranty claims are **not** labeling requirements and are not pre-empted. The fraud and failure-to-warn claims are labeling requirements, but they survive if they **parallel** FIFRA's misbranding standard. Remanded.
- **Takeaway:** Federal law expressly pre-empts state claims only when they impose requirements "in addition to or different from" federal ones. Claims based on parallel duties (design, manufacturing, express warranty) survive. Implied (impossibility) pre-emption is a separate doctrine.

### Compliance with Regulations (CB 1020–30)
- **Noncompliance** with a product safety statute makes the product **defective** as to the risks the statute addresses (Restatement (Third) Products § 4(a)), the products version of negligence per se.
- **Compliance** is **evidence** the product isn't defective, but in the overwhelming majority of states it's **not conclusive**; regulations are often only minimum standards. Some states create a **rebuttable presumption** of non-defect (e.g., Colorado).
- **Statutes of repose** bar product claims a fixed number of years after first sale, regardless of when the injury occurs.

### Products Liability in Other Countries (CB 1030–40)
- **EU Directive:** a product is defective when it doesn't provide the **safety a person is entitled to expect** (a consumer-expectations standard), with a **development risk** defense (the defect couldn't be discovered given the scientific knowledge at the time) and reduction for the injured person's fault. Brazil and Japan have similar strict liability codes.

### Private Nuisance
**Rule: Private nuisance.** A substantial interference with the use and enjoyment of land that causes unreasonable discomfort or annoyance to persons of ordinary sensibilities.
- "Nuisance" is the **legal injury**, not the cause of action or the conduct. The plaintiff must also prove a **basis of liability**: the defendant caused it **intentionally**, **negligently**, or (in limited cases) through an **abnormally dangerous activity**.
- **Intentional:** the defendant acts **for the purpose** of causing the interference or **knows** it is resulting or substantially certain to result (Restatement (Second) § 825). Continuing after notice makes it intentional.
- **Unreasonableness** focuses on the **effect** on the plaintiff, not the reasonableness of the defendant's land use (Texas). Under the Restatement (Second): an intentional invasion is unreasonable if (§ 826) the **gravity of the harm outweighs the utility** of the conduct, or the harm is serious and the defendant could compensate and still stay in business; or (§ 830) the harm is significant and could practicably be avoided.
- **Balancing factors:** character of the neighborhood, each party's land use, the extent, frequency, and duration of the interference, its effect on health and property value, the social utility of the conduct, and who was there first.
- **Trespass vs. nuisance:** see Larkin (Week 2).

**Case: Crosstex North Texas Pipeline, L.P. v. Gardiner** (Tex. 2016)
- **Facts:** Crosstex built a natural-gas compressor station next to the Gardiners' ranch. Its diesel engines ran continuously, making noise and vibration a neighbor compared to "a locomotive engine sitting in [the] driveway." Crosstex's fixes didn't help, and it allegedly declined to fully enclose the station because of cost. The jury found negligent (not intentional) nuisance.
- **Issue:** What must a plaintiff prove for private nuisance, and must the defendant's use of its land have been unreasonable?
- **Holding:** Nuisance is a legal injury (substantial interference causing unreasonable discomfort to ordinary people). Liability requires intentional, negligent, or abnormally dangerous conduct. Neither intentional nor negligent nuisance requires a separate finding that the defendant's use was unreasonable. The court of appeals' remand for a new trial under these principles was affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Private nuisance requires a substantial, unreasonable interference with P's use and enjoyment of land. The injury (the nuisance) is separate from the basis of liability (intentional and unreasonable, negligent, or abnormally dangerous conduct).

### Ordinary Sensibilities
**Rule: Ordinary sensibilities.** Nuisance is judged objectively: would the interference unreasonably affect a person of ordinary sensibilities in the same circumstances? A plaintiff's special sensitivity doesn't count (unlike the eggshell-skull rule in negligence).

**Case: Impellizerri v. Jamesville Federated Church** (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1979)
- **Facts:** A church played carillon bells several times a day for about four minutes each. The neighbors said the bells kept their son (who had a neurological disease) awake and worsened the mother's migraines. The church had already moved the speakers and reduced volume and playing time. No other neighbors complained, and ordinary traffic noise sometimes drowned out the bells.
- **Issue:** Is the carillon a nuisance that should be enjoined?
- **Holding:** No. It wouldn't bother an ordinary person in the same circumstances. The religious freedom argument also failed.
- **Takeaway:** Nuisance is judged from the standpoint of a person of ordinary sensibilities, so a plaintiff's special sensitivity (or a hypersensitive use of land) can't create a nuisance.

### Remedies
**Rule: Nuisance injunctions.** Finding a nuisance and granting an injunction are separate questions; an injunction is equitable and discretionary. Where the economic consequences of an injunction greatly exceed the harm, a court may grant an injunction that is vacated on payment of permanent damages (compensation for a servitude on the land).
- **Traditional rule (Whalen):** substantial damage = injunction, regardless of economic disparity.
- **Balancing of equities:** grant a permanent injunction unless the hardship to the defendant **greatly outweighs** the benefit to the plaintiff.
- **Coming to the nuisance** isn't a bar by itself, just a factor (Restatement (Second) § 840D). In *Spur Industries v. Del Webb*, the developer who brought residents to a feedlot got an injunction but had to **pay the feedlot's** relocation costs.
- **Calabresi–Melamed framework:** (1) injunction for the plaintiff; (2) damages for the plaintiff; (3) no nuisance (the plaintiff bears the loss); (4) injunction only if the plaintiff **pays** the defendant (Spur).
- **Right-to-farm statutes** protect established agricultural operations from nuisance claims.

**Case: Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.** (N.Y. 1970)
- **Facts:** Atlantic's large cement plant, which used the most modern dust controls available, caused dirt, smoke, and vibration that damaged neighbors' properties. Total permanent damages to all plaintiffs were $185,000, small compared with the value of the plant and the consequences of closing it.
- **Issue:** Must the court enjoin a nuisance causing substantial damage when the injunction's economic cost far exceeds the harm?
- **Holding:** No. The court granted an injunction to be **vacated once the defendant pays permanent damages** to the plaintiffs for all present and future loss.
- **Dissent:** This lets the defendant buy a license to keep polluting and removes the incentive to fix the problem.
- **Takeaway:** Even when a nuisance exists, the court may deny an injunction if its cost greatly exceeds the harm and award permanent damages instead. The case contrasts property-rule protection (injunction) with liability-rule protection (damages).

### Public Nuisance
**Rule: Public nuisance.** An unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public. Elements (Rhode Island): (1) an unreasonable interference with a public right, (2) by a defendant who controlled the instrumentality at the time it caused the harm, and (3) causation.
- **Public right:** indivisible resources shared by the public, like **air, water, and public rights of way**, not a collection of individual rights (like each child's right not to be poisoned).
- **Control:** making and selling the product isn't enough.
- Claims against manufacturers for harm caused by their products **sound in products liability**, whatever their label.
- Related: the Protection of Lawful Commerce in Arms Act bars most suits against gun makers for criminal misuse; the Clean Air Act **displaces** federal common-law nuisance claims over carbon emissions (AEP v. Connecticut).

**Case: State v. Lead Industries Ass'n** (R.I. 2008)
- **Facts:** Rhode Island sued former lead pigment manufacturers, alleging that lead paint in buildings throughout the state poisoned children, and sought abatement. After a second trial, the jury found for the State.
- **Issue:** Did the State state a public nuisance claim?
- **Holding:** No. The claim should have been dismissed at the outset: the State didn't allege interference with a **public right**, and the defendants didn't **control** the lead paint when it harmed children. Judgment reversed.
- **Takeaway:** Public nuisance requires interference with a public right and D's control of the instrumentality when the harm occurs. It doesn't fit mass product claims (lead paint); those belong in products liability.

### Private Enforcement of Public Nuisance
**Rule: Special damage.** A public nuisance is ordinarily remedied only by public authorities. A private party may sue only if they suffer special damage: harm different in kind (not just degree) from that suffered by the general public. Substantial obstruction of a public way (not total) is enough. Coming to the nuisance carries little weight where the defendant's use is illegal, and equity can enjoin despite available public remedies.

**Case: Graceland Corp. v. Consolidated Laundries Corp.** (N.Y. App. Div. 1958)
- **Facts:** A laundry regularly parked and stored trucks on the public sidewalk on its block for years. A neighboring landlord that bought and remodeled apartment buildings on the same block showed that the obstruction lowered its rental values.
- **Issue:** Can the neighbor enjoin the public nuisance?
- **Holding:** Yes. Substantial (not total) obstruction suffices, and the reduced rental value was special damage peculiar to the owner. The injunction was affirmed, modified to allow reasonable loading and unloading.
- **Takeaway:** A private party can sue over a public nuisance only if they suffered harm different in kind, not just in degree, from the public generally.

# UNIT VII — Damages (Week 14)

## Week 14 (CB 1059–1149) — Compensatory Damages; Consortium; Death Cases; Attorney's Fees

### Categories of Damages (CB 1059–62)
**Rule: Three categories of tort damages.**
- Nominal: a trivial sum when a legal wrong (usually an intentional tort) occurred but caused no actual or provable injury.
- Compensatory: put the plaintiff, as far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the tort not occurred.
- Punitive: punish and deter malicious, outrageous, or highly reckless conduct. Not available just by proving a tort; the plaintiff must make an additional showing (often clear and convincing evidence).

**Rule: Personal injury compensatory damages.** Past and future medical and rehabilitative expenses, lost earning capacity, physical pain and suffering, mental anguish, impairment or disfigurement, and loss of enjoyment of life.
- **Special damages:** out-of-pocket losses with a market value (medical bills, lost wages). **General damages:** inherently subjective losses (pain and suffering).

### Reviewing Pain and Suffering Awards
**Rule: Reviewing damages awards.** An appellate court may set aside a damages award as excessive only if it is so large that it shocks the conscience and suggests passion, prejudice, or corruption. The trial judge, ruling on a new-trial motion, acts as a thirteenth juror and can reduce the award (remittitur) or increase it (additur, where allowed).
- **Per diem arguments** (asking the jury to award a set amount per day of pain) split the jurisdictions. Failure to object waives the issue.

**Case: Seffert v. Los Angeles Transit Lines** (Cal. 1961)
- **Facts:** A bus's doors closed on Seffert's hand and foot as she boarded, and the bus dragged her. She suffered permanent, disfiguring, painful injuries to her foot, nine operations, and eight months in hospitals, with possible future amputation. The jury awarded $187,903, including $134,000 for pain and suffering, much more than her economic losses.
- **Issue:** Was the pain and suffering award excessive as a matter of law?
- **Holding:** No. The award was high but didn't shock the conscience. The per diem objection was waived. Affirmed.
- **Dissent:** Pain and suffering awards are arbitrary; they compensate a loss money can't restore, and their real function is often to pay the plaintiff's attorney's fees. The award was excessive.
- **Takeaway:** Appellate courts overturn pain-and-suffering awards only if they shock the conscience, and the trial judge can use remittitur or additur. The case also raises the policy debate behind caps on non-economic damages.

### Calculating Economic Damages (CB 1070–85)
- **Lost earning capacity:** compensates lost **capacity** to earn, not just actual lost wages. Available to people outside the paid workforce.
- **Medical expenses:** all reasonably incurred past expenses and estimated future expenses over the plaintiff's life expectancy.
- **Eggshell skull and preexisting conditions:** the defendant takes the plaintiff as they find them, but pays only for the harm it actually caused (aggravation of a preexisting condition, not the condition itself).
- **Mitigation:** plaintiffs must take reasonable steps to minimize damages; the defendant bears the burden of proving failure to mitigate.
- **Present value:** future losses paid as a lump sum are **discounted** to present value and adjusted for **inflation** (or a "total offset" approach).
- **Taxes:** personal-injury compensatory damages are **not taxable** (26 U.S.C. § 104(a)(2)).
- **Interest:** post-judgment interest is universal; many states allow prejudgment interest on past expenses.
- **Collateral source rule:** the defendant can't reduce damages by showing the plaintiff was compensated by **independent sources** (insurance, sick pay). The plaintiff may recover from both, but insurers often have **subrogation** rights to be repaid from the tort recovery. Many states have modified the rule by statute.
- **Periodic payments and structured settlements:** some states require future damages to be paid over time instead of as a lump sum.

### Special Without General Damages
**Rule: Special without general damages (Louisiana).** A verdict awarding medical expenses but no pain and suffering is not legal error per se. The reviewing court asks whether the awards are so inconsistent as to be an abuse of discretion. The plaintiff must prove compensable suffering by a preponderance; damage awards get great deference.
- A tortfeasor who exposes the victim to a reasonable possibility of serious injury is liable for reasonable **precautionary** medical expenses, even if no injury results.

**Case: Wainwright v. Fontenot** (La. 2000)
- **Facts:** A Walgreens pharmacist mislabeled a child's Prozac prescription at four times the prescribed dose. The child took three high doses; his parents said he became violent. He was hospitalized overnight for observation and released. The jury awarded medical expenses but nothing for pain and suffering.
- **Issue:** Is a verdict awarding medical expenses but no general damages automatically wrong?
- **Holding:** No. The jury could reasonably find the hospital visit was **precautionary** and the child didn't prove compensable suffering. The verdict was reinstated.
- **Takeaway:** Awarding medical expenses without pain and suffering isn't automatically error. P must prove compensable suffering, and courts defer heavily to the jury.

### Loss of Enjoyment of Life
**Rule: Loss of enjoyment of life.** Some degree of cognitive awareness is a prerequisite to recovering for pain and suffering and loss of enjoyment of life. Loss of enjoyment of life is not a separate category from pain and suffering; it's one factor in a single award for non-pecuniary loss.
- Rationale: non-pecuniary damages are **compensatory**. An award that the plaintiff can't experience has no compensatory purpose and would be **punitive**.

**Case: McDougald v. Garber** (N.Y. 1989)
- **Facts:** During a C-section, Emma McDougald suffered oxygen deprivation that left her permanently comatose. The parties disputed whether she had any awareness. The jury was told loss of enjoyment of life required **no** awareness and awarded it separately ($1 million for pain and suffering and $3.5 million for loss of enjoyment of life).
- **Issue:** Is awareness required for loss of enjoyment of life, and should it be a separate award?
- **Holding:** Yes, "some level of awareness" is required, and it should **not** be awarded separately (separate awards risk duplication and inflation). New trial on non-pecuniary damages.
- **Dissent:** Loss of enjoyment of life is an **objective** loss the plaintiff suffers whether or not she is aware of it.
- **Takeaway:** Recovery for pain and suffering and for loss of enjoyment of life requires some cognitive awareness. Loss of enjoyment is part of a single award for non-economic loss, not a separate category.

### Consortium
**Rule: Loss of consortium.** A derivative claim by a family member for loss of companionship, society, and services caused by injury to another.
- **Spousal consortium** for a non-fatal injury is recognized virtually everywhere.
- **Child's consortium** for a parent's serious, permanent injury is recognized in many states, including Texas.
- **Parent's (filial) consortium** for a child's **non-fatal** injury: Texas **rejects** it.
- **Unmarried cohabitants** generally can't recover (Elden v. Sheldon).
- Courts weigh deterrence, loss-shifting, and fairness against the burden of expanding liability before recognizing new relational claims.

**Case: Roberts v. Williamson** (Tex. 2003)
- **Facts:** A newborn suffered permanent brain injury and developmental delays allegedly caused by a doctor's delayed treatment. Her life expectancy wasn't shortened. Her parents sought loss of consortium.
- **Issue:** Does Texas recognize a parent's claim for loss of consortium from a non-fatal injury to a child?
- **Holding:** No. The parent–child relationship in this direction doesn't involve the same dependency as a child's reliance on a parent, the damages would overlap with the child's own recovery, and recognizing the claim would invite expansion to other relatives.
- **Dissent:** It's inconsistent to allow parents to recover for a child's death but not a child's serious injury.
- **Takeaway:** Spouses can recover for loss of consortium, but many courts refuse to extend it to other relationships (parents and children) as a line-drawing matter.

### Wrongful Death of a Child
**Rule: Wrongful death and survival.** At common law, tort claims died with the victim. Today, by statute:
- Wrongful death: designated relatives recover their own losses from the death (lost support, services, and, in most states now, companionship).
- Survival: the decedent's own claims survive as estate assets (pre-death pain and suffering, medical expenses, lost earnings up to death).
- No recovery for lost earnings after death in a survival action; most states deduct the decedent's personal consumption; no recovery for the decedent's loss of life's pleasures after death.
- Pecuniary-loss statutes: traditionally barred companionship, but the trend allows it. Grief is generally excluded unless the statute allows it.
- Derivative: the decedent's comparative negligence reduces the beneficiaries' recovery. Remarriage of a surviving spouse is generally inadmissible.

**Case: Green v. Bittner** (N.J. 1980)
- **Facts:** High school senior Donna Green was killed in a car accident. Her parents sued for wrongful death. The jury, told to award only pecuniary losses (mainly household services) minus the costs of raising and educating her, awarded nothing.
- **Issue:** Are recoverable "pecuniary injuries" limited to services and financial contributions, and can a zero verdict stand?
- **Holding:** The jury may award the **pecuniary value of lost companionship** and of lost **advice, guidance, and counsel** as the parents age (limited to the kind of services that could be purchased, not grief). A zero verdict for a child's death should ordinarily be set aside. New trial on damages.
- **Takeaway:** In the wrongful death of a child, "pecuniary loss" can include the value of the child's services, companionship, and guidance, but not grief or emotional loss.

### Attorney's Fees and the Contingency Fee (CB 1140–49)
- **American rule:** each side pays its own attorney's fees, win or lose. The winner recovers only narrow court costs.
- **Contingency fee:** the plaintiff's lawyer takes a percentage of the recovery (usually 33⅓%, sometimes up to 40–45%) and often advances litigation expenses. Some states **cap** contingency fees (e.g., New Jersey's sliding scale).
- **Defense:** liability insurers must **defend** covered claims, and defense costs don't reduce policy limits.
- **English (loser-pays) rule:** used in Britain and most of Europe.
- **Rule 68 offers of judgment:** a plaintiff who rejects an offer and does worse at trial pays the defendant's later costs.
- Pain and suffering awards often effectively fund the plaintiff's attorney's fee (the Seffert dissent's point).

# QUICK REFERENCE — Exam Rules

**Intentional torts**
- **Intent:** purpose **or** knowledge to a substantial certainty (Beauchamp). Battery needs only intent to contact (single intent, Wagner). Children and the mentally ill can intend.
- **Battery:** intent + harmful or offensive contact (reasonable sense of dignity; objects attached to the person count).
- **Assault:** intent + apprehension of **imminent** harmful or offensive contact. Words alone aren't enough; future threats aren't assault (Dickens).
- **False imprisonment:** intent to confine + confinement (barriers, force, threats, false assertion of authority) + awareness or harm. Objective test (McCann vs. Fojtik).
- **Transferred intent:** between persons and between the five trespass-based torts (Singer).
- **Participation:** conspiracy (agreement) vs. aiding and abetting (knowing, substantial assistance); liability for foreseeable acts in furtherance (Halberstam).
- **Trespass to land:** intent to enter; no harm needed; intangible invasions need substantial physical damage (Larkin).
- **Chattels:** trespass (interference, actual damages) vs. conversion (serious interference, full value).

**Defenses to intentional torts**
- **Consent:** actual (capacity, no duress or substantial mistake, willingness) or apparent; scope limits (Hellriegel, Reavis). Emergency/presumed consent.
- **Self-defense:** reasonable, proportional force; deadly force only against death or serious bodily harm; retreat before deadly force except at home (Silas).
- **Property:** reasonable nondeadly force only; never deadly force for property alone (Brown v. Martinez).
- **Necessity:** public = complete; private = privilege to enter, owner can't expel (Ploof), but actor pays for damage (Vincent).

**Negligence**
- **Elements:** duty, breach, cause in fact, scope of liability, damages.
- **Standard:** objective reasonable person (Vaughan); common knowledge (Delair); children = like age, intelligence, experience, except adult activities (Goss); mental disability = no allowance (Creasy); physical disability = reasonable person with that disability (Haley).
- **Breach:** Hand formula **B < PL** (Carroll Towing); foreseeable **and** unreasonable risk (Pitre); jury question except in plain cases (Pokora over Goodman).
- **Negligence per se:** statute + protected class + type of harm + unexcused violation + causation (Martin). Excuses (Tedla); rebuttable approach (Barnum); courts may decline to adopt a statute (Perry).
- **Custom:** evidence, not conclusive (Dempsey), except medical malpractice, where the professional standard governs (Shilkret; outlier Helling). Informed consent = reasonable patient standard (Miller).
- **Res ipsa:** ordinarily doesn't happen without negligence + defendant's control + no plaintiff contribution; permissive inference (Byrne, Morejon); general care evidence doesn't defeat it (Swiney); multiple defendants in control of an unconscious patient (Ybarra).

**Duty**
- **Approaches:** foreseeability-based (Brown v. Kerr; Cardozo in Palsgraf) vs. Restatement (Third) risk-creation default with policy-based no-duty rules (Thompson).
- **No duty to rescue,** except special relationship (Grimes, Farwell), undertaking (Farwell), causing the peril (Maldonado), relationship with a dangerous person + identifiable victim (Thompson v. Alameda vs. Tarasoff), statute (Uhr = no private right). Misfeasance vs. nonfeasance (Price).
- **Policy limits:** crushing liability (Strauss); social hosts (Graff).
- **Landowners:** invitee/licensee/trespasser; exceeding the license makes one a trespasser (Cochran); merger of licensee/invitee (Nelson); Rowland; attractive nuisance § 339 (Bennett); crime: balancing test (Posecai).
- **Emotional harm:** zone of danger (Falzone); bystander = serious injury + close relationship + observation at the scene + severe distress (Portee); Rowland factors (Catsouras); no general NIED duty in Texas (Boyles).
- **Economic loss:** no recovery without physical harm, contract, or special relationship (Aikens).

**Causation and scope**
- **Cause in fact:** but-for, more likely than not; circumstantial proof OK (Stubbs); Daubert gatekeeping (Joiner); loss of chance = proportional damages (Matsuyama).
- **Multiple causes:** sufficient causes → each liable for all (Kingston); alternative liability burden shift (Summers); market share (Sindell).
- **Scope:** direct consequences (Polemis) → foreseeable **kind** of harm (Wagon Mound I); type not manner (Hughes vs. Doughty); same forces, same general sort (Kinsman); real but remote risk (Wagon Mound II); thin skull; Restatement (Third) risk rule.
- **Intervening causes:** foreseeable acts don't supersede (Brauer); unforeseeable ones do (Latzel).

**Plaintiff's conduct and apportionment**
- **Contributory negligence:** total bar (Butterfield) → **pure** (Hoffman) or **modified** 49%/50% (Bradley) comparative fault.
- **Assumption of risk:** express (enforceable unless bargaining power, Tunkl public interest, gross negligence, or a safety statute: LaFrenz); primary = no duty for inherent risks (Jones v. Three Rivers); secondary = merged into comparative fault (Herod); Nevada abolished implied AR (Auckenthaler).
- **Vicarious liability:** scope of employment (Wong-Leong); apparent agency (Jones v. HealthSouth); nondelegable duties.
- **Joint and several:** each pays all for an indivisible injury; contribution; reforms (Walt Disney).

**Strict liability**
- **Animals:** abnormal dangerous propensity + knowledge; otherwise negligence (Duren). Wild animals strictly liable.
- **Abnormally dangerous activities:** Rylands; rejected in Losee; § 520 six factors (Klein); due care can't prevent (Indiana Harbor); harm within the risk (Foster); Coase.

**Products liability**
- **Negligence:** no privity required (MacPherson). **Warranty:** UCC §§ 2-313 to 2-318. **Strict liability:** Greenman; § 402A.
- **Manufacturing defect:** departs from design; malfunction inference (Smoot).
- **Design defect:** consumer expectations vs. **risk-utility + reasonable alternative design** (Branham; Riley).
- **Warning defect:** adequate and specific (Lewis); obvious to users = no duty, but the plaintiff's own knowledge = causation (Burke); reasonably knowable at the time, burden on the defendant (Feldman); learned intermediary.
- **Pre-emption:** express, field, conflict; parallel requirements survive (Bates). Regulatory compliance is evidence, not conclusive.

**Nuisance**
- **Private:** substantial interference + unreasonable effect on a person of ordinary sensibilities + intentional, negligent, or abnormally dangerous conduct (Crosstex, Impellizerri).
- **Remedies:** injunction vs. permanent damages (Boomer); coming to the nuisance is a factor (Spur).
- **Public:** interference with a public right + control (Lead Industries); private plaintiffs need special damage different in kind (Graceland).

**Damages**
- **Compensatory:** medical expenses, lost earning capacity, pain and suffering; deferential review (Seffert, Wainwright); awareness required, no separate hedonic award (McDougald); collateral source rule; present value.
- **Consortium:** spousal yes; parent for a child's non-fatal injury no in Texas (Roberts).
- **Death:** wrongful death (survivors' losses) vs. survival (decedent's losses); pecuniary value of companionship (Green).
- **Fees:** American rule; contingency fees.
