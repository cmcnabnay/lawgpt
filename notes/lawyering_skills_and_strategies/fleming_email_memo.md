Dr. Fleming will likely be shielded from liability by the Texas Good Samaritan statute because he administered emergency care in good faith, he was not at the hospital in any professional capacity, and he would not ordinarily receive or be entitled to receive payment for helping Dr. Golden revive the infant.

Under the Texas Good Samaritan statute, "[a] person who in good faith administers emergency care is not liable in civil damages for an act performed during the emergency unless the act is wilfully or wantonly negligent." Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code Ann. § 74.151(a) (West 2019). The statute does not apply to care administered (1) "for or in expectation of remuneration" or (2) by a person who was at the scene because he was "soliciting business or seeking to perform a service for remuneration." *Id.* § 74.151(b). The Good Samaritan statute is an affirmative defense, thus the doctor raising it has the burden to conclusively establish each of its elements. *McIntyre v. Ramirez*, 109 S.W.3d 741, 745–46 (Tex. 2003). In order to show that he did not act for or in expectation of remuneration, the doctor must prove that he "would not ordinarily receive or ordinarily be entitled to receive payment under the circumstances in which the emergency care was provided." *Id.* at 742. The statute is meant to encourage physicians to respond voluntarily to medical emergencies, even in a hospital, so a doctor is only entitled to the defense if his actions are "truly voluntary, not simply part of the professional's ordinary duties." *Chau v. Riddle*, 254 S.W.3d 453, 456 (Tex. 2008) (per curiam).

In *McIntyre*, the court held that Dr. McIntyre conclusively established the Good Samaritan defense, and the summary judgment in his favor was reinstated. 109 S.W.3d at 749. Dr. McIntyre was on the labor and delivery floor visiting one of his own patients when he responded to a "Dr. Stork" page for a delivery with no doctor present. *Id.* at 743. He was not on call, had never treated the mother, and testified that this was not a situation for which he would ever charge. *Id.* at 748–49. The mother argued that Dr. McIntyre made his living delivering babies at that same hospital, however, the court found that this was not relevant because the case involved a medical emergency and no pre-existing relationship or duty to respond. *Id.* at 749. In *Chau*, the court reached the opposite result and held that Dr. Riddle was not entitled to summary judgment on the defense. 254 S.W.3d at 456. Dr. Riddle was the on-call anesthesiologist for the labor and delivery suites, and the attending obstetrician asked him to intubate a newborn who was not breathing. *Id.* at 454. An expert testified that Dr. Riddle was part of the "labor and delivery team" and that intubating a newborn was part of his job, which raised a fact issue as to whether his care was part of his regular paid duties. *Id.* at 456.

The circumstances of *McIntyre* are similar to Dr. Fleming's. In *McIntyre*, the doctor was at the hospital for a reason unrelated to the emergency and responded to a patient he had never treated. Here, Dr. Fleming was at the hospital to visit his mother after her surgery when he saw Dr. Golden attempting to resuscitate an infant who appeared to not be breathing. This is clearly an emergency, and Dr. Fleming immediately attempted to help. You have asked me to assume that he was not wilfully or wantonly negligent, and nothing in the facts suggests that he acted in bad faith. Dr. Fleming's facts are stronger than Dr. McIntyre's. Dr. McIntyre was treating his own patients at that same hospital, while Dr. Fleming no longer has admitting privileges and does not treat patients at any hospital. Dr. Fleming also neither sought nor received payment for his help. The parents will likely argue that Dr. Fleming is a practicing family medicine doctor who is paid to treat patients, however, the court rejected this same argument in *McIntyre* because the inquiry is whether the doctor would ordinarily be paid under the circumstances of the emergency, not in his everyday practice. Unlike in *Chau*, where Dr. Riddle was on call, was asked by the attending physician to intubate the newborn, and was expected to do so as part of his job, Dr. Fleming was not on call, was not part of the emergency room staff, and had no duty to respond. Dr. Fleming was not at the hospital soliciting business either, thus the exception in § 74.151(b)(2) does not apply. Since the burden is on Dr. Fleming, we should get an affidavit from him similar to Dr. McIntyre's, stating that he did not charge for his care and would not ordinarily charge or be entitled to charge under these circumstances.

It is evident that Dr. Fleming administered emergency care in good faith and was not wilfully or wantonly negligent. Dr. Fleming had no privileges at the hospital, no duty to respond, and no relationship with the infant, and he did not seek or receive payment. As in *McIntyre*, his care was voluntary and not part of his ordinary duties. Therefore, the Texas Good Samaritan statute will likely shield Dr. Fleming from liability in the parents' suit.
