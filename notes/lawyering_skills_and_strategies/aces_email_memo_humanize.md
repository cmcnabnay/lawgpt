Re: Al Gurvin; whether he can succeed on a copyright infringement claim against ProBall Inc., owner of the Franklin Aces, for the use of his logo design

Good afternoon, Ms. Lee,

You have asked me to evaluate the likelihood that Al Gurvin would succeed if he litigated his copyright infringement claim against the Franklin Aces. The answer is probably no. While the Aces logo is virtually identical to Mr. Gurvin's sketch, there is no plausible evidence that Monica Dean, the ForwardDesigns artist who created the logo, ever had access to his sketch.

Courts in this circuit determine copyright infringement through circumstantial evidence because direct evidence of copying is rare. Savia v. Malcolm, slip op. at 2 (D. Frank. 2003). The courts apply a two prong test, which asks whether the works are substantially similar and whether the alleged infringer had access to the copyrighted work. Id. The more similar the works, the less evidence of access is needed, however, "plausible evidence of access must always be found." Id. Access can be inferred when the earlier work was widely available to the public. In Fred Fisher, Inc. v. Dillingham, 298 F. 145 (S.D.N.Y. 1924), Jerome Kern was found liable for copying the bass line of a wildly popular song because he was a working songwriter who kept up with current popular music and therefore must have heard it. Similarly, in Bright Tunes Music Corp. v. Harrisongs Music, Ltd., 420 F. Supp. 177 (S.D.N.Y. 1976), aff'd sub nom. ABKCO Music Inc. v. Harrisongs Music, Ltd., 722 F.2d 988 (2d Cir. 1983), George Harrison was found liable for "unconsciously" copying the melody of an earlier popular song, even though the court believed that he did not consciously copy it.

In Savia, the court granted summary judgment to the defendant even though the melodies of the two songs were virtually identical. Savia, slip op. at 2-3. Savia's song had only been released to the public in the closing credits of an NC-17 rated motion picture that played in a single Franklin City theater for three weeks, and Malcolm was four years old at the time. Id. at 3. Savia argued that Malcolm might have heard the song from someone who had seen the motion picture, but the court rejected this argument because there was not "a scintilla of evidence to justify that conclusion." Id. Since access could not be plausibly inferred, the court concluded that Malcolm's song was original with her. Id.

The first prong of the test is satisfied in Mr. Gurvin's case. His sketch shows a hand holding four aces fanned out, from left to right, the ace of diamonds, ace of clubs, ace of hearts, and ace of spades. The Aces logo is identical to the sketch except that the outline of the hand is somewhat different. Like the melodies in Savia, the two designs are virtually identical, thus a court would likely find that they are substantially similar.

The second prong, access, is where Mr. Gurvin's claim fails. The only way his sketch left his hands was through the fax he sent to Daniel Luce, the CEO of the Franklin Sports Authority, on September 25, 2025. Mr. Luce stated in his affidavit that he believes he threw the fax in the trash, since he knew the team had already hired ForwardDesigns to design the logo. The Franklin Sports Authority is entirely separate from ProBall Inc., and the two are not under common ownership. Mr. Luce stated that his only contact with anyone from ForwardDesigns was passing greetings in the lobby and the elevator, and that the Authority only meets with ProBall executives to coordinate the construction and operation of the new stadium. Ms. Dean stated that she has never met Mr. Luce and that she does not recall ever seeing a sketch of a logo idea created by anyone else. Unlike the songs in Fred Fisher and Bright Tunes, Mr. Gurvin's sketch was never released to the public, so a court cannot infer that Ms. Dean must have come across it the way a working songwriter would hear a popular song. Because the sketch was never public, the theory of unconscious copying from Bright Tunes does not help Mr. Gurvin either, as that theory still requires that the defendant had access to the work.

Mr. Gurvin's strongest argument for access is the layout of the Administrative Building. Ms. Dean worked out of ProBall's offices on the fifth floor of the same building where the Authority's offices and its shared fax machine are located on the second floor. Ms. Dean also occasionally has lunch with Julie Covington, a friend who works in the Authority's transportation office. Mr. Gurvin could argue that his fax sat on the shared machine or passed through the Authority's offices and reached Ms. Dean through Ms. Covington. The timing would also allow for copying, since the fax was sent in September 2025 and Ms. Dean drew her design about five months before her July 2026 affidavit, around February 2026. However, there is no evidence that Ms. Covington ever saw the fax or that she discussed it with Ms. Dean. This argument is the same kind of speculation that the court rejected in Savia, where the plaintiff argued that the defendant might have heard the song through someone who had seen the motion picture. Without evidence connecting the fax to Ms. Dean, a court would likely reject this argument as well.

Ms. Dean also has a credible explanation for how she created the design on her own. She stated that a hand holding the four aces was an obvious choice for a team called the Aces, and that she drew inspiration from many versions of that image in clip art collections on the Internet that were not protected by copyright. She also explained the order of the cards, which alternate between a red suit and a black suit and end with the ace of spades because it is the most striking and familiar card. This explanation accounts for the exact similarity between the two designs without any copying. In Fred Fisher, Kern's argument that he drew from earlier unprotected works failed because he could not prove he was aware of those works before the lawsuit. Ms. Dean may be in a stronger position than Kern if she can produce the clip art she used. Either way, the burden is on Mr. Gurvin to show plausible evidence of access first, and the record does not provide it.

Mr. Gurvin should also be aware that he has not yet registered his copyright with the United States Copyright Office, and he must do so before he can bring a lawsuit.

It is evident that the Aces logo and Mr. Gurvin's sketch are substantially similar. However, Mr. Gurvin's sketch was never public, Mr. Luce likely discarded the fax, and there is no evidence that the sketch ever reached Ms. Dean or anyone at ForwardDesigns or ProBall. As in Savia, the near identity of the two works cannot make up for the absence of plausible evidence of access. Therefore, Mr. Gurvin is unlikely to succeed on his copyright infringement claim against the Franklin Aces.
