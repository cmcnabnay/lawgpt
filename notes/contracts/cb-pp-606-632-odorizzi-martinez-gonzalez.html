1. Rules

Rule: Rescission for lack of real or free consent

  • Source: Case holding. Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District applies Cal. Civ. Code § 1689 (rescission) and § 1567 (consent not real or free). Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC cites Cal. Civ. Code § 1689(b)(1) for both economic duress and undue influence

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A party may rescind a contract or other consensual act where the party's consent "had not been real or free," meaning it was obtained through duress, menace, fraud, undue influence, or mistake

  • Legal Question: When may a party undo a transaction it apparently agreed to?

  • Requirements:

    1. An apparent consent to a transaction, such as a contract or a resignation

    2. That consent was obtained through duress, menace, fraud, undue influence, or mistake

  • Application: This is the umbrella vehicle for the defenses in this reading. Odorizzi used it to try to rescind his resignation. Martinez-Gonzalez used § 1689(b)(1) to resist enforcement of arbitration agreements. The court goes through each ground one by one, and one ground is enough even if the others fail

  • Exceptions: Each ground has its own elements, and a failure on one ground does not defeat another. Odorizzi lost on duress, menace, fraud, and mistake but survived on undue influence. The Odorizzi court expressly did not decide whether his rescission was timely under Cal. Civ. Code § 1691

 

Rule: Pleading sufficiency on demurrer

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A pleading seeking rescission for lack of free consent is sufficient if, "stripped of its conclusions, it sets forth sufficient facts to justify legal relief," and the pleading is "liberally construed"

  • Legal Question: Does a complaint survive a demurrer when it alleges grounds for rescission?

  • Requirements:

    1. Ignore the complaint's legal conclusions

    2. The remaining factual allegations must be enough to justify relief on at least one theory

  • Application: The court tested each theory against the facts alleged rather than the labels used. The bare allegation of a "confidential relationship" failed because no supporting facts were pleaded. The undue influence facts (40 hours without sleep, the visit to his apartment, no time to consult an attorney) were enough. Whether his will was actually overborne "requires a finding of fact" and cannot be decided on the pleadings

  • Exceptions: Conclusory labels such as "confidential relationship" or "fraud" do not count without supporting facts. Surviving a demurrer is not a ruling on the merits, and Odorizzi expressly expressed "no opinion on the merits"

 

Rule: Cal. Civ. Code § 1569

  • Source: Statute as applied in the case holding of Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Duress consists of the unlawful confinement of another's person, relatives, or property, which causes the other to consent to a transaction through fear

  • Legal Question: When does physical coercion through unlawful confinement make consent voidable?

  • Requirements:

    1. Unlawful confinement of the person, the person's relatives, or the person's property

    2. The confinement causes consent

    3. The consent is given through fear

  • Application: Under this traditional, narrow definition, a plaintiff must show actual unlawful confinement. Odorizzi alleged nothing like that, so the claim failed

  • Exceptions: The action must be unlawful. Casebook Note 3 says this definition is "substantially narrower" than the Restatement (Second) concept of duress, and that California law has since grown to include wrongful threats that leave the victim without reasonable alternatives (Rich & Whillock). See the economic duress rule below

 

Rule: Cal. Civ. Code § 1570

  • Source: Statute as applied in Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, which also cites Restatement, Contracts §§ 492, 493

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Menace is technically a threat of duress, or a threat of injury to the person, property, or character of another. It is often used interchangeably with duress, and like duress the threat must be unlawful

  • Legal Question: When does a threat, as opposed to actual confinement, make consent voidable?

  • Requirements:

    1. A threat of duress, or a threat of injury to person, property, or character

    2. The threat is unlawful

    3. The threat induces consent

  • Application: Odorizzi argued the threat to suspend, dismiss, and publicize his arrest injured his "character." The court held the threat was not unlawful because the officials had a legal right and duty to start those proceedings

  • Exceptions: A threat to take legal action is not unlawful unless the person making it knows the claim is false (see the next rule)

 

Rule: Lawful threat of legal action

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, citing Leeper v. Beltrami and Schumm by Whymer v. Berg

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: "A threat to take legal action is not unlawful unless the party making the threat knows the falsity of his claim." Reputational injury from good-faith legal proceedings "would remain incidental so long as the school officials acted in good faith in the performance of their duties"

  • Legal Question: Can a threat to start legal or official proceedings support duress or menace?

  • Requirements:

    1. The threatening party has a legal right, or a duty, to take the threatened action

    2. The threatening party does not know its claim is false and acts in good faith

  • Application: This defeats duress and menace claims built on threats to sue, prosecute, or start discipline. Here the school officials were required by Education Code § 13409 to begin suspension and dismissal proceedings, so the threat was lawful even though it would badly harm Odorizzi's reputation

  • Exceptions: Knowing falsity, or bad faith, makes the threat unlawful. Under the later economic duress doctrine, a "bad faith threatened use of civil process" counts as an impermissible threat. Casebook Note 4 asks whether the outcome depends on whether the officials honestly believed the charges would be upheld

 

Rule: Cal. Civ. Code § 1572

  • Source: Statute as applied in Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, citing Pearson v. Norton, Sixta v. Ochsner, Zinn v. Ex-Cell-O Corp., and Restatement, Contracts § 471

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Actual fraud involves the conscious misrepresentation, concealment, or non-disclosure of a material fact that induces the innocent party to enter the contract

  • Legal Question: What must be pleaded to rescind for actual fraud?

  • Requirements:

    1. Misrepresentation

    2. Knowledge of falsity

    3. Intent to induce reliance

    4. Justifiable reliance

    5. Resulting damage

  • Application: Odorizzi alleged misrepresentation but did not allege knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, or justifiable reliance. His actual fraud claim therefore failed at the pleading stage. Every element must be pleaded

  • Exceptions: Misrepresentation alone is not enough. Compare undue influence, where "misrepresentations of law or fact are not essential"

 

Rule: Cal. Civ. Code § 1573

  • Source: Statute as applied in Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Constructive fraud arises from a breach of duty by someone in a confidential or fiduciary relationship with another, where the breach induces justifiable reliance by the other to that person's prejudice. A confidential relationship "may exist whenever a person with justification places trust and confidence in the integrity and fidelity of another"

  • Legal Question: When can a party rescind for fraud without proving a conscious misrepresentation?

  • Requirements:

    1. A confidential or fiduciary relationship, supported by facts

    2. A breach of duty by the trusted party

    3. Justifiable reliance induced by the breach

    4. Prejudice to the relying party

  • Application: Odorizzi alleged a confidential relationship with the superintendent and principal. The only supporting fact was that they were his employer, so the claim failed

  • Exceptions: An employer-employee relationship does not by itself create a presumption of a confidential relationship. "Additional ties" must be shown. The absence of confidentiality is "especially apparent" when the parties are negotiating to end their relationship, because "each party is expected to look after his own interests"

 

Rule: Mistake as a ground for rescission

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, citing Restatement, Contracts § 502, Comment e

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Mistake requires a misapprehension of fact or law, usually about "the nature of the transaction, the identity of the parties, the identity of the things to which the contract relates, or the occurrence of collateral happenings." Incorrect predictions about future events are not mistakes

  • Legal Question: Can a party rescind because events turned out differently than the parties expected?

  • Requirements:

    1. An error of fact or law about the transaction, such as its nature, the parties, the subject matter, or collateral happenings

    2. Consent obtained through that error, or a misapprehension of law that the other party took advantage of

  • Application: Both sides knew the material facts. Their discussion "principally attempted to evaluate the probable consequences" and "predict the future course of events." The later dismissal of the criminal charges did not turn those predictions into a mistake

  • Exceptions: Mistaken speculation about future outcomes does not qualify. The fact that "speculations did not forecast the exact pattern which events subsequently took" is not enough

 

Rule: Cal. Civ. Code § 1575

  • Source: Statute as applied in Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District and Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Undue influence consists of any of the following:

    1. The use, by someone in whom confidence is placed or who holds real or apparent authority, of that confidence or authority to obtain an unfair advantage

    2. Taking unfair advantage of another's weakness of mind

    3. Taking a grossly oppressive and unfair advantage of another's necessities or distress

  • Legal Question: What conduct counts as undue influence under the California statute?

  • Requirements: Any one of these three:

    1. Abuse of confidence or authority to gain an unfair advantage

    2. Unfair advantage of weakness of mind

    3. Grossly oppressive and unfair advantage of necessities or distress

  • Application: Odorizzi relied on the weakness and distress prongs. Martinez-Gonzalez argued his economic necessity fit the third prong, but the Ninth Circuit held that his situation did not establish "weakness of mind," significant "necessities or distress," or enough "confidence" placed in Elkhorn

  • Exceptions: A confidential or authoritative relationship "need not be present when the undue influence involves unfair advantage taken of another's weakness or distress." Undue influence "cannot be used as a pretext to avoid bad bargains"

 

Rule: Undue influence two-element test

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, relying on Estate of Ricks, Kelly v. McCarthy, Hall v. Hall, and Webb v. Saunders. Adopted in Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Undue influence is "persuasion which overcomes the will without convincing the judgment." It "involves the use of excessive pressure to persuade one vulnerable to such pressure, pressure applied by a dominant subject to a servient object." The two elements "act in balance," so if either one is present to a large degree, the other need not be as great

  • Legal Question: When does persuasion become undue influence that justifies rescission?

  • Requirements:

    1. Undue susceptibility in the servient party

    2. Excessive pressure by the dominant party

    3. The result is that the servient party's apparent will is really the dominant party's will, so a "free and competent judgment" was not "merely influenced" but "dominated"

  • Application: Courts weigh the two elements together. "Whether a person of subnormal capacities has been subjected to ordinary force or a person of normal capacities subjected to extraordinary force, the match is equally out of balance." Because Martinez-Gonzalez showed no heightened susceptibility, he had to prove "extraordinary force." The "ultimate question" (Williston § 1625; Restatement, Contracts § 497, Comment c) is a question of fact

  • Exceptions: Misrepresentation is not required. "A party may be led but not driven." Ordinary persuasion and later second thoughts are not enough (see the bad-bargain limitation below)

 

Rule: Undue susceptibility

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, citing Cal. Civ. Code §§ 38, 39, and 1575. Applied in Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC, citing Keithley v. Civil Service Board

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Susceptibility is "a lessened capacity of the object to make a free contract." It runs along a spectrum:

    1. Total weakness of mind, meaning no understanding (§ 38)

    2. A lesser weakness that destroys contractual capacity (§ 39)

    3. A "still lesser weakness" that is enough for undue influence (§ 1575). This weakness "need not be longlasting nor wholly incapacitating"

  • Legal Question: How weak or vulnerable must a party be to satisfy the first element of undue influence?

  • Requirements: A lessened capacity to contract freely. It may come from "a lack of full vigor due to age, physical condition, emotional anguish, or a combination of such factors," which leaves the person unable "to act with unencumbered volition"

  • Application: Odorizzi pleaded enough: severe mental and emotional strain from his arrest, questioning, booking, and release on bail, plus 40 hours without sleep. Martinez-Gonzalez did not show susceptibility because he had a secondary-school education, could read and write Spanish, had worked in agriculture since age six, and supported his family. The fact that he quit voluntarily in 2017 "confirms that he had no undue susceptibility"

  • Exceptions: Reported cases "usually involve elderly, sick, senile persons." A modest socioeconomic background or financial need, "without more," does not establish susceptibility under the Martinez-Gonzalez majority. The dissent stresses his "dire circumstances"

 

Rule: Excessive pressure factors

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, illustrated by Moore v. Moore and Weger v. Rocha. Applied in Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC, citing Keithley v. Civil Service Board

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Overpersuasion usually follows a pattern involving several of seven elements. "If a number of these elements are simultaneously present, the persuasion may be characterized as excessive," but "there are no fixed definitions or inflexible formulas," and courts look at "the entire context"

  • Legal Question: When has persuasion "overflowed" its normal banks and become excessive pressure?

  • Requirements: Several of the following, usually present at the same time:

    1. Discussion of the transaction at an unusual or inappropriate time

    2. Consummation of the transaction in an unusual place

    3. Insistent demand that the business be finished at once

    4. Extreme emphasis on untoward consequences of delay

    5. Use of multiple persuaders by the dominant side against a single servient party

    6. Absence of third-party advisers to the servient party

    7. Statements that there is no time to consult financial advisers or attorneys

  • Application: Odorizzi: two officials came to his apartment right after his arrest, said there was no time to consult an attorney, and used a "carrot-and-stick technique" (resign now and avoid publicity, or be dismissed and publicized). Moore: the husband's relatives approached his pregnant widow two days after the funeral. Weger: a two-hour session in a hospital with a plaintiff in a cast. The Martinez-Gonzalez majority found the hotel parking lot, the hurrying, and the lack of time to read or consult explained by business practicalities, and noted that Elkhorn did not "interfere" with asking for time. The dissent counted five of the seven factors in the district court's findings

  • Exceptions: Not all seven are required. Keithley found undue influence without factors (4) and (7), and Kelly v. Provident Life allowed a claim even with none of the factors, where the defendant knew of the plaintiff's mental illness and acted in bad faith. The Odorizzi court said a business-hours meeting, with notice of the statutory options, freedom to consult counsel, and an overnight to decide, would make an excessive-pressure claim "extremely unlikely." Under Martinez-Gonzalez (citing Robison v. City of Manteca), failing to offer time to read or consult is not pressure if nothing prevented the party from asking. Hurrying to accommodate others waiting in line is not an "insistent demand"

 

Rule: Bad-bargain limitation on undue influence

  • Source: Case holding, Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District, citing Estate of Anderson. Quoted in Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: "Undue influence cannot be used as a pretext to avoid bad bargains or escape from bargains which refuse to come up to expectations." If someone is "temporarily persuaded against our better judgment," they "must abide the consequences of the risks inherent in managing our own affairs"

  • Legal Question: Is persuasive salesmanship, or later regret, enough for rescission?

  • Requirements: The party seeking rescission must show more than regret, hindsight, or skilled persuasion. It must show the pattern of overpersuasion acting on a susceptible party

  • Application: The Odorizzi court's examples: a woman who buys a dress on impulse after the saleswoman "used all her wiles," and a man who buys desert land expecting "another Palm Springs." Neither can rescind. The Martinez-Gonzalez majority invoked this limit in rejecting the claim

  • Exceptions: Does not apply once the persuasion has become excessive pressure on a susceptible party, as alleged in Odorizzi

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 177(1)

  • Source: Restatement, quoted in the casebook narrative (Note 1 after Odorizzi)

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Undue influence is "unfair persuasion of a party who is under the domination of the person exercising the persuasion or who by virtue of the relation between them is justified in assuming that that person will not act in a manner inconsistent with his welfare"

  • Legal Question: What is the Restatement's definition of undue influence?

  • Requirements:

    1. Unfair persuasion

    2. Of a party who is either under the persuader's domination, or justified, because of the relationship, in assuming the persuader will not act against the party's welfare

  • Application: Like Odorizzi, this definition does not strictly require a special relationship, but a relationship of domination or trust is often a significant factor in the analysis

  • Exceptions: A close relationship alone, or proof that some influence was used, does not necessarily prove undue influence (Robertson v. Robertson)

 

Rule: Confidential relationship presumption of undue influence

  • Source: Casebook narrative, Note 1, citing In re Estate of Jones (Wash. Ct. App. 2012) and Robertson v. Robertson (Mo. Ct. App. 2000)

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A fiduciary relationship creates a rebuttable presumption of undue influence, but the mere existence of a close relationship, or evidence of some influence, does not necessarily prove undue influence

  • Legal Question: How does a special relationship between the parties affect proof of undue influence?

  • Requirements:

    1. A fiduciary or confidential relationship between the parties, which triggers a rebuttable presumption

    2. Actual proof of undue influence, not mere suspicion, where the presumption does not apply or is rebutted

  • Application: In Robertson, a farm manager in a relationship of confidence with an elderly grantor received a deed reciting a $100 price for land worth $100,000 to $250,000. The plaintiffs still raised only "suspicion" and offered no actual proof

  • Exceptions: The presumption is rebuttable. A suspicious price or a close relationship alone is not enough

 

Rule: Economic duress

  • Source: Case holding, Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC, citing Perez v. Uline, In re Cheryl E., Sheehan v. Atlanta Int'l Ins. Co., and Rich & Whillock v. Ashton Dev. Also casebook narrative, Note 3 after Odorizzi

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A contract signed under economic duress may be rescinded when one party commits a "wrongful act" that is "sufficiently coercive to cause a reasonably prudent person faced with no reasonable alternative" to agree to an unfavorable contract, and the act actually caused the consent

  • Legal Question: When does economic pressure, as opposed to physical confinement, make a contract voidable?

  • Requirements:

    1. A wrongful act

    2. The act is coercive enough that a reasonably prudent person, faced with no reasonable alternative, would agree to an unfavorable contract

    3. Causation: rescission is unavailable if consent would have been given notwithstanding the duress

  • Application: The doctrine is "designed to preclude the wrongful exploitation of business exigencies to obtain disproportionate exchanges of value." It is a doctrine of "last resort," used only when "conventional alternatives and remedies" are absent. The party seeking rescission bears the burden of proof. In Martinez-Gonzalez the claim failed on both the wrongful-act and reasonable-alternative elements. Note 3 asks whether Odorizzi's resignation could be rescinded under this broader modern definition. Problem 7-2 asks how it applies to a former wife's threats to disappear and her hints of suicide

  • Exceptions: "Simple hard bargaining" is not duress. The doctrine does not interfere with "freedom of contract" or "the desirability of finality of private dispute resolution." Claims rarely succeed: a cited study found only nine of 88 published cases between 1996 and 2003 decided for the claimant

 

Rule: Wrongful act requirement

  • Source: Case holding, Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC, citing Rich & Whillock, Hester v. Public Storage, Uniwill v. City of Los Angeles, Leeper v. Beltrami, and the Restatement definition of impermissible threats as adopted by California courts

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Wrongful acts "need not be unlawful or tortious," but they are limited to actions that "make a mockery of freedom of contract and undermine the proper functioning of our economic system." They must be taken for a "coercive purpose" or "in bad faith," and they do not include arrangements that "serve a practical business function"

  • Legal Question: What conduct is "wrongful" enough to support economic duress?

  • Requirements: Conduct of the following kinds, taken with a coercive purpose or in bad faith:

    1. Asserting a false claim

    2. A bad-faith threat to breach a contract

    3. A threat to withhold payment of an acknowledged debt

    4. Bad-faith threatened use of civil process

    5. Threats that breach the duty of good faith and fair dealing under a contract with the recipient

    6. Threats that would harm the recipient without significantly benefiting the party making the threat

    7. Threats where "what is threatened is otherwise a use of power for illegitimate ends"

  • Application: Rich & Whillock: a contractor forced a subcontractor facing "imminent bankruptcy" to accept a fraction of an undisputed $72,000 debt, a "classic case." Other wrongful acts found in California cases include extracting an easement and fees over $339,000 (Uniwill) and trying to foreclose on a home (Leeper). Elkhorn's post-arrival, parking-lot signing was not wrongful because there was no false claim, no bad-faith threat, no withholding of wages, no finding of coercive purpose, and the setting served a practical business function. The majority also doubted that extracting a "commonplace" arbitration agreement could ever be wrongful, since no disproportionate value was lost

  • Exceptions: Not wrongful: hard bargaining, tough business tactics, and practical business arrangements, even ones causing significant economic harm (Hester's "null and void" clause). Under the majority's reading, sympathy for a party's socioeconomic position cannot substitute for California authority. The dissent argues that the district court's findings of a deliberately "coercive environment" did establish coercive purpose, and that the majority's question about arbitration agreements attacks a "strawman"

 

Rule: Reasonable alternative requirement

  • Source: Case holding, Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC, citing CrossTalk Prods. v. Jacobson, In re Exec. Life Ins. Co., Perez v. Uline, Hester, Lanigan v. City of Los Angeles, Rich & Whillock, and Leeper

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A reasonable alternative is one that "a reasonably prudent person would follow" to avoid a coerced agreement. If one exists, there is "no compelling necessity" to submit, and "merely being put to a voluntary choice of perfectly legitimate alternatives is the antithesis of duress"

  • Legal Question: Did the party claiming duress have a realistic way out other than signing?

  • Requirements: The absence of any alternative course that a reasonably prudent person would follow instead of submitting. Whether the party acted as a reasonably prudent person is a question of fact

  • Application: Bankruptcy, financial ruin, or selling one's home are not reasonable alternatives. In the majority's view, Martinez-Gonzalez had two: he could have asked whether signing was required, and he could have used the agreement's 10-day revocation right. The district court and the dissent would defer to findings that he reasonably believed signing was mandatory, did not know of the revocation clause, and had no alternative job, housing, or way home

  • Exceptions: The need for "a job and the money offered" does not count as duress, or duress would apply in "almost any case." Losing some income is not enough when the party could make it up elsewhere. Under Lanigan, speculation about bad outcomes, even "highly likely" termination, and failure to revoke when revocation is allowed both defeat the claim

 

Rule: 9 U.S.C. § 2

  • Source: Statute (Federal Arbitration Act) as applied in Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Arbitration agreements "shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract," so generally applicable contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability may invalidate them

  • Legal Question: Can ordinary state contract defenses be used to avoid an arbitration agreement?

  • Requirements:

    1. A written arbitration agreement

    2. A generally applicable contract defense, available for the revocation of any contract, to escape enforcement

  • Application: When an employer moves to compel arbitration, the worker can raise California economic duress and undue influence. The federal court applies state substantive law (Sonner v. Premier Nutrition)

  • Exceptions: Only defenses that apply to contracts generally qualify. The majority noted that arbitration agreements are "encouraged by California law"

 

Rule: Standard of review for arbitration enforceability

  • Source: Case holding, Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC. The dissent cites Allen v. Iranon, United States v. Bontemps, and Ocean Garden v. Marktrade

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Enforceability of an arbitration agreement is reviewed de novo, but the factual findings underlying the decision are reviewed for clear error. Credibility determinations by the trial judge receive "special deference"

  • Legal Question: How much deference does an appellate court owe a trial court's findings on duress and undue influence?

  • Requirements:

    1. Legal conclusions on enforceability: de novo review

    2. Factual findings: reversed only if clearly erroneous, meaning "illogical, implausible, or without support in the record"

    3. Credibility findings: special deference

  • Application: The majority held it was clearly erroneous to find no reasonable alternatives and to find excessive pressure, because the district court "overlooked key facts." The dissent said the majority made its own findings instead of deferring, and that a clearly erroneous decision "must strike us as wrong with the force of a five-week old, unrefrigerated dead fish"

  • Exceptions: Whether found facts amount to a "wrongful act" under California law can be treated as a legal question. The majority reversed on that element "even without disturbing any of the district court's findings of fact"

 

Rule: Premarital agreement voluntariness

  • Source: Casebook narrative, Note 5, discussing In re Marriage of Bonds (Cal. 2000) and the legislative response, Cal. Fam. Code § 1615(c)(1)

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Under the Family Code provisions applied in Bonds, the party challenging a premarital agreement bears the burden of proving it was not voluntarily made, and the absence of counsel is not decisive. After the amendment, a premarital agreement is not voluntary if a party was not represented by counsel when signing, unless that party waived the right to independent counsel "in a separate writing"

  • Legal Question: When is a premarital agreement involuntary and therefore unenforceable in California?

  • Requirements:

    1. Under Bonds: the challenger proves the agreement was not voluntary

    2. Under amended § 1615(c)(1): the challenger was unrepresented and did not waive counsel in a separate writing

  • Application: Sun Bonds signed the day before the wedding, without counsel, in a language that was not her first. The agreement was upheld because there was no threat or coercion, she had been told she could seek counsel, and she understood it. The Legislature then tightened the rule on counsel

  • Exceptions: A written waiver of independent counsel in a separate writing preserves voluntariness despite lack of representation

 

Rule: Free and knowledgeable execution of prenuptial agreements

  • Source: Casebook narrative, Note 5, discussing Ellis v. Ellis (Tenn. Ct. App. 2014)

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: Under Tennessee law, a prenuptial agreement must be executed freely and knowledgeably, which is a requirement separate from the absence of duress

  • Legal Question: Can a prenuptial agreement be unenforceable even when duress is not proven?

  • Requirements:

    1. Free execution

    2. Knowledgeable execution, meaning knowledge of the full nature, extent, and value of the other party's property, through disclosure or independent knowledge

  • Application: In Ellis the agreement failed. The husband did not disclose holdings worth $4,750,000, and the wife received the agreement three days before the wedding and two days before Christmas, with no reasonable chance to consult independent counsel. Her duress claim was rejected

  • Exceptions: Duress need not be shown. This is an independent ground

 

Rule: Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act § 9

  • Source: Uniform act, quoted in the casebook narrative, Note 5. The casebook states it was adopted in 2012

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Statement: A premarital or marital agreement is unenforceable if the party against whom enforcement is sought proves any of the four listed defects

  • Legal Question: On what grounds can a premarital or marital agreement be avoided under the Uniform Act?

  • Requirements: The challenging party proves any one of these:

    1. Consent was involuntary or the result of duress

    2. The party lacked access to independent legal representation

    3. Without independent representation, the agreement lacked a notice of waiver of rights or a plain-language explanation of the marital rights modified or waived

    4. The party did not receive adequate financial disclosure before signing

  • Application: This brings duress-like concerns and procedural safeguards together into a statutory checklist. The Act also specifies what counts as access to representation, the form of the waiver notice, and the disclosure requirements

  • Exceptions: Some terms are unenforceable regardless, such as a waiver of child support (§ 10(b)(1))

2. Case Narrative

Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

Donald Odorizzi, the plaintiff, was an elementary school teacher. The defendant, Bloomfield School District, was a California public school district that employed him during 1964. He also had a contract to keep teaching the following year as a permanent employee. The opinion does not name the city or county.

On June 10 (the opinion gives the dates but does not clearly state the year), Odorizzi was arrested on criminal charges of homosexual activity. He went through arrest, police questioning, booking, and release on bail, and by his account went about 40 hours without sleep. He says he was under such severe mental and emotional strain that he could not think rationally. While he was in that state, the District's superintendent and the principal of his school came to his apartment. They said they were trying to help him and had his best interests at heart. They told him to take their advice and resign right away, and said there was no time to consult a lawyer. If he did not resign immediately, they said, the District would suspend and dismiss him and publicize the proceedings and his arrest, causing him "extreme embarrassment and humiliation." If he resigned at once, the incident would not be publicized and would not hurt his chances of teaching elsewhere. Odorizzi says that because he trusted them, they were able to substitute their judgment for his. He signed a written resignation and delivered it on June 11, and the District accepted it on June 13.

In July the criminal charges were dismissed under Penal Code § 995. The opinion does not say why. In September, Odorizzi tried to return to his teaching job, and the District refused to reinstate him. Odorizzi then sued the District for declaratory and other relief. He sought to rescind his resignation as invalid because it was obtained through duress, fraud, mistake, and undue influence, and was given while he lacked capacity to contract.

Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

Dario Martinez-Gonzalez, the plaintiff, was a farm laborer living in Mexicali, Mexico. The defendants are Elkhorn Packing Company, a farm labor contractor, and D'Arrigo Brothers, a California vegetable grower for which Elkhorn supplied labor. The court refers to them together as "Elkhorn." In Mexico, Martinez-Gonzalez earned the equivalent of about $150 a week. He had a secondary-school education, could read and write Spanish, and had worked in agriculture since he was six. He supported his wife, his mother, his stepfather (since deceased), and his diabetic mother-in-law, providing their housing and medical care. In 2015 he learned he could work for Elkhorn in the United States for up to five times what he earned in Mexico. Elkhorn knew that "behind every employee, there are three, five, even up to eight people from their families who depend on that worker."

In 2016 Elkhorn accepted his application, helped him get an H-2A temporary agricultural worker visa, and took him on an Elkhorn bus, a trip of about twelve hours, to Monterey County, California, to harvest lettuce. Elkhorn housed the workers in a hotel and drove them between the hotel and the fields. Martinez-Gonzalez believed the visa limited him to working for Elkhorn. On his first day, an Elkhorn representative told the workers it was a privilege to work for the company, that they should work hard, and that they were free to go back to Mexico if they did not want to work hard. Supervisors told them to follow company rules.

A few days after he started in the fields, Elkhorn held an orientation at around 4 p.m., after a full day's work, in the hotel parking lot. About 150 workers lined up. Martinez-Gonzalez stood for about 40 minutes, tired and hungry, with no seating, while the supervisors collecting signatures sat. At the table a supervisor flipped through a stack of new-hire papers (IRS forms, a food safety form, a workers' compensation agreement, and others) and pointed to where he should sign. Supervisors urged workers to hurry so the people behind them could sign. The stack included a Spanish-language arbitration agreement requiring all disputes with Elkhorn to be arbitrated, with a clause allowing revocation within ten days. He signed without reading it. No one explained it, gave him a copy, told him it was optional, or told him he could consult a lawyer. He did not ask for a copy, time to read, or a lawyer. No one expressly told him he had to sign to keep working, but he believed that if he refused he would get no work and be sent back to Mexico. One Elkhorn representative testified the documents were required to start work. Of the thousands of workers hired, no one had ever refused to sign.

He finished the 2016 season and went home on Elkhorn-paid transportation. He returned for the 2017 season and signed another arbitration agreement, but quit partway through and went back to Mexico on his own. In 2018 he sued Elkhorn in California state court for himself and similarly situated workers, claiming unpaid federal minimum wages under the Fair Labor Standards Act and state-law violations involving meals, wages, rest periods, and privacy. Elkhorn sought to force the claims into arbitration under the two agreements, and he resisted on the ground that the agreements were the product of economic duress and undue influence.

3. Cases

Case Name: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Odorizzi was an elementary school teacher employed by the District in 1964, under contract to teach the next year as a permanent employee

    • He was arrested on June 10 on criminal charges of homosexual activity, then questioned, booked, and released on bail, and had gone 40 hours without sleep

    • While he was in that condition, the superintendent and principal came to his apartment, said they had his best interests at heart, told him to resign immediately, and said there was no time to consult an attorney

    • They warned that if he did not resign, the District would suspend and dismiss him and publicize the proceedings, but that resigning would avoid publicity and protect his chances of teaching elsewhere

    • He signed the resignation June 11, and the District accepted it June 13

    • The criminal charges were dismissed in July under Penal Code § 995, and the District refused to reinstate him in September

    • The officials had a statutory right and duty under Education Code §§ 13403, 13408, and 13409 to begin suspension and dismissal proceedings

    • The only alleged basis for a confidential relationship was the employer-employee relationship

  • Procedural Posture:

    • California trial court: sustained the District's demurrer to Odorizzi's amended complaint without leave to amend and entered judgment dismissing the amended complaint

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Odorizzi sought review in the California District Court of Appeal on the grounds that his amended complaint stated a cause of action to rescind his resignation for duress, menace, fraud, mistake, undue influence, and lack of capacity

    • California District Court of Appeal: Odorizzi (plaintiff below) is the appellant; Bloomfield School District (defendant below) is the respondent

  • Issue: Whether a teacher's amended complaint, alleging that school officials obtained his resignation by pressing him at his apartment shortly after his arrest and sleepless detention, states a cause of action to rescind the resignation for duress, menace, fraud, mistake, or undue influence

  • Holding: The California District Court of Appeal held that the amended complaint did not state a cause of action for duress, menace, fraud (actual or constructive), or mistake, but, liberally construed, did state a cause of action for rescission based on undue influence, because it pleaded both undue susceptibility and excessive pressure

  • Rule:

    • Rescission for lack of real or free consent: A party may rescind under Cal. Civ. Code § 1689 where consent was not real or free under § 1567 because it was obtained through duress, menace, fraud, undue influence, or mistake

    • Pleading sufficiency on demurrer: A rescission pleading is sufficient if, stripped of its conclusions, it sets forth enough facts to justify legal relief

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1569: Duress is unlawful confinement of a person, relatives, or property that causes consent through fear

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1570: Menace is a threat of duress or of injury to person, property, or character, and it must be unlawful

    • Lawful threat of legal action: A threat to take legal action is not unlawful unless the threatening party knows its claim is false

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1572: Actual fraud requires misrepresentation, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, and resulting damage

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1573: Constructive fraud requires a breach of duty within a confidential or fiduciary relationship, and an employer-employee relationship alone does not create a presumption of one

    • Mistake as a ground for rescission: Mistake involves errors about the nature of the transaction, the parties, the subject matter, or collateral happenings, not incorrect predictions of future events

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1575: Undue influence includes taking unfair advantage of another's weakness of mind or a grossly oppressive and unfair advantage of another's necessities or distress

    • Undue influence two-element test: Undue influence is excessive pressure by a dominant party on a party vulnerable to it, so that the will is overborne without the judgment being convinced

    • Undue susceptibility: A lessened capacity to contract freely, which need not be long-lasting or wholly incapacitating, satisfies the first element

    • Excessive pressure factors: Persuasion may be characterized as excessive when several of the seven Odorizzi factors are present at the same time

    • Bad-bargain limitation on undue influence: Undue influence cannot be used as a pretext to avoid bad bargains or escape bargains that fail to meet expectations

  • Reasoning:

    • The appeals court rejected duress and menace because the officials' threat to begin suspension and dismissal proceedings was a lawful exercise of their legal right and duty, and any reputational harm was incidental to good-faith performance of their duties

    • The appeals court rejected actual fraud because Odorizzi alleged misrepresentation but not knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, or justifiable reliance

    • The appeals court rejected constructive fraud because the only fact pleaded was the employer-employee relationship, which creates no presumption of confidentiality, and parties negotiating the end of their relationship are expected to look after their own interests

    • The appeals court rejected mistake because both sides knew the material facts and were only predicting future events, and incorrect predictions are not mistakes

    • The appeals court found undue susceptibility adequately pleaded, because exhaustion and emotional turmoil after arrest, booking, and 40 sleepless hours could leave a person unable to exercise judgment

    • The appeals court found excessive pressure adequately pleaded: an inappropriate time right after the arrest, an unusual place (his apartment), a demand for immediate action, emphasis on the consequences of delay, two persuaders against one person, no adviser present, and a statement that there was no time to consult an attorney, all used in a "high-pressure carrot-and-stick technique"

    • The appeals court contrasted a legitimate approach (a business-hours meeting a day or two later, explanation of the Education Code options, freedom to consult counsel, overnight to decide) that would make an undue influence claim "extremely unlikely"

    • The appeals court reasoned that weakness on one side and strength on the other are equally unbalancing, so the "ultimate question" of whether a mind was dominated rather than merely influenced is a question of fact that cannot be resolved on the pleadings

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Odorizzi was the plaintiff-appellant here

    • His resignation was invalid because it was obtained through duress, menace, fraud, mistake, and undue influence

    • He lacked capacity because of severe mental and emotional strain and 40 hours without sleep

    • A confidential relationship existed between him and the superintendent and principal as the District's agents, supporting constructive fraud

    • Because of his faith and confidence in their representations, the officials substituted their will and judgment for his own

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: The District was the defendant-respondent here

    • Neither duress nor menace was involved, because the officials threatened only lawful action they had a right and duty to take, and the appeals court agreed with this contention

    • The opinion does not state the District's other arguments beyond its demurrer to the amended complaint

  • Disposition: The California District Court of Appeal reversed the judgment dismissing the amended complaint, expressing no opinion on the merits, on Odorizzi's fitness to teach, or on whether his rescission was timely

  • Importance: Odorizzi is the leading California statement of undue influence. It shows that persuasion without lies or unlawful threats can still invalidate consent when excessive pressure meets undue susceptibility. It supplies the seven-factor test that later courts, including Martinez-Gonzalez, apply. It also contrasts undue influence with duress, menace, fraud, and mistake, showing why those doctrines failed where undue influence survived. Note 3 asks you to reconsider the facts under the broader modern economic duress doctrine, and Note 4 raises questions about the officials' good faith and how you would have advised Odorizzi

 

Case Name: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

  • Document: CB pp 606-632

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Elkhorn recruited Martinez-Gonzalez from Mexicali, Mexico, helped him get an H-2A visa, and bused him about twelve hours to Monterey County, California, where it housed him in a hotel

    • A few days after he started harvesting lettuce, he signed an arbitration agreement in a stack of new-hire documents at a 4 p.m. orientation in the hotel parking lot after a full workday, tired and hungry, after standing in line about 40 minutes without seating

    • The supervisors flipped pages, pointed to signature lines, and urged workers to hurry so others in line could sign

    • The agreement was in Spanish and allowed revocation within ten days

    • He was not given an explanation, a copy, notice that signing was optional, or notice that he could consult an attorney, and he did not ask for any of these

    • No one expressly told him signing was required to keep working, but he believed it was. The district court found Elkhorn supervisors' testimony that signing was not required not credible, and one Elkhorn representative testified the documents were required to start work

    • No employee among thousands hired had ever refused to sign

    • He signed again in 2017, then quit mid-season and returned to Mexico on his own

    • He had a secondary-school education, could read and write Spanish, had worked in agriculture since age six, and supported four family members

  • Procedural Posture:

    • California state court: Martinez-Gonzalez filed suit for himself and similarly situated workers alleging FLSA minimum-wage violations and state-law claims on meals, wages, rest periods, and privacy; the opinion does not state any ruling by this court

    • Removal: Defendants Elkhorn Packing Company and D'Arrigo Brothers removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California; the opinion does not state the grounds for removal

    • U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California: held the 2016 and 2017 arbitration agreements invalid and unenforceable as products of economic duress and undue influence, and denied Defendants' motion to compel arbitration, on findings after a two-day bench trial on enforceability

    • Interlocutory appeal: Defendants-Appellants Elkhorn Packing Company and D'Arrigo Brothers sought review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit on the grounds that the district court erred in refusing to enforce the arbitration agreements

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit: Elkhorn Packing Company and D'Arrigo Brothers (defendants below) are the appellants; Martinez-Gonzalez (plaintiff below) is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether arbitration agreements that a migrant farmworker signed at a mass after-work orientation in a hotel parking lot, after traveling from Mexico and starting work, are unenforceable under California law because of economic duress or undue influence

  • Holding: The Ninth Circuit majority held the agreements not voidable for economic duress, because Elkhorn committed no wrongful act and Martinez-Gonzalez had reasonable alternatives, and not voidable for undue influence, because he showed neither undue susceptibility nor the extraordinary force required without it. The district court's contrary findings on reasonable alternatives and excessive pressure were clearly erroneous

  • Rule:

    • 9 U.S.C. § 2: Arbitration agreements are enforceable except on grounds that exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract, so generally applicable defenses such as duress may invalidate them

    • Standard of review for arbitration enforceability: Enforceability is reviewed de novo and the underlying factual findings for clear error, with special deference to credibility determinations

    • Rescission for lack of real or free consent: A contract obtained through economic duress or undue influence may be rescinded under Cal. Civ. Code § 1689(b)(1)

    • Economic duress: Economic duress requires a wrongful act coercive enough to cause a reasonably prudent person with no reasonable alternative to agree to an unfavorable contract, plus causation

    • Wrongful act requirement: A wrongful act need not be unlawful but must be taken with a coercive purpose or in bad faith such that it makes a mockery of freedom of contract, and it excludes hard bargaining and practical business arrangements

    • Reasonable alternative requirement: There is no duress if a reasonably prudent person would have followed an available alternative, and the need for a job and its money is not enough

    • Cal. Civ. Code § 1575: Undue influence arises from abuse of confidence or authority, unfair advantage of weakness of mind, or grossly oppressive and unfair advantage of necessities or distress

    • Undue influence two-element test: Undue influence requires undue susceptibility and excessive pressure, which act in balance

    • Undue susceptibility: Susceptibility means a lessened capacity to make a free contract due to age, physical condition, emotional anguish, or similar factors

    • Excessive pressure factors: Excessive pressure is assessed through the seven Odorizzi factors in the entire context, with no fixed formula

    • Bad-bargain limitation on undue influence: Undue influence cannot be used as a pretext to avoid bad bargains

  • Reasoning:

    • The Ninth Circuit majority held there was no wrongful act: nothing unlawful or tortious, no false claim, no bad-faith threat, no withholding of wages, and no finding of coercive purpose or bad faith. Holding the orientation in the hotel parking lot served a practical business function in gathering hundreds of workers

    • The majority distinguished Rich & Whillock, where the contractor deliberately threatened "economic disaster" by withholding an undisputed debt, whereas Elkhorn never threatened his wages

    • The majority doubted that extracting a "commonplace," lawful arbitration agreement could be wrongful, since he lost no identifiable "value," unlike the disproportionate losses in Rich & Whillock, Uniwill, and Leeper

    • The majority found reasonable alternatives: he could have asked whether signing was mandatory, since a reasonably prudent person would not just assume it was, and he could have revoked within ten days. His subjective belief that he would be fired was speculation under Lanigan

    • On undue influence, the majority found no susceptibility given his education, literacy, lifelong work, and role as breadwinner, and said his voluntary quitting in 2017 confirmed this

    • Without susceptibility, the majority required extraordinary force and found none. Odorizzi's examples (the widow approached days after her husband's death, the hysterical hospital patient, the 1 a.m. demand at a person's home) were "a far cry" from this case. The timing and place had business explanations, being tired and hungry is not "oppressive," Elkhorn did not stop him from asking for time or counsel, hurrying accommodated others in line, and the first-day "privilege" remarks had nothing to do with arbitration

    • Dissent (Judge Rawlinson): the majority gave "short shrift" to clear-error review and specially deferred credibility findings. The district court found a deliberately "coercive environment," which supplies the coercive purpose. Reasonable alternatives were lacking given his finances, housing, and inability to get home, an Elkhorn representative's admission that signing was required, and the fact that no worker ever refused. He could not have known of the revocation clause he was never allowed to read. Five of the seven Odorizzi factors were found. The majority's "accommodation" description and its arbitration-agreement point (a "strawman") replace the district court's findings with the majority's own

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments:

    • Elkhorn committed a wrongful act by having him sign only after he traveled from Mexico, depended on Elkhorn housing, and had started harvesting

    • Rich & Whillock supports finding economic duress

    • He lacked reasonable alternatives because his financial obligations required him to keep the job, and he reasonably believed signing was mandatory

    • The conditions of signing (after work, in a parking lot, rushed, unexplained, with no time to read or consult a lawyer) amounted to excessive pressure

    • The district court's credibility-based findings deserve deference under clear-error review

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • The arbitration agreements are enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act, and the motion to compel should have been granted

    • Elkhorn never told workers that signing was required, and its supervisors testified that signing was not required to remain employed. The district court found this testimony not credible

    • Workers were invited to ask questions about the documents. The district court did not credit this testimony

    • The agreement was in Spanish and contained a ten-day revocation right

  • Disposition: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed the district court's denial of the motion to compel arbitration and remanded, over Judge Rawlinson's dissent

  • Importance: The case shows how California's modern economic duress doctrine (wrongful act, no reasonable alternative, causation) works, and how hard it is to win. It applies Odorizzi's undue influence test in a commercial employment setting. It shows the balancing between susceptibility and pressure: when susceptibility is weak, pressure must be extraordinary. The split between majority and dissent turns on the standard of review and on how much weight economic vulnerability and an orchestrated signing environment deserve. That debate between freedom of contract and protecting weaker parties is a likely exam theme. It also shows these defenses being used against arbitration agreements under FAA § 2

4. Definitions

Undue influence

  • Definition: Persuasion that tends to be coercive, which "overcomes the will without convincing the judgment." It is excessive pressure applied by a dominant party to a party vulnerable to that pressure. Cal. Civ. Code § 1575 also defines it as abuse of confidence or authority, or unfair advantage of weakness of mind or of necessities or distress

  • Significance: It is the only ground on which Odorizzi survived the demurrer, and the second defense rejected in Martinez-Gonzalez

  • Distinction: Unlike fraud, it requires no misrepresentation. Unlike duress or menace, it requires no unlawful act or threat. Unlike incapacity, the weakness need not be total

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Overpersuasion

  • Definition: Another name for undue influence: high-pressure persuasion that works on mental, moral, or emotional weakness until it "approaches the boundaries of coercion"

  • Significance: The seven Odorizzi factors describe the typical "pattern" of overpersuasion

  • Distinction: Different from legitimate salesmanship or hard selling, which leaves the buyer bound (the dress and desert-land examples)

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Undue susceptibility

  • Definition: The first element of undue influence: "a lessened capacity of the object to make a free contract," arising from age, physical condition, emotional anguish, or a combination of these, which need not be long-lasting or wholly incapacitating

  • Significance: Odorizzi pleaded it through his exhaustion and emotional turmoil. Martinez-Gonzalez failed to show it

  • Distinction: Less than the total weakness of mind under Cal. Civ. Code § 38 or the incapacity to contract under § 39. The Odorizzi court suggested it might be called "weakness of spirit"

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Excessive pressure

  • Definition: The second element of undue influence: the dominant party's use of excessive strength, usually shown by several of the seven Odorizzi factors present at once

  • Significance: Works in balance with susceptibility. Without heightened susceptibility, "extraordinary force" is required

  • Distinction: Different from the ordinary "forces of persuasion" and from hard bargaining, which are permitted

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Servient party

  • Definition: The vulnerable party whose will is overborne in an undue influence case. Odorizzi calls this party the "servient object," and calls the party applying pressure the "dominant subject"

  • Significance: Undue influence makes the servient party's apparent will "in fact the will of the dominant person"

  • Distinction: Domination in this sense can exist without any confidential or fiduciary relationship

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Duress

  • Definition: Under Cal. Civ. Code § 1569 as applied in Odorizzi, the unlawful confinement of a person, the person's relatives, or the person's property, causing consent through fear

  • Significance: Rejected in Odorizzi because nothing unlawful occurred. Note 3 calls this definition substantially narrower than the Restatement (Second) concept

  • Distinction: Distinct from menace, which is a threat, and from economic duress, which requires a wrongful but not necessarily unlawful act

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Menace

  • Definition: Under Cal. Civ. Code § 1570, a threat of duress or a threat of unlawful injury to the person, property, or character of another

  • Significance: The threat to publicize Odorizzi's arrest and dismiss him was not menace because it was a lawful threat of proceedings the officials were required to bring

  • Distinction: Often used interchangeably with duress, but technically it is the threat rather than the act of confinement

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Economic duress

  • Definition: Under California law, a wrongful act coercive enough to cause a reasonably prudent person with no reasonable alternative to agree to an unfavorable contract, where the act actually caused the consent

  • Significance: A doctrine of "last resort" aimed at the "wrongful exploitation of business exigencies to obtain disproportionate exchanges of value." Rejected in Martinez-Gonzalez

  • Distinction: Broader than traditional duress because the act need not be unlawful or tortious. Narrower than undue influence in requiring a wrongful act and the absence of reasonable alternatives

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Wrongful act

  • Definition: For economic duress, conduct taken with a coercive purpose or in bad faith that makes "a mockery of freedom of contract," such as false claims, bad-faith threats to breach, withholding an acknowledged debt, or the Restatement's impermissible threats

  • Significance: The element the majority found missing in Martinez-Gonzalez

  • Distinction: Not the same as an unlawful act. Excludes hard bargaining and arrangements serving a practical business function

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Reasonable alternative

  • Definition: A course that "a reasonably prudent person would follow" to avoid a coerced agreement. If one exists, there is no compelling necessity to submit

  • Significance: The majority identified asking whether signing was required, and revoking within ten days, as reasonable alternatives

  • Distinction: Bankruptcy, financial ruin, or selling one's home are not reasonable alternatives, but losing a particular job or income generally does not mean there is no alternative

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Hard bargaining

  • Definition: Tough but legitimate negotiating or business tactics

  • Significance: Expressly outside economic duress. The majority treated Elkhorn's conduct as, at most, non-ideal business practice

  • Distinction: Different from a wrongful act, which requires coercive purpose or bad faith

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Actual fraud

  • Definition: Conscious misrepresentation, concealment, or non-disclosure of a material fact that induces the innocent party to contract (Cal. Civ. Code § 1572)

  • Significance: Odorizzi's claim failed for lack of allegations of knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, and justifiable reliance

  • Distinction: Requires a conscious falsehood, unlike constructive fraud and undue influence

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Constructive fraud

  • Definition: A breach of duty by someone in a confidential or fiduciary relationship that induces justifiable reliance to the other's prejudice (Cal. Civ. Code § 1573)

  • Significance: Failed in Odorizzi because no confidential relationship was supported by facts

  • Distinction: Requires no knowing misrepresentation, but does require a confidential relationship, which undue influence based on weakness or distress does not

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Confidential relationship

  • Definition: A relationship in which a person "with justification places trust and confidence in the integrity and fidelity of another"

  • Significance: Not presumed from an employer-employee relationship alone. Note 1 states that a fiduciary relationship can create a rebuttable presumption of undue influence

  • Distinction: Required for constructive fraud and for the first prong of § 1575, but not for undue influence based on weakness or distress

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Mistake

  • Definition: An error of fact or law about matters such as the nature of the transaction, the identity of the parties, the subject matter, or collateral happenings

  • Significance: Rejected in Odorizzi because the parties only made predictions that turned out wrong

  • Distinction: Different from a mistaken forecast of future events, which does not justify rescission

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Rescission

  • Definition: Undoing a transaction, such as a contract or resignation, because consent was not real or free (Cal. Civ. Code § 1689)

  • Significance: The remedy Odorizzi sought for his resignation and Martinez-Gonzalez sought for the arbitration agreements

  • Distinction: Must be timely under Cal. Civ. Code § 1691, an issue Odorizzi left undecided

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District; Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Demurrer

  • Definition: A pleading-stage challenge arguing that the complaint's facts, taken as true and stripped of conclusions, do not state a cause of action

  • Significance: The trial court sustained it without leave to amend in Odorizzi, and the appeals court reversed because undue influence was adequately pleaded

  • Distinction: Tests whether the claim is adequately pleaded, not the merits. Whether the will was overborne required a later factual finding

  • Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District

 

Clear error

  • Definition: The deferential standard for reviewing factual findings. A finding is reversed only if "illogical, implausible, or without support in the record," and credibility determinations get special deference

  • Significance: The center of the majority-dissent disagreement in Martinez-Gonzalez

  • Distinction: Contrasted with de novo review of the legal question of enforceability

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Arbitration agreement

  • Definition: A contract requiring the parties to resolve disputes through arbitration rather than in court. Under 9 U.S.C. § 2 it is enforceable except on grounds for revoking any contract

  • Significance: The contract challenged in Martinez-Gonzalez. The majority called it "commonplace" and "encouraged by California law"

  • Distinction: Subject to the same general defenses (fraud, duress, unconscionability) as other contracts

  • Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co. LLC

 

Premarital agreement

  • Definition: A contract made before marriage that sets out the parties' property and financial rights. A prenuptial agreement is the same thing. Agreements made during marriage are called postnuptial or marital agreements

  • Significance: Note 5 presents these agreements as a frequent setting for duress and undue influence claims (Bonds, Ellis, the Uniform Act)

  • Distinction: Often governed by special statutory requirements (counsel, waiver notices, financial disclosure) beyond common-law duress

  • Case: None (general terminology)
