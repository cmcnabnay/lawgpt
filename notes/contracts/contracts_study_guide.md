# UNIT I — Introduction & Mutual Assent (Classes 1–5)

## Class 1 — What Is a Contract?

### Reading: Introduction (CB 1–34)
**Rule: Contract defined (R2 § 1).** A contract is a promise or set of promises for whose breach the law gives a remedy, or whose performance the law recognizes as a duty.

**Rule: Requirement of a bargain (R2 § 17(1)).** Formation requires a bargain in which there is (1) a manifestation of mutual assent to the exchange and (2) consideration.

**Rule: The objective theory.** Assent is judged by what the parties said and did, read the way a reasonable person would. Secret intentions don't count.

**Case: Ray v. William G. Eurice & Bros.** (Md. 1952)
- **Facts:** The Rays and a builder signed a contract that incorporated the Rays' own detailed specifications. The builder later claimed it thought it was signing on its own, cheaper specs, and refused to build.
- **Issue:** Is a party bound by a signed contract when it was mistaken about which specifications the writing incorporated?
- **Holding:** A valid contract existed. A party who has the capacity to understand a writing, reads it, and signs it is bound by the signature, absent fraud, duress, or mutual mistake. The builder's unilateral mistake about what it was signing did not prevent formation.
- **Takeaway:** A party who reads and signs a writing is bound by its terms, and a unilateral mistake about what was signed doesn't prevent formation.

### Reading: R2 §§ 1, 2, 4, 17(1), 20, 153
**Rule: Promise (R2 § 2).** A promise is a manifestation of intent to act or refrain, made so the promisee is justified in understanding a commitment has been made. The promisor makes it. The promisee receives it. A beneficiary is a third party who benefits from it.

**Rule: How a promise is made (R2 § 4).** A promise may be made in words (oral or written) or inferred from conduct.

**Rule: Effect of misunderstanding (R2 § 20).** There is no contract if the parties attach materially different meanings to their manifestations and neither knows or has reason to know the other's meaning. If one party knows or has reason to know the other's meaning, a contract forms on the innocent party's meaning.

**Rule: Unilateral mistake (R2 § 153).** A mistake by one party about a basic assumption that has a material effect on the exchange makes the contract voidable by that party if (a) enforcement would be unconscionable, or (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it. The mistaken party also must not bear the risk of the mistake.

**Compare § 20 with § 153:** § 20 asks whether a contract was ever formed (both sides innocently meant different things). § 153 assumes a contract was formed and asks whether one side can get out of it.

## Class 2 — The Objective Theory Applied

### Reading: *Lucy v. Zehmer* (Va. 1954)

**Rule: Objective theory of assent.** "The law imputes to a person an intention corresponding to the reasonable meaning of his words and acts." An undisclosed intent to joke is irrelevant if the words and conduct would lead a reasonable person to believe the deal was real.

**Rule: Intoxication.** Intoxication voids a contract only if a party was so drunk they could not understand the nature and consequences of the transaction.

**Rule: Jest.** A person can't say they were joking if their words and conduct would make a reasonable person believe they meant it. Even a secret joke doesn't help when the other side reasonably took it seriously.

**Case: Lucy v. Zehmer** (Va. 1954)
- **Facts:** Over drinks in Zehmer's restaurant, Lucy offered $50,000 for the Ferguson farm. Zehmer wrote "We hereby agree to sell to W. O. Lucy the Ferguson Farm complete for $50,000.00, title satisfactory to buyer." Zehmer and his wife both signed. Zehmer later said it was all a joke and that he was "high as a Georgia pine."
- **Issue:** Is a party bound by a signed agreement to sell land when he claims he was only joking and had been drinking?
- **Holding:** There was a binding contract. The court ordered specific performance. The court relied on about 40 minutes of discussion, the writing was redrafted (first "I agree," then "We agree" so the wife would sign), Zehmer insisted his wife sign, they discussed title, and Lucy treated it as serious (he hired a lawyer and arranged financing). Zehmer wasn't so drunk that he couldn't understand the transaction.
- **Takeaway:** Assent is judged objectively, so a party whose words and acts reasonably appear serious is bound, whatever their secret intent.

## Class 3 — Offer, Acceptance, and Timing

### Reading: Timing Issues: Offer & Acceptance (CB 35–55)

**Rule: Offer (R2 § 24).** A manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain, made so as to justify another person in understanding that their assent is invited and will conclude it.

**Rule: Preliminary negotiations (R2 § 26).** No offer exists if the addressee knows or has reason to know that the speaker doesn't intend to be bound until a further manifestation of assent. Ads, price quotes, catalogs, and "I'd consider selling for…" are usually invitations to deal.

**Case: Lonergan v. Scolnick** (Cal. Ct. App. 1954)
- **Facts:** Scolnick advertised land. Lonergan asked about it. Scolnick replied with a description and a "rock-bottom" price of $2,500, said Lonergan should "act fast" because another buyer was interested, and told him to have an escrow opened. Before Lonergan's acceptance arrived, Scolnick sold to someone else.
- **Issue:** Was a seller's letter quoting a price and urging the buyer to "act fast" an offer, or only preliminary negotiation?
- **Holding:** No contract. Read as a whole, the correspondence was preliminary negotiation. It was not an offer. The "act fast" language signaled that Scolnick meant to sell to the first buyer who closed, so he was reserving the final word (R2 § 26).
- **Takeaway:** A communication is not an offer if a reasonable person would understand that the sender reserved the final word, as with first-come, first-served sales language.

**Rule: Termination of the power of acceptance (R2 § 36).** It ends by (a) rejection or counteroffer, (b) lapse of time, (c) revocation, or (d) the death or incapacity of either party. It also ends if a condition stated in the offer fails.

**Rule: Other termination and acceptance rules.**
- **Lapse (§ 41):** at the time the offer specifies, otherwise at the end of a reasonable time.
- **Counteroffer (§ 39):** It rejects the original offer and proposes a substitute bargain. A mere inquiry ("Would you take less?") is not a counteroffer.
- **Indirect revocation (§ 43):** The power ends when the offeror takes definite action inconsistent with the offer (such as selling to someone else) and the offeree learns of it from a reliable source.
- **Mailbox rule (§ 63):** An acceptance is effective on dispatch. Offers, revocations, and rejections are effective on receipt. The offeror can override this by requiring receipt.
- **Mirror image rule (§§ 58–59):** At common law, an acceptance must match the offer exactly. A reply that adds conditions is a counteroffer. UCC § 2-207 changes this for goods (Class 5).

**Case: Normile v. Miller** (N.C. 1985)
- **Facts:** Normile offered to buy Miller's house. Miller's agent changed the terms (a counteroffer) with a deadline. Normile didn't accept. The agent then told Normile the property was sold to Segal, and Normile tried to accept anyway.
- **Issue:** Could the buyers accept a counteroffer after learning from the seller's agent that the property had been sold to someone else?
- **Holding:** No contract. (1) Normile's own offer was rejected by Miller's counteroffer. (2) The counteroffer was revoked before acceptance, indirectly, when Normile learned from a reliable source (the agent) that the house had been sold. The deadline in the counteroffer did not create an option, because Normile gave no consideration to hold it open.
- **Takeaway:** A counteroffer rejects the original offer, and an offeror can revoke and offer wthout telling the offeree

## Class 4 — Offer & Acceptance Continued: Unilateral Contracts and Ads

### Reading: CB 55–67 (Cook; Sateriale)

**Rule: Option contract (R2 § 25).** A promise that meets the requirements for forming a contract and limits the promisor's power to revoke an offer.

**Rule: Option created by part performance (R2 § 45).** When an offer invites acceptance only by performance, an option contract is created once the offeree begins the invited performance (or tenders it). The offeror's duty is conditional on completion. Mere preparation to perform doesn't count. Starting the performance itself does.

**Rule: Unilateral Contract:** A contract that is accepted when the requested performance is rendered, and the offeror cannot revoke the offer once the offeree has begun performance
- **Example:** Dog owner offers to pay $100 to whoever finds his missing dog

**Rule: Bilateral Contract:** A contract where both parties make mutual promises to each other. The contract forms when a deal has been made, neither side has done anything at the time of the deal.
- **Example:** Employment agreement, employer offers to pay a salary and the worker starts on Monday

**Case: Cook v. Coldwell Banker / Frank Laiben Realty** (Mo. Ct. App. 1997)
- **Facts:** The broker announced a year-end bonus program for agents who hit commission targets, then added a requirement that agents still be employed in March of the next year to collect. Cook earned the bonus, then left for another firm. She was refused payment.
- **Issue:** Can an employer change the terms of a bonus offer after the employee has substantially performed?
- **Holding:** This was a unilateral contract offer. Once Cook substantially performed, the offer became irrevocable and the employer could not change the terms. She recovered the bonus.
- **Takeaway:** Once the offeree substantially begins the performance a unilateral offer invites, the offer becomes irrevocable (R2 § 45).

**Rule: Advertisements as offers.** An advertisement or promotion is usually an invitation to deal. It is an offer when it is clear, definite, and explicit and leaves nothing open for negotiation, as with reward offers.

**Case: Sateriale v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco** (9th Cir. 2012)
- **Facts:** RJR's "Camel Cash" program let smokers collect certificates and redeem them for merchandise. After years of honoring redemptions, RJR ended the program, leaving smokers holding certificates they could no longer redeem.
- **Issue:** Can a customer-rewards promotion be an offer for a unilateral contract?
- **Holding:** The program could be an offer for a unilateral contract (the plaintiffs stated a claim). Unlike ordinary ads, it invited a specific performance (buy, save, redeem) and promised a specific reward.
- **Takeaway:** A promotion can be an offer for a unilateral contract when it is clear, definite, and explicit and leaves nothing open for negotiation.

### Reading: *Izadi v. Machado (Gus) Ford* (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1989)

**Rule: Advertisements and fine print.** An ad can be an offer when it's clear and definite (citing *Lefkowitz*). A dealer can't use fine print designed to be overlooked to take back what the large print offers. The ad was read as an objectively reasonable consumer would read it, in favor of the consumer.

**Case: Izadi v. Machado Ford** (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1989)
- **Facts:** A newspaper ad for a Ford Ranger prominently showed $3,595 alongside a "$3,000 minimum trade-in" for any trade-in vehicle. The fine print limited the trade-in to two specific older models. Izadi showed up with a trade-in and the cash. The dealer refused.
- **Issue:** Can a newspaper ad be an offer, and can the advertiser rely on fine print to avoid the terms the ad's large print conveys?
- **Holding:** The breach-of-contract claim (and the deceptive-practices claim) survived dismissal. The fraud claim was dismissed. Even if the ad was a "bait and switch," the dealer is bound by the offer it actually made.
- **Takeaway:** An advertisement can be a binding offer, and an advertiser can't use confusing fine print in a bait-and-switch to escape the offer its ad reasonably conveys.

## Class 5 — Other Mutual Assent Issues: The UCC and Indefiniteness

### Reading: UCC §§ 2-204(1), 2-207(3)
**Rule: UCC § 2-204.** A contract for sale of goods may be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, including conduct by both parties that recognizes a contract.
- (1) Agreement may be found even if the moment of its making is undetermined, agreement could be shown through words, writings, or conducts
- (2) A valid contract may exist even if the parties cannot determine when it was made
- (3) Open terms does not invalidate a contract

**Rule: UCC § 2-207 (the "battle of the forms").**
- (1) A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even if it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is expressly conditional on assent to those terms.
- (2) Between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract unless (a) the offer limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they materially alter it, or (c) the offeror objects.
- (3) If the writings don't form a contract but the parties' conduct recognizes one, the contract consists of the terms on which the writings agree, plus the UCC's gap-filler provisions.

**Case: Harlow & Jones v. Advance Steel** (E.D. Mich. 1976)
- **Facts:** Advance (buyer) and Harlow (seller) agreed by phone through a broker on about 1,000 tons of imported steel. Harlow's sales confirmation and Advance's purchase order differed on delivery terms. The steel was shipped in installments. Advance rejected a later shipment as late.
- **Issue:** Was a contract formed when the parties' forms conflicted, and if so, what supplied the delivery terms?
- **Holding:** A contract existed on the parties' agreement and conduct. Where the forms conflicted, the UCC gap fillers supplied the terms. Under § 2-504, the seller in a shipment contract performs by putting the goods with a carrier, so the shipment was timely. Advance's rejection was wrongful.
- **Takeaway:** Under UCC Article 2 a contract can be formed by conduct even when the forms don't match, and the Code's gap fillers supply the missing or conflicting terms.

### Reading: Indefiniteness (CB 68–85)

**Rule: Gap-filling under the UCC.** the UCC (§ 2-204(3), § 2-305 open price term) fills gaps freely when the parties intended to contract. *Walker* reflects the stricter common-law view. Modern courts and R2 § 33 lean toward enforcement.

**Case: Walker v. Keith** (Ky. 1964)
- **Facts:** A 10-year lease gave the tenant an option to renew for another 10 years, at a rent "to be agreed upon by the parties… in the light of subsequent competitive business conditions."
- **Issue:** Is a lease renewal option enforceable when the rent is "to be agreed upon" by the parties?
- **Holding:** The renewal option was too indefinite to enforce. The court would not set the rent itself. An "agreement to agree" on a material term is unenforceable unless the contract supplies a mechanism or objective standard (a formula, an index, or an arbitrator) to fix it.
- **Takeaway:** An agreement to agree on a material term like rent is too indefinite to enforce unless the contract supplies an objective standard or mechanism to fix it.

# UNIT II — Consideration (Classes 6–8)

**Rule: Consideration (R2 §§ 71, 79).** A performance or return promise is consideration if it is bargained for, meaning it is sought by the promisor in exchange for the promise and given by the promisee in exchange for that promise. Under the bargain theory, the question is "was it sought in exchange?" not "was it valuable?" The older test asks for a benefit to the promisor or a legal detriment to the promisee.

## Class 6 — Bargain, Detriment, and Gifts

### Reading: CB 87–93, 102–107, 215–216 (Hamer; Kirksey; Dougherty)

**Rule: Benefit or detriment.** Consideration can be a benefit to the promisor or a detriment to the promisee. Courts don't ask whether the detriment was actually harmful.

**Case: Hamer v. Sidway** (N.Y. 1891)
- **Facts:** An uncle promised his nephew $5,000 if the nephew refrained from drinking, using tobacco, swearing, and gambling until age 21. The nephew complied.
- **Issue:** Is refraining from drinking, smoking, swearing, and gambling consideration for a promise of money?
- **Holding:** Consideration existed. Giving up a legal right to do something is a legal detriment, whether or not it benefited the uncle, and even if it was good for the nephew.
- **Takeaway:** Giving up a legal right is a legal detriment that counts as consideration, even if it benefits the promisee.

**Case: Kirksey v. Kirksey** (Ala. 1845)
- **Facts:** A man wrote to his widowed sister-in-law: "If you will come down and see me, I will let you have a place to raise your family." She gave up her land and moved about 60 miles. He later evicted her.
- **Issue:** Was the sister-in-law's move consideration for the promise of a place to live, or only a condition of a gift?
- **Holding:** No consideration. This was a gratuitous promise. Moving was a condition she had to meet to receive the gift. It wasn't the price of the promise.
- **Takeaway:** Meeting a condition of a gift is not consideration, so a gratuitous promise is unenforceable.

**Rule: False recitals.** A false recital of consideration doesn't satisfy the requirement (R2 § 71 cmt. b and § 87 cmt. b; compare option contracts, Class 13).

**Case: Dougherty v. Salt** (N.Y. 1919)
- **Facts:** An aunt gave her 8-year-old nephew a $3,000 promissory note "for value received." It was a gift motivated by affection.
- **Issue:** Is a promissory note given as a gift enforceable because it recites "value received"?
- **Holding:** Unenforceable. A promise to make a gift is not enforceable. The recital "value received" doesn't create consideration where none existed.
- **Takeaway:** A promise to make a gift is unenforceable, and a false recital of consideration doesn't change that.

## Class 7 — Consideration: Conditions of Gifts and Adequacy

### Reading: CB 108–116 (Plowman) 

**Rule: Bargained-for exchange.** A performance is consideration only if the promisor sought it in exchange for the promise. Past service, and acts that are only conditions of a gift, are not consideration.

**Case: Plowman v. Indian Refining Co.** (E.D. Ill. 1937)
- **Facts:** A company promised older employees half pay for life after a layoff. Recipients had to come to the office to collect the checks. The company stopped paying.
- **Issue:** Was there consideration for the company's promise of lifetime half pay to laid-off employees?
- **Holding:** No consideration. (1) Past service isn't consideration for a later promise. (2) Picking up the checks was a condition of a gift. The company didn't bargain for it. (3) Giving up any right to sue wasn't bargained for either. (The court also held that the officers who made the promise lacked authority.)
- **Takeaway:** Past service and conditions of a gift aren't consideration because the promisor didn't bargain for them.

### Reading: *Dohrmann v. Swaney* (Ill. App. Ct. 2014)

**Rule: Adequacy of consideration.** Courts usually don't weigh the adequacy of consideration, but they may refuse enforcement when consideration is so grossly inadequate that it shocks the conscience, especially when combined with unfairness in how the deal was made.

**Case: Dohrmann v. Swaney** (Ill. App. Ct. 2014)
- **Facts:** An 89-year-old woman (Rogers) signed an agreement to leave a young friend, Dohrmann, her Manhattan apartment and $4 million in exchange for his promise to change his children's names to include the Rogers name. She didn't consult her lawyer, and the agreement wasn't reflected in her estate plan. Her estate refused to perform.
- **Issue:** Will a court enforce a promise of an apartment and $4 million in exchange for renaming the promisee's children?
- **Holding:** Unenforceable. Courts usually don't weigh adequacy of consideration, but they will refuse enforcement when consideration is so grossly inadequate that it shocks the conscience, especially when combined with unfairness in how the deal was made (her age, no independent advice, the relationship of trust).
- **Takeaway:** Courts don't weigh adequacy of consideration, but they may refuse enforcement when consideration is grossly inadequate and the deal was made under unfair circumstances.

## Class 8 — Adequacy of Consideration

### Reading: *Batsakis v. Demotsis* (Tex. Civ. App. 1949)

**Rule: No equivalence required (R2 § 79).** R2 § 79: if there is a bargain, there is no further requirement of a benefit, a detriment, or equivalence in value.

**Rule: Want vs. failure of consideration.** *Want* means no consideration was ever given, so no contract formed. *Failure* means the promised consideration was never delivered.

**Case: Batsakis v. Demotsis** (Tex. Civ. App. 1949)
- **Facts:** On April 2, 1942, in Greece during WWII, Batsakis lent Demotsis 500,000 drachmae (then worth about $25). She signed an instrument promising to repay $2,000 in U.S. money with 8% interest. When sued, she pleaded want of consideration: she had received only about $25 in value. The trial court awarded Batsakis only $750 principal plus interest, apparently the value it placed on what he gave her. Batsakis appealed.
- **Issue:** Can a borrower avoid a promise to repay $2,000 because the drachmae she received were worth only about $25?
- **Holding:** Judgment reformed to the full $2,000 plus 8% interest from April 2, 1942, and affirmed as reformed. The transaction was a sale of the drachmae in exchange for her signing the instrument. The drachmae had some value, so the plea of want of consideration failed. That plea means the instrument never became a valid obligation in the first place. "Mere inadequacy of consideration will not void a contract." Failure of consideration failed too: by her own testimony, she "got exactly what she contracted for."
- **Takeaway:** If there is a bargain, courts won't inquire into the adequacy or equivalence of the consideration (R2 § 79).

# UNIT III — Promissory Estoppel (Classes 9–13)

**Rule: Promissory estoppel (R2 § 90(1)).** A promise is binding if:
1. the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce action or forbearance by the promisee or a third person,
2. it does induce such action or forbearance, and
3. injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.
- The remedy "may be limited as justice requires." Courts can award reliance damages instead of expectation.
- **§ 90(2):** A charitable subscription or a marriage settlement is binding without proof of reliance.

**Common four-element version** (used in *Aceves*, *Pop's*): (1) a clear and definite promise; (2) the promisor's reasonable expectation of reliance; (3) actual, reasonable reliance; (4) detriment of a definite and substantial nature.

## Class 9 — Promissory Estoppel: Family Promises

### Reading: *Wright v. Newman* (Ga. 1996)

**Rule: Reliance by third persons (R2 § 90).** Promissory estoppel protects reasonable reliance by the promisee or by a third person, and the remedy may be limited as justice requires.

**Case: Wright v. Newman** (Ga. 1996)
- **Facts:** Newman wasn't the biological father of Wright's son. He agreed to be listed as the father on the birth certificate, gave the child his surname, and held himself out as the father for years. Wright alleged she relied by not trying to find the biological father. Newman stopped paying support. Newman was the promisor, Wright was the promisee, the son was the third person.
- **Issue:** Can a man who held himself out as a child's father be required to pay child support through promissory estoppel?
- **Holding:** Newman could be held to a child-support obligation through promissory estoppel (Georgia's version of § 90). By voluntarily assuming the role of father, he made a promise he should have expected to induce reliance by the child (a third person, per § 90) and the mother. Enforcement was needed to avoid injustice.
- **Takeaway:** Promissory estoppel can enforce a promise relied on by a third person, here a man's assumption of a father's role creating a child-support duty.

### Reading: *Greiner v. Greiner* (Kan. 1930)

**Rule: Family promises under § 90.** A gratuitous promise is enforceable when the promisor should reasonably expect it to induce substantial action, it does induce that action, and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

**Case: Greiner v. Greiner** (Kan. 1930)
- **Facts:** A mother promised her son Frank 80 acres if he moved back with his family. He moved, gave up his home and job, and made improvements on the land. She then refused to deed it.
- **Issue:** Is a mother's promise of land to her son enforceable when he moved and improved the land in reliance on it?
- **Holding:** Enforced under Restatement (First) § 90. The promise was one she should reasonably have expected to induce substantial action, it did, and injustice could be avoided only by enforcement.
- **Takeaway:** Promissory estoppel enforces a family promise of land when the promisee reasonably and substantially relies on it and injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

### Reading: CB 216–225 (Harvey)

**Rule: General promises under § 90.** A specific promise isn't required. A general promise, combined with conduct and the surrounding circumstances, can be a promise for § 90 purposes, and the reasonableness of reliance is usually a fact question.

**Rule: Equitable vs. promissory estoppel.** Equitable estoppel stops someone from denying a misrepresentation of existing fact. Promissory estoppel enforces a promise about the future.

**Case: Harvey v. Dow** (Me. 2008)
- **Facts:** Parents told their daughter they'd give her land. She built a house on it, spending substantial money with her parents' knowledge and help. When the relationship broke down, they refused to deed her the land.
- **Issue:** Must a promise be specific to support promissory estoppel, or can a general promise to give land be enough?
- **Holding:** The trial court erred in requiring a specific promise. A general promise to give land, combined with conduct and the surrounding circumstances (her parents helped plan and build the house), can be a promise for § 90 purposes. The court remanded.
- **Takeaway:** A general promise, combined with conduct and the circumstances, can support promissory estoppel without a specific promise.

## Class 10 — Promissory Estoppel: Commercial and Charitable Promises

### Reading: CB 225–243 (King; Katz)

**Rule: Reliance by giving up a job.** Reliance under § 90 doesn't require spending money. Retiring or giving up a job in reliance on a promise is enough.

**Case: Katz v. Danny Dare, Inc.** (Mo. Ct. App. 1980)
- **Facts:** After 25 years, an injured employee (Katz) was urged by the company's president to retire, in exchange for a pension of $13,000 a year for life. He retired in reliance. The company stopped paying after a few years.
- **Issue:** Is an employer's promise of a lifetime pension enforceable when the employee retired in reliance on it?
- **Holding:** Enforceable under promissory estoppel. Retirement was a detrimental change in position that the employer expected and intended. It didn't matter that Katz's job was uncertain, or that he was induced to give up a job rather than a legal right.
- **Takeaway:** An employee who retires in reasonable reliance on an employer's pension promise can enforce it through promissory estoppel.

**Rule: Charitable subscriptions (R2 § 90(2)).** In many jurisdictions charitable subscriptions are binding without reliance.

**Case: King v. Trustees of Boston University** (Mass. 1995)
- **Facts:** In 1964, Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. wrote BU a letter saying he was depositing his papers there and that "in the event of my death, all such materials deposited with the University shall become from that date the absolute property" of BU. Coretta Scott King later sued to recover the papers.
- **Issue:** Was Dr. King's letter an enforceable promise to give his papers to Boston University?
- **Holding:** The jury reasonably found an enforceable charitable pledge. The letter was a promise to make a gift, not just a statement of intent. It was supported by consideration (BU's promise to care for the papers, which it did) or by reliance.
- **Takeaway:** A charitable pledge is enforceable when it is a promise rather than a statement of intention and is supported by consideration or reliance.

## Class 11 — Promissory Estoppel: Commercial Promises and Promissory Fraud

### Reading: CB 243–253 (Aceves)

**Rule: Promise to negotiate.** A promise to negotiate can be a clear and unambiguous promise for promissory estoppel, even though the final deal was never certain. Damages are measured by what was lost through the reliance.

**Case: Aceves v. U.S. Bank, N.A.** (Cal. Ct. App. 2011)
- **Facts:** Aceves filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy. She planned to convert to Chapter 13, which would have let her keep her house. The bank promised to work with her on a loan modification if she didn't. She gave up the Chapter 13 option. The bank never negotiated and foreclosed.
- **Issue:** Can a bank's promise to work with a borrower on a loan modification support a promissory estoppel claim?
- **Holding:** She stated a claim for promissory estoppel (and for promissory fraud). The promise was clear and unambiguous: a promise to negotiate a modification, not a promise to make one. Her reliance (giving up Chapter 13) was reasonable and foreseeable. Her detriment was real. She lost her chance to save her home through Chapter 13.
- **Takeaway:** A promise to negotiate can be enforced through promissory estoppel, with damages measured by the reliance loss.

## Class 12 — Firm Offers through Reliance: Construction Bids

### Reading: CB 262–272 (Baird; Drennan)

**Rule: Offers for an exchange.** Promissory estoppel doesn't apply to an offer made for an exchange, because the offeror is bargaining rather than promising a gift. An offeree who wants protection should bargain for an option.

**Case: James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros.** (2d Cir. 1933)
- **Facts:** Gimbel sent general contractors a bid for linoleum on a public building. The bid had a big mistake (about half the right quantity). Baird used it in its general bid. Gimbel revoked before Baird accepted. Baird then won the job.
- **Issue:** Does promissory estoppel make a subcontractor's bid irrevocable once the general contractor relies on it?
- **Holding:** No contract. Gimbel's bid was an offer for an exchange (a bilateral contract) that Baird hadn't accepted. Promissory estoppel doesn't apply to offers made for an exchange, because the offeror is bargaining, not making a promise to give.
- **Takeaway:** Promissory estoppel doesn't make an offer for a bargained-for exchange irrevocable, so a sub's bid may be revoked before acceptance.

**Rule: Reliance option (R2 § 87(2)).** An offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce substantial action or forbearance before acceptance, and that does so, is binding as an option to the extent necessary to avoid injustice. This codifies *Drennan*. § 45 (Class 4) protects an offeree who begins performance. § 87(2) protects one who relies before accepting.

**Rule: Limits on reliance on bids.** The general contractor can't reopen bargaining ("bid shopping") and still hold the sub to its bid. Reliance must be reasonable: a bid so low it's obviously mistaken can't be relied on.

**Case: Drennan v. Star Paving Co.** (Cal. 1958)
- **Facts:** Star Paving phoned in a $7,131 paving bid. Drennan used it in his general bid, which won. Star then said its bid was a mistake and refused to perform. Drennan hired another paver for $10,948.
- **Issue:** Can a subcontractor revoke its bid after the general contractor relied on it in winning the job?
- **Holding:** Drennan recovers the difference. Under § 90, Star's offer contained a subsidiary promise not to revoke for a reasonable time once its bid was used. Star should have expected Drennan to rely, because subcontractors know general contractors use their bids. Drennan's reliance was reasonable. Drennan had no reason to know of Star's error. The loss falls on the party who made the mistake.
- **Takeaway:** A subcontractor's bid carries an implied promise not to revoke once the general contractor reasonably relies on it in making its own bid (R2 § 87(2)).

## Class 13 — Options and Promissory Estoppel in Negotiations

### Reading: CB 255–262 and 272–282 (Berryman; Pop's)

**Rule: Option contract (R2 § 87(1)).** An offer is binding as an option if it's in writing, signed by the offeror, recites a purported consideration, and proposes a fair exchange within a reasonable time. A statute can also make an offer irrevocable (UCC § 2-205 firm offer by a merchant).

**Case: Berryman v. Kmoch** (Kan. 1977)
- **Facts:** Kmoch signed an option giving Berryman 120 days to buy farmland. It recited "$10 and other valuable consideration," but the $10 was never paid. Berryman spent effort lining up financing and buyers. Kmoch sold to someone else, and Berryman learned of it.
- **Issue:** Is a written option enforceable when the recited $10 consideration was never paid?
- **Holding:** No enforceable option. (1) Because the recited $10 was never paid, there was no consideration, so the "option" was only a revocable offer. (2) Berryman's own efforts weren't bargained for. (3) No promissory estoppel, because Kmoch didn't expect or induce Berryman's actions in reliance on the offer staying open. (4) The offer was revoked when Kmoch sold the land and Berryman learned of the sale (indirect revocation). The Restatement would enforce a signed written option that recites consideration, even if it wasn't paid. *Berryman* rejects that approach.
- **Takeaway:** An option whose recited nominal consideration was never paid is only a revocable offer.

**Rule: Definiteness in negotiations (New Jersey).** The degree of definiteness required for a promise under promissory estoppel depends on the circumstances, and courts are less strict when the claimant seeks only reliance damages.

**Case: Pop's Cones, Inc. v. Resorts International Hotel** (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 1998)
- **Facts:** Pop's, a TCBY franchise, was negotiating to move into Resorts' Atlantic City space. Resorts' executive told Pop's it was "95% there" and to give up its existing lease. Pop's did so and moved its equipment into storage. Resorts then withdrew its offer.
- **Issue:** Can a party recover through promissory estoppel for reliance on assurances made during lease negotiations?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment for Resorts reversed. Pop's could recover under promissory estoppel. New Jersey relaxed the requirement of a "clear and definite promise." The degree of definiteness required depends on the circumstances. Courts are less strict when the claimant seeks only reliance damages. Pop's was limited to reliance damages (the costs of giving up its lease and moving), not the profits of a lease it never had.
- **Takeaway:** Promissory estoppel can compensate reliance on promises made during negotiations, before any contract is formed.

# UNIT IV — Electronic Contracting (Class 14)

## Class 14 — Electronic Contracting

### Reading: CB 181–208 (Long; DeFontes)
**Rule: Online assent.** The same mutual-assent rules apply online. The question is whether the user had reasonable notice of the terms and manifested assent to them.

| Format | How assent is shown | Usual result |
|---|---|---|
| Clickwrap | User clicks "I agree" after the terms are presented | Generally enforced |
| Browsewrap | Terms sit behind a hyperlink. Assent is inferred from continued use of the site | Enforced only with actual or constructive notice (a conspicuous link and clear notice) |
| Shrinkwrap / "rolling" contract | Terms come in the box. The buyer accepts by keeping the product past a return period | Split. *ProCD* and *Hill* (7th Cir.) enforce. Others treat the terms as § 2-207 proposals |
| Sign-in-wrap | Notice near a sign-up button that says using the site means agreeing to the terms | Depends on how conspicuous the notice is |

**Rule: Notice of online terms.** *Specht v. Netscape* (clicking "download" doesn't show assent to terms the user can't see) and *Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble* (the proximity and conspicuousness of the hyperlink alone aren't enough without notice that use equals assent).

**Rule: Burden of proving an agreement to arbitrate.** The burden is on the party seeking arbitration to show an agreement. The FAA's pro-arbitration policy doesn't apply to the question of whether an agreement was formed.

**Case: Long v. Provide Commerce, Inc.** (Cal. Ct. App. 2016)
- **Facts:** Long bought flowers through ProFlowers' website and later sued. Provide moved to compel arbitration based on its Terms of Use, which were reachable only through hyperlinks on the checkout pages.
- **Issue:** Did a website user assent to Terms of Use that were reachable only through hyperlinks on the checkout pages?
- **Holding:** No assent; arbitration denied. The site was a browsewrap. The links were not conspicuous enough (small text, not near the buttons the user clicked, and no notice that buying meant agreeing). Long had no actual or constructive notice.
- **Takeaway:** Browsewrap terms bind a user only if the user had actual or constructive notice of them.

**Rule: Rolling contracts.** Under the UCC a contract may form in any manner sufficient to show agreement (§ 2-204) and may be accepted in any reasonable manner (§ 2-206), but the offeree must still have notice of the terms and of how to reject them.

**Case: DeFontes v. Dell, Inc.** (R.I. 2009)
- **Facts:** Dell shipped computers with "Terms and Conditions" (including an arbitration clause) inside the box. Dell argued that customers accepted them by keeping the computers.
- **Issue:** Did buyers accept the terms shipped inside the box by keeping their computers?
- **Holding:** No agreement to arbitrate. Even under a "rolling contract" (*ProCD* / *Hill*) approach, the seller must give reasonable notice that the buyer can reject the terms by returning the product. Dell's materials did not make that clear. Its return policy was even ambiguous about whether returns were allowed.
- **Takeaway:** Shrinkwrap or rolling-contract terms bind a buyer only if the seller gave reasonable notice of the terms and of the right to reject them by returning the product.

# UNIT V — Restitution (Classes 15–16)

## Class 15 — Restitution in the Absence of Agreement

### Reading: CB 286–316 (Commerce; Pelo)
**Rule: Unjust enrichment (Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 1).** A person who is unjustly enriched at another's expense must make restitution. Elements:
1. The plaintiff conferred a benefit on the defendant.
2. The defendant knew of and accepted or retained the benefit.
3. Under the circumstances, it would be inequitable for the defendant to keep it without paying its value.

Not a contract. Restitution, "quasi-contract," and "implied-in-law" contracts are all remedies created by law. Compare an implied-in-fact contract, which is a real contract inferred from conduct.

**Case: Commerce Partnership 8098 v. Equity Contracting Co.** (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1997)
- **Facts:** A subcontractor (Equity) did work on a building and wasn't paid by the general contractor. It sued the owner directly for unjust enrichment.
- **Issue:** Is a property owner unjustly enriched by a subcontractor's work when the general contractor didn't pay the subcontractor?
- **Holding:** Reversed and remanded. An owner isn't unjustly enriched just because the sub wasn't paid. The sub has to show the owner hasn't paid anyone for the work, meaning the owner got the benefit free. If the owner paid the general contractor, it gave value and isn't enriched unjustly. The sub's remedy is against the general contractor (and any mechanic's lien).
- **Takeaway:** An owner who paid the general contractor isn't unjustly enriched by an unpaid subcontractor's work.

**Rule: Limits on restitution.**
- Volunteers (officious intermeddlers) don't recover. You can't force a benefit on someone and send a bill.
- Emergencies are an exception. A person who provides emergency services to protect someone's life or health (R3 Restitution § 20), or their property (§ 21), recovers, if the services were intended to be paid for.

**Case: Credit Bureau Enterprises v. Pelo** (Iowa 2000)
- **Facts:** Pelo was involuntarily committed to a hospital after a suicide threat. He refused to sign financial-responsibility forms. The hospital sued for the cost of his care.
- **Issue:** Must an involuntarily committed patient pay for hospital care he never agreed to?
- **Holding:** Pelo was liable on an implied-in-law contract for the reasonable value of the services. He received necessary emergency medical care, and it would be unjust for him to keep the benefit without paying. His lack of consent didn't matter because the duty is imposed by law.
- **Takeaway:** A person who receives necessary emergency services owes their reasonable value under an implied-in-law contract, even without consent.

**Case (note): Watts v. Watts** (Wis. 1987). An unmarried cohabitant can bring an unjust enrichment claim for contributions to the property the couple accumulated. Posner's view: restitution works where transaction costs made bargaining impossible (emergencies), but not where the plaintiff could have bargained first.

## Class 16 — Promissory Restitution

### Reading: CB 316–329 (Mills; Webb)
**Rule: Past consideration is no consideration.** A promise made because of a benefit already received wasn't bargained for.

**Case: Mills v. Wyman** (Mass. 1825)
- **Facts:** Mills cared for Levi Wyman, the 25-year-old son of the defendant, who fell ill returning from sea. The father later promised to pay Mills's expenses, then refused.
- **Issue:** Is a father's promise to pay for care already given to his adult son enforceable?
- **Holding:** Unenforceable. The father got no benefit (the son was an adult), and a moral obligation alone is not consideration. The promise was made after the services, so they weren't bargained for. The court noted the father's conduct was morally wrong, but ruled the law could not enforce it.
- **Takeaway:** A moral obligation alone is not consideration for a promise made after the benefit was received.

**Rule: Material benefit rule (R2 § 86).** A promise made in recognition of a benefit previously received by the promisor is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice. It is not binding if (a) the promisee conferred the benefit as a gift or the promisor otherwise isn't unjustly enriched, or (b) the promised value is disproportionate to the benefit.

**Case: Webb v. McGowin** (Ala. Ct. App. 1935)
- **Facts:** Webb, a mill worker, was dropping a 75-pound pine block when he saw McGowin below. He diverted it, saving McGowin, but was permanently crippled. McGowin then promised to pay Webb $15 every two weeks for life. He paid until his death. His estate stopped.
- **Issue:** Is a promise to pay a man who was crippled saving the promisor's life enforceable?
- **Holding:** Enforceable. McGowin received a material benefit (his life and health), and his later promise affirmed that benefit. That makes a moral obligation sufficient consideration.
- **Takeaway:** A promise made in recognition of a material benefit already received is enforceable (R2 § 86).

# UNIT VI — Statute of Frauds (Classes 17–18)

**Framework:**
1. Is the contract within the Statute (one of the covered categories)?
2. If so, is there a sufficient writing, signed by the party to be charged?
3. If not, does an exception apply (part performance, § 139 reliance, UCC § 2-201(2)–(3))?
4. If not, is restitution still available for benefits conferred?

## Class 17 — The Statute of Frauds

### Reading: CB 335–371 (Crabtree; Beaver; Rice)
**Rule: Covered categories (R2 § 110). Mnemonic "MY LEGS":**
- **M**arriage: a promise made in consideration of marriage (prenups), not mutual promises to marry. Construed narrowly.
- **Y**ear: a contract that cannot be performed within one year of its making.
- **L**and: a contract for the sale of an interest in land.
- **E**xecutor or administrator: a promise to pay the estate's debts personally.
- **G**oods: sale of goods for $500 or more (UCC § 2-201; Class 18).
- **S**uretyship: a promise to answer for the debt of another.

**Rule: One-year provision (R2 § 130).**
- It's about possibility, not probability. A contract is within the statute only if, by its terms, it cannot possibly be performed within a year (R2 § 130). "Build a bridge" is outside, even if it will take three years.
- Lifetime employment is outside the statute in most courts, because the employee could die within a year, which fully performs the contract.
- But the possibility of termination or excuse within a year doesn't take a contract outside. Only possible full performance does. A two-year contract is within.

**Rule: Suretyship (R2 §§ 112, 116).** Covers a promise made to the creditor to pay another's debt. The main purpose / leading object exception: if the promisor's main purpose is to benefit itself, the promise is outside the statute.

**Rule: Sufficient writing (R2 §§ 131–134).**
- It must reasonably identify the subject matter, show a contract was made, state the essential terms, and be signed by the party to be charged.
- The writing need not be formal or meant as a contract (a letter, a napkin, a crayon memo). Any mark intended to authenticate is a signature, including a printed letterhead.
- **E-SIGN / UETA:** Electronic records and signatures satisfy the statute.

**Rule: Piecing together writings.** A memorandum can be pieced together from several writings, only some of them signed, if the signed writing establishes the contractual relationship and the unsigned writings clearly refer to the same transaction.

**Case: Crabtree v. Elizabeth Arden Sales Corp.** (N.Y. 1953)
- **Facts:** Crabtree wanted a 3-year deal and agreed orally to a two-year contract as sales manager, with pay rising over time. The terms were recorded on payroll change cards, some signed by Arden's officers, plus an unsigned memo that said "2 years to make good." Arden refused the second raise.
- **Issue:** Can several writings, some of them unsigned, together satisfy the Statute of Frauds?
- **Holding:** Enforceable. A memorandum can be pieced together from several writings, only some of them signed, if the signed writing establishes the contractual relationship and the unsigned writings clearly refer to the same transaction. Oral testimony may show the connection.
- **Takeaway:** The Statute of Frauds memorandum can be pieced together from signed and unsigned writings that clearly refer to the same transaction.

**Rule: Part performance (R2 § 129).** A land contract can be specifically enforced if the party seeking enforcement reasonably relied on the contract and the other party's continuing assent, and changed position so that injustice can be avoided only by specific enforcement.

**Case: Beaver v. Brumlow** (N.M. Ct. App. 2010)
- **Facts:** Landowners orally agreed to sell the Brumlows land and let them put a house on it. The Brumlows moved in, built the house, and made improvements. No price or closing date was ever set. The landowners later tried to evict them.
- **Issue:** Can an oral agreement to sell land be enforced despite the Statute of Frauds when the buyers took possession and built a house?
- **Holding:** Specific performance granted under the part performance doctrine. The buyers' possession plus substantial improvements, done in reliance on the oral agreement and unequivocally referable to it, take the contract outside the statute. The missing price could be supplied by fair market value.
- **Takeaway:** Possession plus substantial improvements made in reliance on an oral land contract take it out of the Statute of Frauds under the part performance doctrine.

**Rule: Reliance exception to the Statute of Frauds (R2 § 139).** A promise that induces foreseeable reliance is enforceable despite the statute if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. Factors: (a) whether other remedies like restitution are available and adequate; (b) the definite and substantial character of the reliance; (c) how far the reliance corroborates the promise, or the promise is shown by clear and convincing evidence; (d) the reasonableness of the reliance; (e) its foreseeability.

**Rule: Split on § 139.** Some states reject § 139 entirely (estoppel can't override the statute). Others limit it to unconscionable injury.

**Case: Alaska Democratic Party v. Rice** (Alaska 1997)
- **Facts:** Rice was promised orally a two-year job as executive director of the Alaska Democratic Party. She quit her Maryland job and moved to Alaska. The party's executive committee refused to hire her.
- **Issue:** Can an oral two-year employment promise be enforced despite the Statute of Frauds when the employee relied on it?
- **Holding:** The oral two-year contract was within the one-year provision, but Rice could recover under promissory estoppel (R2 § 139).
- **Takeaway:** Reasonable reliance on an oral promise can make it enforceable despite the Statute of Frauds (R2 § 139).

Restitution survives the statute. Even when the statute bars enforcement, a party can recover the value of benefits conferred under the unenforceable contract (R2 § 375).

## Class 18 — The Statute of Frauds and the UCC

### Reading: CB 372–382 (Hart)
**Rule: UCC § 2-201(1).** A contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more requires a writing, sufficient to indicate a contract, signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought.
- The writing needs only a quantity term. Price and other terms may be missing or even wrong.
- The contract isn't enforceable beyond the quantity shown in the writing.

**Rule: Exceptions to UCC § 2-201.**
- **§ 2-201(2): Merchant's confirmation.** Between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time binds the recipient too, unless the recipient objects in writing within 10 days.
- **§ 2-201(3)(a): Specially manufactured goods.** Applies to goods made for the buyer that aren't suitable for sale to others, if the seller has made a substantial beginning before learning of repudiation.
- **§ 2-201(3)(b): Admission.** The party admits in a pleading, testimony, or in court that a contract was made (enforceable up to the quantity admitted).
- **§ 2-201(3)(c): Part performance.** Applies to goods for which payment has been made and accepted, or that have been received and accepted.

**Case: Buffaloe v. Hart** (N.C. Ct. App. 1994)
- **Facts:** Buffaloe orally agreed to buy five tobacco barns from Hart. He gave Hart a check for a down payment, and Hart held it for several days before returning it. Buffaloe had also arranged to resell the barns. Hart sold to someone else.
- **Issue:** Was the buyer's check a sufficient writing under UCC § 2-201, and if not, was the oral contract enforceable anyway?
- **Holding:** Jury verdict for Buffaloe ($21,000) affirmed. (1) The check was not a sufficient writing. It stated a quantity, but it was signed only by Buffaloe, not by the Harts, the parties to be charged. (2) The contract was still enforceable under the part-performance exception, § 2-201(3)(c). There was evidence that Buffaloe accepted the barns (he treated them as his and arranged to resell them) and that the Harts accepted payment by holding the check for days. Acceptance is a jury question.
- **Takeaway:** A writing must be signed by the party to be charged, but a goods contract is enforceable to the extent payment was made and accepted (UCC § 2-201(3)(c)).

**Other points from the reading:**
- **UCC § 1-103(b):** Principles of law and equity, including estoppel, supplement the Code. Courts are split on whether promissory estoppel can overcome § 2-201.
- UCC § 1-201 definitions: "Signed" means any symbol adopted with present intent to adopt or accept a writing. "Writing" includes printing or any other intentional reduction to tangible form. A "record" can be electronic.
- **CISG Art. 11:** International sales contracts need not be in writing.

# UNIT VII — Implied Terms & Good Faith (Classes 20–21)

## Class 20 — Implied Promises

### Reading: CB 485–496 (Wood; Leibel)

**Rule: Best efforts under the UCC (§ 2-306).** UCC § 2-306(2): an exclusive dealing agreement imposes a duty of best efforts on both sides. § 2-306(1) does the same for output and requirements contracts, measured in good faith.

**Case: Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon** (N.Y. 1917)
- **Facts:** Lady Duff-Gordon, a fashion designer, gave Wood an exclusive right to place her endorsements and market her designs, in exchange for half the profits. She then endorsed products behind his back. She argued the deal lacked consideration because Wood never promised to do anything.
- **Issue:** Is an exclusive agency agreement supported by consideration when the agent made no express promise to do anything?
- **Holding:** Enforceable. A promise by Wood to use reasonable efforts is fairly implied from the whole writing. The law has outgrown the "primitive formalism" of requiring precise words: "A promise may be lacking, and yet the whole writing may be 'instinct with an obligation,' imperfectly expressed." The exclusive grant would make no business sense unless Wood was obligated to try, since her income depended entirely on his efforts.
- **Takeaway:** A promise to use reasonable efforts can be implied in an exclusive dealing arrangement, making it enforceable.

**Rule: Termination of indefinite contracts (UCC § 2-309(3)).** Ending a contract of indefinite duration governed by Article 2 requires reasonable notification. A distributorship falls under Article 2 when its predominant purpose is the sale of goods.

**Case: Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.** (Ky. Ct. App. 1978)
- **Facts:** Raynor (a garage-door manufacturer) and Leibel had an oral exclusive dealership of indefinite duration. Raynor ended it with no advance notice.
- **Issue:** Does terminating an exclusive dealership of indefinite duration require reasonable notice?
- **Holding:** The distributorship was governed by UCC Article 2, since its predominant purpose was the sale of goods. Under UCC § 2-309(3), ending an indefinite contract requires reasonable notification. Summary judgment was improper. Reasonableness of notice is a fact question.
- **Takeaway:** Terminating an indefinite-term distributorship governed by UCC Article 2 requires reasonable notice (§ 2-309(3)).

### Reading: CB 496–517 (Morin)

**Rule: Satisfaction clauses.**
- **Personal taste, fancy, or aesthetics** (a portrait, a suit): subjective standard, meaning honest dissatisfaction is enough.
- **Commercial quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility:** objective standard (a reasonable person).

**Rule: Preference for the objective standard.** When the contract is unclear, courts prefer the objective standard, because a subjective one invites forfeiture.

**Case: Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction** (7th Cir. 1983)
- **Facts:** Morin supplied aluminum siding for a GM plant. The contract required the work to meet GM's "complete satisfaction." GM rejected the siding because it looked uneven in certain light, even though it met the specifications.
- **Issue:** Is a "complete satisfaction" clause measured by the buyer's actual satisfaction or by a reasonable person's?
- **Holding:** The objective standard applied. The question was whether a reasonable person would be satisfied, not whether GM actually was. The siding was a functional wall for a factory, so the objective standard applied, even though it involved some appearance.
- **Takeaway:** When a satisfaction clause concerns commercial quality or fitness rather than personal taste, the objective reasonable-person standard applies.

**Case (note): Seidenberg v. Summit Bank** (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 2002). A breach of the implied covenant of good faith claim can survive dismissal even between sophisticated parties. Equal bargaining power is one factor, not a bar. The parol evidence rule doesn't block the claim.

### Reading: Warranties (CB 551–552)
**Rule: Express warranty (UCC § 2-313).** Created by (a) an affirmation of fact or promise about the goods, (b) a description, or (c) a sample or model, that becomes part of the basis of the bargain. No special words or intent to warrant are needed. Puffing ("best car in town") isn't a warranty.

**Rule: Implied warranty of merchantability (UCC § 2-314).** Arises when the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind. The goods must be fit for their ordinary purpose and pass without objection in the trade.

**Rule: Fitness for a particular purpose (UCC § 2-315).** Applies when the seller has reason to know of the buyer's particular purpose and that the buyer is relying on the seller's skill or judgment. It applies to any seller, not just merchants.

- **Javins v. First National Realty** (D.C. Cir. 1970): an implied warranty of habitability is read into residential leases. It replaced the common-law no-repair rule. Compare URLTA § 2.104, which codifies the landlord's duty to maintain the premises.

## Class 21 — Good Faith Continued

### Reading: CB 518–544 (Locke; Geysen)
**Rule: Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing (R2 § 205; UCC § 1-304).** Every contract imposes a duty of good faith in its performance and enforcement. Neither party may do anything to deprive the other of the benefits of the agreement.
- It cannot override express terms. If the contract expressly gives a party the right to do something, exercising that right isn't bad faith (the *Carma* rule).
- It applies most often where one party has discretion. The discretion must be exercised honestly and consistent with the parties' reasonable expectations.
- Bad faith includes evading the spirit of the bargain, lack of diligence, abuse of discretion, and interfering with the other's performance (R2 § 205 cmt. d).

**Rule: Express terms limit the implied covenant.** a contract that expressly lets a party refrain from acting (for example, "the studio has no obligation to produce") would defeat the implied covenant.

**Case: Locke v. Warner Bros.** (Cal. Ct. App. 1997)
- **Facts:** After her split from Clint Eastwood, Sondra Locke got a $1.5 million "development deal" with Warner. Warner paid the money but had discretion to reject her projects. Evidence showed Warner never intended to use her. Eastwood had secretly reimbursed Warner for the deal.
- **Issue:** Does a studio with discretion to reject a party's projects breach the implied covenant of good faith by refusing to consider them at all?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment for Warner reversed. Even a contract that gives a party subjective creative discretion requires that discretion to be exercised honestly and in good faith. A categorical refusal to consider her proposals on their merits, while just going through the motions, would breach the implied covenant. Paying the guaranteed money didn't fully perform the contract. Locke also bargained for the opportunity to develop projects and advance her career.
- **Takeaway:** Even subjective discretion must be exercised honestly and in good faith, so refusing to consider a party's proposals on their merits can breach the implied covenant.

**Rule: Bad-faith termination.** An employer may fire an at-will employee for any reason, but firing an employee in bad faith to avoid paying commissions for work already done breaches the implied covenant of good faith.

**Case: Geysen v. Securitas Security Services** (Conn. 2016)
- **Facts:** Geysen, an at-will salesperson, was fired shortly before large commissions would have been paid. His contract said commissions were paid only on amounts invoiced before termination.
- **Issue:** Is a commission cutoff at termination enforceable, and can firing an at-will employee to avoid paying commissions breach the implied covenant?
- **Holding:** The commission cutoff was enforceable. It didn't violate the wage statute (§ 31-72) or public policy, because under the contract's express terms he hadn't earned the unpaid commissions. He could pursue a claim for breach of the implied covenant. An employer may fire an at-will employee, but it may not fire someone in bad faith to avoid paying commissions the employee reasonably expected for work already done (following *Fortune v. NCR*, Mass. 1977).
- **Takeaway:** An employer may fire an at-will employee but breaches the implied covenant of good faith if it fires the employee to avoid paying earned commissions.

**At-will employment notes:**
- The presumption is at-will (either side can end it for any reason or no reason).
- **Exceptions:** public policy (firing someone for refusing to break the law, or for jury duty), implied-in-fact for-cause terms (employee handbooks, as in *Toussaint*), and bad-faith firing to grab earned benefits (*Fortune*, *Geysen*).

# UNIT VIII — Interpretation & the Parol Evidence Rule (Classes 22–26)

**Big picture:**
- Interpretation finds what the parties meant. Construction decides the legal effect of the words, including gap-filling and implied terms.
- The parol evidence rule (PER) has two prongs:
  - **Ambiguity** (Classes 22–23): Can outside evidence be used to explain the writing's meaning?
  - **Integration** (Classes 25–26): Can outside evidence add to or contradict a final writing?

## Class 22 — The Ambiguity Determination

### Reading: CB 387–405
**Rule: Goal of interpretation (R2 § 200).** Find the intent of the parties, as shown by their language read objectively.

**Two approaches to deciding whether a term is ambiguous:**

| | Textualism (plain meaning / four corners) | Contextualism (Corbin / R2 / *Taylor*) |
|---|---|---|
| Stage one: is there ambiguity? | Look only at the text. Ambiguity must be patent (visible on the face). | Consider extrinsic evidence (negotiations, circumstances, trade usage) to see whether the language is reasonably susceptible to the proposed meaning. Ambiguity can be latent. |
| If unambiguous | Apply the plain meaning. No outside evidence. | Same. But the court gets there only after hearing the context. |
| Stage two: resolving ambiguity | Then extrinsic evidence comes in to decide the meaning. | Same. Usually a question for the fact-finder. |
| Who decides ambiguity? | The judge (question of law) | The judge (question of law) |

- **Patent ambiguity:** apparent from the text, for example a vague term or two conflicting provisions. Latent ambiguity: the text looks clear, but facts outside the writing show two meanings (as in *Raffles v. Wichelhaus*, two ships named *Peerless*).
- **Not ambiguity:** The parties disagreeing about what a term means doesn't make it ambiguous. Ask whether both readings are reasonable, in the contested respect.
- Dictionaries are not extrinsic evidence. Textualist courts use them.
- **Holmes/Corbin critique:** Words have no meaning apart from context, so a judge can't decide that text is "plain" without knowing its setting.

**Case: Prochazka v. Bee-Three Development, LLC** (Ark. Ct. App. 2015)
- **Facts:** A land purchase agreement let the buyer terminate "in its sole and absolute discretion" if the property was "not suitable for Buyer's intended use." The clause sat inside the article on inspecting the property. The buyer (Bee-Three) terminated during the inspection period because its undisclosed prospective tenant backed out, not because of anything wrong with the property. It sued to recover its $7,000 earnest money. The trial court granted it summary judgment, reading the clause as an absolute right to terminate.
- **Issue:** Was a clause letting the buyer terminate "in its sole and absolute discretion" unambiguous?
- **Holding:** Reversed. The clause was ambiguous, so summary judgment was improper. Read as a whole, its placement in the inspection article, the contract's other termination clauses (each tied to the subject of its own section), and the undefined term "intended use" made it reasonable to read the discretion as limited to inspection-related grounds. By contrast, *Harris Corp. v. Giesting* held a standalone "termination for convenience" clause unambiguous.
- **Takeaway:** A clause's placement and the contract's structure can make an apparently absolute term ambiguous.

## Class 23 — The Ambiguity Prong of the PER; Contextualist Methodology

### Reading: CB 440–455 (Taylor) (skip notes 1 and 5, pp. 450–51)

**Rule: Interpretation and the parol evidence rule (Corbin § 573).** The rule doesn't bar evidence offered to show what the writing means. It bars evidence offered to vary or contradict an integrated writing. R2 § 214(c): prior agreements and negotiations are admissible to establish the meaning of the writing, whether or not it is integrated.

**Rule: Reasonably susceptible test (Corbin).**
- There's no preliminary finding of ambiguity required before the judge looks at context.
- It's a sliding scale: the more implausible the proposed meaning ("white is black"), the more convincing the evidence must be.
- The judge retains discretion to keep out evidence that would contradict the text in an implausible way.
- "Reasonably susceptible" is a question of law for the court. The meaning is a question of fact.
- The parties' later conduct is evidence of what they meant.

**Case: Taylor v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.** (Ariz. 1993)
- **Facts:** Taylor caused an accident and was hit with a judgment above his policy limits. He signed a release with State Farm settling his claims under the policy. He later sued State Farm for bad faith (failing to settle within limits). State Farm said the release covered the bad-faith claim. Taylor offered evidence from the negotiations that it didn't.
- **Issue:** May a court consider extrinsic evidence of the parties' negotiations to interpret a release that seems clear on its face?
- **Holding:** Arizona follows Corbin. The judge first considers the offered evidence and asks whether the language is "reasonably susceptible" to the proposed meaning. If it is, the evidence comes in. Here the release language seemed broad on its face, but read in light of the evidence it was reasonably susceptible to Taylor's reading that it did not release the bad-faith claim. The trial court properly admitted the evidence, and the jury's finding for Taylor on the release issue stood.
- **Takeaway:** The judge considers the offered extrinsic evidence first and admits it if the language is reasonably susceptible to the proposed meaning, without a preliminary finding of ambiguity.

### Reading: Contextualist Methodology (CB 406–418) (Frigaliment)

**Rule: Subjective intent.** A party's subjective intent matters only if it coincides with an objective meaning of the term.

**Case: Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales** (S.D.N.Y. 1960)
- **Facts:** A Swiss buyer ordered "U.S. Fresh Frozen Chicken, Grade A" in two sizes (1½–2 lb and 2½–3 lb). The buyer meant young broilers and fryers. The seller, new to the poultry trade, shipped stewing chickens ("fowl") in the larger size. "Chicken" can mean either.
- **Issue:** Does "chicken" in the contract mean only young broilers and fryers, or does it include stewing chickens?
- **Holding:** For the seller. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving that "chicken" was used in its narrower sense, and it failed to carry it. The seller's meaning matched the dictionary, the regulations, and market realities. The court weighed, in order: (1) The contract itself: Two weight classes, but nothing in the text forced both to be young chickens. "A contract for 'apples' of two different sizes could be filled with different kinds of apples."; (2) Negotiations: The cables were in German and English. The buyer used the English word "chicken" because it was supposedly more precise. That proved little; (3) Trade usage: It binds a party new to the trade only if they actually knew it, or the usage was so notorious that knowledge can be presumed (*Walls v. Bailey*). The evidence was conflicting, and the buyer's own witnesses protected themselves by saying "broiler."; (4) Regulations: The contract referred to USDA grading, and the USDA definition of chicken included stewing chickens; (5) Market price: The price was below the market price for broilers, so the seller could reasonably think it was selling fowl; (6) Course of performance: The buyer accepted the first shipment and then ordered more.
- **Takeaway:** The party arguing for a narrower meaning of a term bears the burden of proving it, and subjective intent counts only if it matches an objective meaning.

**Rule: UCC § 1-303 hierarchy.** Express terms control over course of performance (this contract), which controls over course of dealing (past contracts between the parties), which controls over usage of trade (the industry).

**Rule: Canons of interpretation (R2 §§ 202–203).**
1. Read the contract as a whole.
2. Avoid readings that put terms in conflict.
3. Avoid readings that make terms surplus.
4. Prefer reasonable, lawful meanings over unreasonable ones.
5. Give weight to the contract's principal purpose.
6. **Noscitur a sociis:** a word is known by the company it keeps.
7. **Ejusdem generis:** general words after specific ones cover only similar things.
8. **Expressio unius:** listing some things excludes others.
9. Specific terms control over general ones, and negotiated terms over boilerplate.
10. **Contra proferentem:** ambiguity is construed against the drafter. It's a tiebreaker of last resort.

## Class 24 — Whose Meaning?

### Reading: CB 418–429 (C & J Fertilizer; Problem 5-1)

**Rule: Reasonable expectations doctrine.** In insurance and other form contracts, courts may honor the objectively reasonable expectations of the non-drafting party, even against fine print.

**Rule: Reasonable expectations and standardized contracts (R2 § 211(3)).**
- Most states have adopted some version of the doctrine. Many limit it to ambiguous or hidden or inconspicuous terms. Florida rejected it outright.
- **R2 § 211(3):** When the drafter has reason to believe the other party wouldn't sign if it knew a particular term was in the form, that term is not part of the agreement. This narrows the doctrine (it focuses on what the drafter knew) but extends it to all standardized contracts, not just insurance.
- **Adhesion contract:** a standardized form offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis by a party with stronger bargaining power. Rakoff: the drafter makes many such deals, the adherent few, and the adherent's main duty is usually to pay money. He argued these contracts should be presumptively unenforceable.

**Case: C & J Fertilizer, Inc. v. Allied Mutual Insurance Co.** (Iowa 1975)
- **Facts:** C & J's burglary policies defined "burglary" to require visible marks of forcible entry "upon . . . the exterior of the premises at the place of such entry." Thieves left truck tire tracks and broke an interior door with tools, but the outer plexiglass door could be forced open without leaving marks. Loss: about $10,000 of chemicals. When the coverage was sold, the agent said only that there had to be visible evidence of a burglary (that is, not an inside job). The policy was mailed afterward. The insured's president, a farmer with a high school education, never read the fine-print definition. The agent himself was surprised by the denial. The trial court found the definition unambiguous and ruled for the insurer.
- **Issue:** Is the insured bound by a fine-print definition of burglary that defeats coverage for an actual third-party burglary?
- **Holding:** Reversed (5–4). The court applied the doctrine of reasonable expectations: "The objectively reasonable expectations of applicants and intended beneficiaries regarding the terms of insurance contracts will be honored even though painstaking study of the policy provisions would have negated those expectations." An ordinary insured would expect coverage for a real third-party burglary. The court also relied on implied warranty and unconscionability (R2 § 237 cmt. f, Tentative Draft, which became § 211).
- **Takeaway:** In insurance and other form contracts, courts honor the insured's objectively reasonable expectations even against unambiguous fine print.

**Rule: Standards of meaning (R2 § 201).**
- (1) If the parties attach the same meaning to a term, that meaning controls, even if it's unusual.
- (2) If they attach different meanings, the meaning of party A controls if, when the contract was made, (a) B knew A's meaning and A didn't know B's, or (b) B had reason to know A's meaning and A had no reason to know B's.
- (3) Otherwise, neither party is bound by the other's meaning. There may be no contract (R2 § 20).

**Case: Joyner v. Adams** (N.C. Ct. App. 1987)
- **Facts:** A commercial lease amendment required the lessee, a developer, to have the lots "developed" by a deadline or pay retroactively increased rent. The lessee had put in the infrastructure (streets, water, sewer) on every lot but hadn't built buildings on all of them. The lessor said "developed" meant buildings.
- **Issue:** How should a court resolve a dispute when the parties attached different meanings to "developed"?
- **Holding:** The trial court shouldn't have resolved the dispute by construing the term against the drafter. Where each party attached a different meaning, the court must apply R2 § 201: did either party know or have reason to know the other's meaning? The case was remanded for those findings.
- **Takeaway:** When the parties attached different meanings to a term, the court must apply R2 § 201 (who knew or had reason to know the other's meaning) before resorting to construing against the drafter.

## Class 25 — The Integration Prong of the PER

### Reading: *Mitchill v. Lath* (N.Y. 1928); CB 429–440 and note 1, p. 450 (Thompson)

**Rule: Parol evidence rule (R2 §§ 209–218; UCC § 2-202).**
- **Complete (full) integration:** The writing is the final and exclusive statement of the whole agreement. Prior or contemporaneous terms can't contradict it or add to it (R2 §§ 210(1), 216).
- **Partial integration:** The writing is final on the terms it covers but isn't the whole deal. Earlier terms can't contradict it but may add consistent additional terms (R2 § 216(1)).
- **No integration** (a preliminary draft): The rule doesn't apply.
- **What it covers:** Prior agreements (oral or written) and contemporaneous oral agreements. It does not cover later agreements (that's modification, Class 35).
- **Substantive, not evidentiary:** Even evidence that came in without objection is disregarded if the objection is made before the case goes to the fact-finder. In a jury trial, the judge rules on admissibility in camera (outside the jury).
- Who decides? Integration is a question of law for the judge.

**Two approaches to whether a writing is integrated:**

| | Williston / classical (*Thompson*, *Mitchill* majority) | Corbin / R2 § 210 cmt. b |
|---|---|---|
| What the judge looks at | The four corners of the writing | The writing plus all surrounding circumstances |
| Facially complete writing | Conclusively presumed fully integrated | Only evidence of integration. "A writing cannot of itself prove its own completeness." |
| Merger clause | Conclusive, or nearly so | Strong evidence but not decisive |
| Collateral-term test | The term must concern a distinct subject (*Thompson*), or be one parties wouldn't ordinarily be expected to put in the writing (*Mitchill*) | R2 § 216(2): a term agreed to for separate consideration, or one that "might naturally be omitted" from the writing |

- UCC § 2-202 cmt. 3 is even looser: consistent additional terms come in unless they "would certainly have been included" in the writing.

**Rule: Exceptions to the parol evidence rule.**
1. Interpretation (R2 § 214(c)): evidence offered to show what the writing means, not to add a term (see *Taylor*).
2. **Subsequent agreements:** modifications made after the writing.
3. **Oral condition precedent** (R2 § 217): the parties agreed orally that the writing wouldn't take effect until some event happened.
4. Invalidity (R2 § 214(d)): fraud, duress, undue influence, incapacity, mistake, illegality.
   - Some courts allow only fraud in the execution (factum), meaning a trick about what's being signed. Most also allow fraud in the inducement, even with a merger clause. Some bar fraud evidence that directly contradicts the writing (*Sherrodd*, Class 26).
5. Equitable remedy (R2 § 214(e)): reformation for a mistake in writing down the deal. It requires clear and convincing evidence.
6. Collateral agreement (R2 § 216(2)): separate consideration, or a term naturally omitted.

**Supplementation vs. interpretation:** Supplementation tries to prove a term that's missing from the writing (*Thompson*'s oral warranty), and the PER blocks it if the writing is fully integrated. Interpretation uses context to show what the written words mean (*Taylor*'s release), and the PER never blocks that.

**Case: Thompson v. Libby** (Minn. 1885)
- **Facts:** A signed writing for the sale of logs covered the parties, goods, price ($10 per thousand feet), and payment. It said nothing about quality. The buyer (Libby) tried to prove an oral warranty of quality made at the time of sale. There was no claim of fraud and no request for reformation.
- **Issue:** Can a buyer prove an oral warranty of quality that isn't in a written sales contract?
- **Holding:** Excluded. The writing was complete on its face, so it's conclusively presumed to hold the whole agreement. The rule "forbids to add by parol where the writing is silent, as well as to vary where it speaks." A warranty is part of the sale, not a collateral agreement on a "distinct subject."
- **Takeaway:** A writing that is complete on its face is conclusively presumed fully integrated, so an oral warranty can't be added.

**Rule: Collateral oral agreements (three conditions).** An oral agreement may vary a written contract only if:
1. it is collateral in form;
2. it does not contradict express or implied provisions of the writing; and
3. it is not one the parties would ordinarily be expected to embody in the writing.

**Case: Mitchill v. Lath** (N.Y. 1928)
- **Facts:** The Laths sold Mrs. Mitchill their farm. They orally promised to remove an unsightly icehouse that they owned on a neighbor's land across the road. She signed a detailed written contract of sale that said nothing about the icehouse, took the deed, and spent money improving the property. The Laths never removed the icehouse.
- **Issue:** Can a buyer prove the seller's oral promise to remove an icehouse that isn't in the written contract of sale?
- **Holding:** Excluded. Condition 1 was met. Condition 2 was doubtful (a detailed writing may imply that the sellers have no other obligations). Condition 3 failed: the promise was "so clearly connected with the principal transaction as to be part and parcel of it," so the parties would have put it in the contract. Judgment for Mitchill was reversed.
- **Takeaway:** A collateral oral agreement may vary a written contract only if it is collateral in form, doesn't contradict the writing, and is not one the parties would ordinarily be expected to put in the writing.

## Class 26 — Integration Continued

### Reading: CB 454–475 (Sherrodd; Nanakuli)

**Rule: Fraud exception (narrow view).** The fraud exception to the parol evidence rule applies only when the fraud doesn't relate directly to the subject of the contract. An oral representation that directly contradicts the express written terms is barred. Most courts also don't let promissory estoppel get around the parol evidence rule.

**Case: Sherrodd, Inc. v. Morrison-Knudsen Co.** (Mont. 1991)
- **Facts:** An earthwork subcontractor bid $97,500, based on an alleged pre-bid statement that the job involved 25,000 cubic yards. It started work, then found the job was roughly double that. It signed the written subcontract only after (allegedly) being threatened with nonpayment for work already done and promised that extra pay would be worked out. The subcontract said "LS" (lump sum) for $97,500. It said the subcontractor had satisfied itself "by examination" about the quantity of materials. It had a merger clause, a no-oral-modification term, and required changes to be in signed writings.
- **Issue:** Does the fraud exception to the parol evidence rule admit oral representations that contradict the written subcontract?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment for the defendants. The fraud exception applies only when the fraud doesn't relate directly to the subject of the contract. When an oral representation directly contradicts the express written terms, the PER applies. The later promise of extra pay failed the modification statute (a written or executed oral agreement was required). The good-faith claim failed because no express term was breached.
- **Takeaway:** Under the narrow view, the fraud exception to the parol evidence rule doesn't admit oral representations that directly contradict the express written terms.

**Rule: Agreement, usage, and course of performance under the UCC.**
- "Agreement" means the bargain in fact, including usage, dealing, and performance (§ 1-201(3)). Usage and course of dealing become part of the deal unless "carefully negated" (§ 2-202 cmt. 2).
- Hierarchy (§ 1-205(4), now § 1-303(e)): express terms > course of performance > course of dealing > usage of trade. But read them as consistent whenever reasonable.
- Course of performance (§ 2-208) is the best evidence of what the parties meant.

**Case: Nanakuli Paving & Rock Co. v. Shell Oil Co.** (9th Cir. 1981)
- **Facts:** A long-term asphalt supply contract set the price at "Shell's Posted Price at time of delivery" and said nothing about price protection. In the Hawaii paving trade, suppliers routinely price protected pavers, holding the old price for tonnage the paver had already committed to fixed-price government bids. Shell had price protected Nanakuli twice before (1970, 1971). After the 1973 oil embargo, Shell's new management raised the price from $44 to $76 with no notice and no protection.
- **Issue:** Can trade usage and course of performance require price protection under a contract that set the price at "Shell's Posted Price at time of delivery"?
- **Holding:** Jury verdict for Nanakuli reinstated. The relevant trade is the Hawaii asphaltic paving trade, not just asphalt suppliers. A party is bound by usages of its own trade, and by local business practices it knew or should have known. A jury could find a usage of trade of price protection, and that Shell's two earlier protections were a course of performance, not one-time waivers. Usage can cut down an express term without negating it, as long as it doesn't "swallow it entirely." Alternatively, good faith (UCC § 1-203, now § 1-304) required advance notice and price protection, because those were the trade's commercial standards.
- **Takeaway:** Usage of trade and course of performance can become part of the agreement and qualify an express term, as long as they don't negate it entirely.

# UNIT IX — Conditions & Breach (Classes 27–29)

**Big picture:**
- A promise creates a duty. A condition is an event that must happen before a duty to perform arises (R2 § 224). The same term can be both (a promissory condition).
- Express conditions are created by the parties and require strict compliance. Constructive conditions are imposed by the court for fairness and require only substantial performance.
- **The sequence:** Did a condition to the other party's duty fail? Is the failure excused? Was there a breach? Was it material (so the other side can suspend) or total (so the other side can terminate and sue)? Or was there an anticipatory repudiation?

## Class 27 — Express Conditions

### Reading: CB 825–850 (Oppenheimer; J.N.A. Realty)
**Rule: Express conditions (R2 §§ 224–227, 235, 237).**
- Breach (R2 § 235(2)): any non-performance of a duty when it's due. If a condition hasn't occurred, performance isn't due, so not performing isn't a breach.
- **Condition precedent:** an event (other than the passage of time) that must occur before a duty to perform arises. A condition can also be precedent to the formation of the contract itself.
- **Effect** (R2 § 225): Until the condition occurs, the duty doesn't arise. Once it can't occur, the duty is discharged.
- Interpretation preference (R2 § 227(1)): If it's doubtful whether a term is a condition, courts read it as a promise or a constructive condition. The preference is especially strong when calling it a condition would risk forfeiture. But clear language ("unless and until," "null and void," "on condition that") will be honored even if forfeiture results.
- **No substantial performance for express conditions** (R2 § 237 cmt. d): Materiality doesn't matter. The only way out is an excuse.
- **Pay-when-paid vs. pay-if-paid:** "When paid" is just a timing term, so the general contractor must pay the sub within a reasonable time even if the owner never pays. "If paid" makes the owner's payment an express condition, but only if it says so clearly and unambiguously.
- **Notice:** Written notice required by a date must actually be received by that date. Option notices must be timely, and the mailbox rule doesn't apply to options.

**Rule: Excuses for an express condition (R2 §§ 84, 229, 245).**
1. **Disproportionate forfeiture** (R2 § 229): A court may excuse the condition unless its occurrence was a material part of the agreed exchange.
2. **Waiver** (R2 § 84): The obligor gives up the condition by words or conduct. No consideration is needed, but only for non-material conditions. A waiver can be retracted before the time for the condition if a reasonable time remains and the other side hasn't relied on the waiver.
3. **Modification:** Like any other term, a condition can be changed with consideration, under § 89, or through reliance.
4. **Prevention** (R2 § 245): If the obligor wrongfully hinders the condition, it's excused, because good faith requires not blocking a condition of your own duty.
5. **One-sided conditions:** A condition that protects only one party can be waived by that party alone. A condition for both parties can't be waived unilaterally.

**Case: Oppenheimer & Co. v. Oppenheim, Appel, Dixon & Co.** (N.Y. 1995)
- **Facts:** A sublease letter agreement said that if the plaintiff didn't deliver the prime landlord's written consent to the tenant's work by the (extended) deadline, the agreement would be "null and void." The parties would not sign the sublease "unless and until" the condition was met. On the deadline the plaintiff only phoned to say consent had been obtained. The written consent arrived 23 days late. The defendant walked away. The jury found substantial performance and awarded $1.2 million.
- **Issue:** Does substantial performance satisfy an express condition requiring written consent by a deadline?
- **Holding:** Reversed. This was an unambiguous express condition precedent, so it required literal performance. Substantial performance doesn't apply to express conditions. There was no forfeiture to excuse: the plaintiff hadn't spent anything in reliance on this sublease, and its $1 million licensing fee to the landlord wasn't lost. Substantial performance is a question for the judge when the inferences are certain.
- **Takeaway:** An express condition requires strict compliance, so substantial performance doesn't satisfy it, and relief comes only through an excuse such as forfeiture.

**Rule: Equitable relief from forfeiture of a renewal option.** Equity may relieve a tenant in possession who missed the deadline to exercise a renewal option if the default was mere inadvertence, the forfeiture is out of proportion to the fault, and the landlord wasn't prejudiced. Courts almost never give this relief for options to purchase real estate.

**Case: J.N.A. Realty Corp. v. Cross Bay Chelsea, Inc.** (N.Y. 1977)
- **Facts:** A restaurant tenant had paid $155,000 for the business and lease and made improvements. It negligently missed the deadline to send written notice exercising a 24-year renewal option. It sent notice about 5 months late. The landlord, which had sent reminders about other obligations but not this one, refused to renew.
- **Issue:** Can equity relieve a tenant who negligently missed the deadline to exercise a lease renewal option?
- **Holding:** Equity may relieve a tenant in possession from forfeiting a renewal option when the default was "mere venial inattention" (not willful or grossly negligent), the forfeiture (improvements, goodwill of a long-standing location) is out of proportion to the fault, and the landlord was not prejudiced. The case was remanded to let the landlord prove prejudice.
- **Takeaway:** Equity may relieve a tenant from forfeiting a renewal option missed through mere inadvertence if the forfeiture is disproportionate and the landlord isn't prejudiced.

## Class 28 — Implied (Constructive) Conditions

### Reading: CB 850–862 (Jacob & Youngs)
**History of dependent promises:**
- **Independent covenants** (*Nichols v. Raynbred*, 1615): Each side had to perform even if the other didn't, and could only sue separately.
- **Kingston v. Preston** (1773): The buyer's promise to give security was a condition precedent to the seller's duty to hand over the business. Mansfield identified three kinds of covenants: (1) mutual and independent; (2) conditions and dependent, where one performance depends on the prior performance of the other; and (3) mutual conditions to be performed at the same time.
- **Morton v. Lamb:** When performances can be exchanged at the same time, they're concurrent conditions. A plaintiff must show it performed or tendered.

**Rule: Order of performance (R2 § 234).**
- **R2 § 234(1):** If the performances can be exchanged simultaneously, they're due at the same time. Each party's tender is a condition of the other's duty.
- **UCC §§ 2-507, 2-511:** Tender of delivery conditions the buyer's duty to pay. Tender of payment conditions the seller's duty to deliver.
- **R2 § 234(2):** If one performance takes time (such as construction), it must be done first. So the builder builds and then gets paid.
- **R2 § 226:** The Restatement sorts conditions into express, implied-in-fact (from conduct), and constructive (imposed by the court).

**Rule: Substantial performance (R2 § 237).** Each party's duty is constructively conditioned on there being no uncured material failure by the other. A minor deviation isn't a failure of the condition. The other party must still perform, but it can recover damages for the defect.

**Case: Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent** (N.Y. 1921)
- **Facts:** The contract for a $77,000 country house specified "wrought-iron pipe of Reading manufacture." Through the plumbing subcontractor's oversight, much of the pipe was made by other manufacturers. It was identical in quality, appearance, market value, and cost. Fixing it meant demolishing finished walls. The architect refused the final certificate. Kent refused to pay the $3,483.46 balance.
- **Issue:** Must the owner pay when the builder used a different brand of pipe of identical quality, and if so, how are damages measured?
- **Holding:** For the builder. An omission "both trivial and innocent" doesn't breach a condition, so there's no forfeiture. It's "atoned for" by damages. When replacement cost is "grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," damages are the difference in value, here "nominal or nothing." The court reasoned: Whether a promise is a condition depends "partly on justice and partly on presumable intention."; "The willful transgressor must accept the penalty of his transgression."; Forfeiture is especially harsh when the work is "united to the land" and can't be returned; Parties can make any term a condition by "apt and certain words." The law just won't presume it; Materiality is "a question of degree." It goes to the jury if the facts are in doubt.
- **Takeaway:** A trivial and innocent deviation is not a failure of a constructive condition, so the builder recovers the price less damages measured by the difference in value when repair cost is grossly disproportionate.

## Class 29 — Constructive Conditions: Material Breach and Anticipatory Repudiation

### Reading: CB 863–879 (Sackett; Truman Flatt)
**Rule: Partial, material, and total breach (R2 §§ 237, 242).**
- **Partial breach:** The injured party gets damages but must keep performing.
- **Material breach** (R2 § 237): The injured party may suspend its own performance while the breaching party has a chance to cure.
- **Total breach** (R2 § 242): An uncured material breach becomes total. The injured party may terminate (its remaining duties are discharged) and sue for damages for total breach.

**Rule: Materiality factors (R2 § 241).**
1. How much the injured party is deprived of the benefit it reasonably expected.
2. Whether damages can adequately compensate it.
3. How much the breaching party will forfeit (its partial performance or preparation).
4. How likely the breaching party is to cure.
5. Whether the breaching party acted in good faith and with fair dealing (willful, negligent, or innocent).

**Rule: When material becomes total (R2 § 242).** Weigh the § 241 factors plus how much further delay would prevent substitute arrangements, and how important timely performance is under the agreement.
- "Time is of the essence" clauses are considered with other circumstances. They don't automatically make every delay material. Parties can, however, make timely performance an express condition.

**The self-help gamble:** If you terminate for what a court later finds was only a partial breach, your termination is a repudiation, and you become the breaching party.

**Case: Sackett v. Spindler** (Cal. Ct. App. 1967)
- **Facts:** Sackett agreed to buy all the stock of a newspaper company for $85,000. He paid about $26,000 on time. His check for the $59,000 balance bounced. Spindler gave repeated extensions and written notice. Sackett kept missing deadlines, then proposed paying over time through a "liquidating trust." Spindler declared "there will be no sale," then resold the stock for far less and sued.
- **Issue:** Was the seller justified in treating the buyer's repeated failures to pay as a total breach and ending the contract?
- **Holding:** Spindler was justified in treating Sackett's breach as total. Even though Sackett had partly performed and kept saying he was willing to pay, his performance was extremely uncertain and his failure was grossly negligent or willful. So Spindler's termination wasn't a wrongful repudiation, and Spindler recovered damages.
- **Takeaway:** An injured party may treat a breach as total and terminate when the other party's future performance is highly uncertain and its failure is willful or grossly negligent.

**Rule: Anticipatory repudiation.**
- **Rule:** A definite and unequivocal statement or act, before performance is due, that the party won't perform. "Doubtful and indefinite" statements aren't enough.
- **What counts:**
  - Saying you'll perform only on conditions beyond the contract.
  - Conduct that makes performance impossible or apparently impossible.
- **What doesn't count:**
  - A request to modify the contract, including asking for a lower price.
  - Financial difficulty, even insolvency. But insolvency lets the other party demand adequate assurance of performance.
- **The injured party's options:** treat the contract as ended, sue immediately, or wait for the performance date and urge performance.
- **Retraction** (R2 § 256; UCC § 2-611): A repudiation can be withdrawn until the injured party (a) materially changes position in reliance on it, or (b) indicates that it considers the repudiation final. That indication must be communicated (by suing, giving notice, or similar). A private decision doesn't count.
- Whether a repudiation occurred is a question of fact.

**Case: Truman L. Flatt & Sons Co. v. Schupf** (Ill. App. Ct. 1995)
- **Facts:** A buyer agreed to buy land for $160,000. The contract was contingent on rezoning, and if rezoning was denied the contract was "voidable at Buyer's option." Facing opposition, the buyer withdrew its rezoning request. It then wrote that it was "still interested" but offered $142,500 and asked if this "revision in the contract" was acceptable. The sellers declined. Two weeks later the buyer said it would proceed at the original price. The sellers said the contract was "voided."
- **Issue:** Was the buyer's letter offering a lower price an anticipatory repudiation, and if so, was it retracted in time?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment for the sellers reversed. First, the letter wasn't a clear and unequivocal repudiation. At most it implied one ambiguously. A request to modify isn't a repudiation. Second, even if it was a repudiation, the buyer retracted it in time. The sellers hadn't materially changed position (no other sale, offers, or talks) and hadn't told the buyer they treated the contract as ended.
- **Takeaway:** An anticipatory repudiation must be clear and unequivocal, a request to modify isn't one, and a repudiation can be retracted until the other side materially relies or communicates that it treats it as final.

# UNIT X — Defenses to Enforcement (Classes 30–33)

**Big picture:** Even with assent and consideration, a contract may be voidable (the victim may avoid it or ratify it) or occasionally void. Avoidance usually means rescission plus restitution.

| Defense | Core question | Key sources |
|---|---|---|
| Incapacity | Could this person contract at all? | Infancy; R2 §§ 12–16 (*Dodson*, *Sparrow*) |
| Duress | Was assent coerced by an improper threat? | R2 §§ 174–176 (*Totem*, *Kelsey-Hayes*) |
| Undue influence | Unfair persuasion of a susceptible party? | R2 § 177 (*Odorizzi*) |
| Misrepresentation / nondisclosure | Was assent induced by a false assertion? | R2 §§ 159–173 (*Syester*, *Hill*) |
| Unconscionability | Unfair process plus unfair terms? | UCC § 2-302; R2 § 208 (*Williams*) |
| Public policy | Should society refuse to enforce this? | R2 §§ 178–191 (*Valley Medical*, *P.M.*) |
| Mistake | Was there a mistaken belief about a basic assumption? | R2 §§ 151–158 (*Lenawee*, *BMW*) |

## Class 30 — Capacity and Duress

### Reading: CB 575–606 (Dodson; Sparrow; Totem Marine)
**Rule: Infancy.**
- A minor's contracts are voidable, not void. The minor may disaffirm before reaching majority or within a reasonable time after. Ratification after majority (by words, or by failing to disaffirm in time) binds the minor.
- **Traditional rule:** The minor gets a full refund and returns only what's left, with nothing owed for use or depreciation.
- **Minority rules:** the benefit rule (deduct the value of the minor's use) and the use-and-depreciation rule (deduct use plus depreciation).
- **Necessaries:** The minor is liable for their reasonable value, in restitution, not under the contract.
- **Other limits:**
  - Misrepresenting age or willfully destroying goods may restrict the right to disaffirm (courts split three ways on misrepresentation).
  - The minor must accept or reject the whole contract. It can't keep the benefits and reject a forum-selection clause.
  - Statutes make some contracts binding (insurance, education loans, medical care).
  - Emancipated minors are treated as adults.
  - Parental pre-injury releases for minors are usually unenforceable (a slight majority). Court-approved settlements can't be disaffirmed.

**Case: Dodson v. Shrader** (Tenn. 1992)
- **Facts:** A 16-year-old bought a used truck for $4,900. Nobody asked his age and he didn't lie about it. He drove it until the engine blew, and a hit-and-run driver later damaged it. He tried to return it, now worth $500, for a full refund.
- **Issue:** When a minor disaffirms a purchase, must the seller give a full refund, or can it offset the minor's use and the item's depreciation?
- **Holding:** Tennessee adopted a modified use-and-depreciation rule. If the minor wasn't overreached, there was no undue influence, the deal was fair and reasonable, and the minor paid for and used the item, then the seller may offset reasonable compensation for use, depreciation, and willful or negligent damage.
- **Takeaway:** A minor can back out of a contract and get their money back. But if the deal was fair and the seller didn't take advantage of them, the minor can't get a full refund.

**Rule: Mental incapacity (R2 §§ 13, 15, 16).**
- Cognitive test (traditional, R2 § 15(1)(a)): The person couldn't understand the nature and consequences of the transaction.
- Affective (volitional) test (R2 § 15(1)(b)): The person couldn't act reasonably in relation to the transaction and the other party had reason to know of the condition.
- Capacity is judged at the time of the transaction. The person seeking avoidance has the burden.
- **Restoration:** Unlike a minor, an incompetent person who avoids must restore what they received (R2 § 15(2)), unless special circumstances exist.
- **Guardianship** (R2 § 13): A person under guardianship has no capacity to contract.
- **Intoxication** (R2 § 16): The contract is voidable only if the other party had reason to know the person couldn't understand or act reasonably.

**Case: Sparrow v. Demonico** (Mass. 2012)
- **Facts:** At a day-long mediation over the family home, Susan Demonico was crying, slurring her words without having had alcohol, and "not thinking rationally." She had stopped taking Zoloft. She authorized her lawyer to sign a $100,000 settlement, then backed out.
- **Issue:** Can a temporary emotional condition establish incapacity to enter a settlement, and what evidence is needed to prove it?
- **Holding:** Incapacity doesn't require a permanent or long-standing illness, so a temporary condition can qualify. But medical or expert evidence is required to show that the condition interfered with her understanding or her ability to act reasonably. Lay testimony about her emotional state wasn't enough, so the settlement was enforced.
- **Takeaway:** Incapacity can rest on a temporary condition, but it must be proved with medical or expert evidence.

**Rule: Duress (R2 §§ 174–176).**
- **Physical compulsion** (R2 § 174): The "contract" is void.
- **Improper threat** (R2 § 175): The contract is voidable if (1) an improper threat (2) induced assent and (3) left the victim no reasonable alternative.
- Improper threats (R2 § 176) include:
  - threats of a crime or tort;
  - threats of criminal prosecution;
  - bad-faith threats to sue;
  - bad-faith threats to breach a contract or withhold an admitted debt.
- Inducement is subjective: did this victim, given their age, background, and relationship to the other party, actually give in because of the threat?
- **Majority rule:** The threatening party must have caused the victim's financial distress. Taking advantage of hardship you didn't cause isn't enough. Using leverage from a market change isn't duress.
- Duress contracts are voidable and can be ratified, for example by keeping the benefits without protest.

**Case: Totem Marine Tug & Barge, Inc. v. Alyeska Pipeline Service Co.** (Alaska 1978)
- **Facts:** Alyeska misrepresented the cargo (about 7,000 tons instead of about 2,000), delayed Totem, and then terminated the contract without giving a reason. It owed Totem $260,000–$300,000. Knowing Totem faced bankruptcy without immediate cash, Alyeska withheld payment of the acknowledged debt. Totem accepted $97,500 and signed a release.
- **Issue:** Can a release be avoided for economic duress when the other party withheld an admitted debt from a creditor facing bankruptcy?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment reversed. If proved, these facts are economic duress: a wrongful act (not necessarily illegal), no reasonable alternative (suing would take too long), and inducement. The release would be voidable.
- **Takeaway:** Wrongfully withholding an admitted debt from a party facing bankruptcy to force a low settlement can be economic duress that makes the release voidable.

## Class 31 — Undue Influence, Misrepresentation, and Nondisclosure

### Reading: CB 606–632 (Odorizzi; Martinez-Gonzalez)
**Rule: Undue influence (R2 § 177).** Unfair persuasion of a party who is (a) dominated by the persuader, or (b) in a relationship where it's justified in assuming the persuader will act in its welfare (a confidential or fiduciary relationship). A fiduciary relationship creates a rebuttable presumption of undue influence.
- *Odorizzi* definition: "persuasion which overcomes the will without convincing the judgment."
- **Two elements that balance against each other:** (1) undue susceptibility of the weaker party, and (2) excessive pressure by the stronger party. If one is strong, less of the other is needed.

**The *Odorizzi* excessive-pressure factors** (several, usually together):
1. Discussion at an unusual or inappropriate time
2. Completion in an unusual place
3. Insistent demand that the deal be finished at once
4. Extreme emphasis on the bad consequences of delay
5. Multiple persuaders against a single party
6. No third-party advisers for the weaker party
7. Statements that there's no time to consult a lawyer or adviser

- **Limit:** Undue influence "cannot be used as a pretext to avoid bad bargains." Regret isn't enough.

**Case: Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School District** (Cal. Ct. App. 1966)
- **Facts:** A teacher was arrested, questioned, and released after 40 hours without sleep. The superintendent and principal came to his apartment, told him to resign immediately, and said there was no time to consult a lawyer. If he resigned, there'd be no publicity. If not, he'd be dismissed publicly. He resigned. The charges were later dismissed, and the district refused to reinstate him.
- **Issue:** Can a teacher's resignation, obtained under pressure right after his arrest, be rescinded?
- **Holding:** There was no duress or menace: threatening lawful action (dismissal proceedings) in good faith isn't unlawful. There was no fraud: there was no misrepresentation, and an employer–employee relationship isn't confidential. There was no mistake: a wrong guess about future events isn't a mistake. But he stated a claim for undue influence: he was susceptible (exhausted, distressed), and the pressure hit most of the seven factors.
- **Takeaway:** Undue influence exists when excessive pressure is applied to a person who is unduly susceptible to it, making the resulting agreement voidable.

**Rule: Economic duress (California).** a wrongful act (not necessarily illegal, but done in bad faith or for a coercive purpose; ordinary business arrangements don't qualify), no reasonable alternative, and causation.

**Case: Martinez-Gonzalez v. Elkhorn Packing Co.** (9th Cir. 2022)
- **Facts:** A migrant H-2A farmworker signed an arbitration agreement in a stack of hiring papers at an after-work orientation in a hotel parking lot. He was tired and hungry, and supervisors rushed workers through the line. The agreement was in Spanish and could be revoked within 10 days. No one said signing was required, but he believed it was.
- **Issue:** Was an arbitration agreement signed in rushed hiring paperwork voidable for economic duress or undue influence?
- **Holding:** The agreement was enforceable. There was no economic duress: the employer committed no wrongful act, and he had reasonable alternatives (ask questions, decline, revoke). There was no undue influence: he wasn't unduly susceptible, so the pressure would have had to be extraordinary, and it wasn't.
- **Takeaway:** Economic duress requires a wrongful act and no reasonable alternative, and undue influence requires real susceptibility or extraordinary pressure, so rushed hiring paperwork alone isn't enough.

### Reading: CB 632–657 (Syester; Hill)
**Rule: Misrepresentation (R2 § 164(1)).** A contract is voidable if assent was induced by a misrepresentation that was either fraudulent or material, and the recipient was justified in relying on it.
- Fraudulent (R2 § 162(1)): The speaker knows the statement is false, or asserts it without confidence in its truth or knowing there's no basis for it (recklessness counts).
- Material (R2 § 162(2)): It would induce a reasonable person to agree, or the speaker knows it would induce this particular person.
  - An innocent but material misrepresentation is enough for rescission.
  - Tort damages require fraud, proved by clear and convincing evidence.
- **Opinions** (R2 § 168): An opinion is usually not a misrepresentation of fact. But it is actionable if:
  - the speaker doesn't actually hold the opinion (R2 § 159 cmt. d); or
  - under R2 § 169, the speaker is in a relationship of trust, is an expert, or is speaking to someone especially susceptible (because of age or other factors).
  - Whether a statement is fact or opinion is usually a jury question.
- **Justified reliance:** Reliance isn't justified when there's obvious contrary evidence or the parties had equal access to the facts. Failing to read a document doesn't by itself defeat a fraud claim.
- **Remedies:** The victim elects:
  - Rescission plus restitution (the contract route); or
  - **Tort damages**: "benefit of the bargain" or "out of pocket," plus possible punitive damages.
- **Fraud vs. the contract's own clauses:**
  - "Parol evidence is always admissible to show fraud."
  - A party can't contract out of liability for its own fraud.
  - A general merger clause doesn't bar a fraud claim. A specific disclaimer of the particular representation may defeat reliance (*Danann*).
  - An "as is" clause may bar claims for nondisclosure of latent defects, but not for fraudulent misrepresentation or concealment.

**Case: Syester v. Banta** (Iowa 1965)
- **Facts:** A lonely, elderly widow bought 4,057 hours of Arthur Murray dance lessons for about $29,000. Instructors trained in high-pressure "emotional selling" told her she would become a professional dancer and gave her medals she hadn't earned. When she sued, the studio rehired her favorite instructor to talk her into firing her lawyer and signing a release in exchange for a $6,090 refund.
- **Issue:** Can sales statements and a release obtained from an elderly customer be avoided for fraud?
- **Holding:** The jury could find the releases were obtained by fraud (a release is itself a contract that can be avoided), and that the sales involved actionable fraud rather than opinion or puffing, given her special susceptibility (R2 § 169). $14,300 actual and $40,000 punitive damages were affirmed.
- **Takeaway:** Statements of opinion can be actionable fraud when made to an especially susceptible person, and a release obtained by fraud can be avoided.

**Rule: Nondisclosure (R2 § 161).** Staying silent counts as asserting that a fact doesn't exist only when the party knows that disclosure is needed:
- (a) to correct an earlier assertion that is or has become false (this includes half-truths);
- (b) to correct the other party's mistake about a basic assumption, and not disclosing would violate good faith and fair dealing;
- (c) to correct a mistake about the contents or effect of a writing; or
- (d) because of a relationship of trust (fiduciaries must disclose all material facts, R2 § 173).
- **Concealment** (R2 § 160): Active steps to hide a fact (painting over damage, for example) count as an assertion. This is separate from silence.
- **Classical rule** (*Laidlaw v. Organ*): No duty to disclose. Each side protects itself. Kronman: Information obtained by deliberate, costly investigation needn't be disclosed. Casually acquired information should be.
- Keeton factors for when fairness requires disclosure: the parties' relationship, how the information was obtained, whether the fact is latent, and the importance of the fact.

**Rule: Seller's duty to disclose.** A home seller who knows of facts materially affecting value that are not readily observable and not known to the buyer must disclose them.

**Case: Hill v. Jones** (Ariz. Ct. App. 1986)
- **Facts:** Sellers of a house knew of past termite infestation and unrepaired damage. They had renewed a termite guarantee every year. They said nothing to the buyers, the realtor, or the inspector. When the buyer asked about a "ripple" in the floor, the seller said it was water damage (a half-truth). The contract had an integration clause and said the buyer had investigated the premises.
- **Issue:** Did home sellers have a duty to disclose known termite damage, and does an integration clause bar the buyers' fraud claim?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment for the sellers reversed. The integration clause can't shield fraud. Materiality, the buyer's diligence, and inducement were jury questions.
- **Takeaway:** A home seller must disclose known facts that materially affect the property's value and aren't readily observable or known to the buyer, and an integration clause doesn't bar a fraud claim.

## Class 32 — Unconscionability and Public Policy

### Reading: CB 661–676 (Williams)
**Rule: Unconscionability (UCC § 2-302; R2 § 208).**
- UCC § 2-302 and R2 § 208: A court may refuse to enforce a contract or term that was unconscionable at the time it was made. It may also limit the term's application or enforce the rest without it.
- It's a question of law for the court, but the parties get a chance to present evidence about commercial setting and purpose.
- **Williams definition:** "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party."
  - **Procedural** (absence of meaningful choice): gross inequality of bargaining power, fine print, hiding terms, high-pressure tactics, no real alternative, and the party's education.
  - **Substantive** (unreasonable terms): terms "so extreme as to appear unconscionable according to the mores and business practices of the time and place."
- Most courts require both, usually on a sliding scale (more of one, less of the other). Some courts let substantive unconscionability alone suffice in extreme cases, such as an excessive price (R2 § 208 cmt. c).
- **Duty to read:** Normally a party who signs without reading assumes the risk. But that doesn't hold when a party with little bargaining power signs a commercially unreasonable contract with little knowledge of its terms.

**Case: Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.** (D.C. Cir. 1965)
- **Facts:** A furniture store sold goods on installment "leases." Under its add-on (cross-collateral) clause, every payment was spread pro rata across all items ever bought, so the store kept a security interest in everything until the entire balance was paid. Williams, a mother of seven living on a $218/month government stipend (which the store knew), bought a $514 stereo and then defaulted. The store sought to repossess everything she had bought since 1957.
- **Issue:** May a court refuse to enforce an unconscionable installment contract?
- **Holding:** Remanded. Courts have common-law power to refuse enforcement of an unconscionable contract, even though D.C.'s § 2-302 wasn't yet in force. The trial court must apply the two-part definition.
- **Takeaway:** Courts may refuse to enforce a contract that was unconscionable when made, meaning an absence of meaningful choice plus terms unreasonably favorable to the other party.

### Reading: CB 706–738 (Valley Medical; P.M. v. T.B.)
**Rule: Public policy (R2 § 178).** A term is unenforceable if a statute says so, or if the interest in enforcing it is clearly outweighed by a public policy against it.
- The court weighs the parties' justified expectations, the forfeiture that would result, and whether refusing enforcement furthers the policy.
- Freedom of contract weighs heavily, so courts invalidate contracts only "in cases free from doubt." Declaring public policy is mainly a job for the legislature.
- **Statutory illegality:** If a statute is silent about enforcement, violating a regulatory statute (one that protects the public) can make a contract unenforceable. Violating a revenue-raising statute generally doesn't.
- **In pari delicto:** When both parties are equally at fault, the court leaves them where it finds them.
- **Other categories:**
  - Unreasonable restraints on marriage or harm to the marital relationship (R2 §§ 189–190).
  - Child-custody terms that aren't in the child's best interest (R2 § 191).

**Rule: Covenants not to compete.**
1. **Ancillary:** The covenant must be part of a valid transaction, such as the sale of a business, a partnership, or employment. A naked restraint is void.
2. **Legitimate interest:** It must protect something beyond the desire to avoid competition, such as customer relationships, referral sources, trade secrets, or goodwill.
3. **Reasonable** (R2 § 188): It's unenforceable if it's greater than needed to protect that interest, or the interest is outweighed by the hardship to the employee and the likely injury to the public. Check duration, geography, and activity (the restriction must be limited to the competing specialty).
4. **Scrutiny:** Employment covenants are disfavored and construed against the employer. Sale-of-business covenants get more leeway because the buyer paid for goodwill. Physician covenants get strict scrutiny because of patient choice. Lawyer non-competes are banned (Model Rule 5.6).
5. **Burden:** The party seeking enforcement must prove reasonableness.
6. **Fixing an overbroad covenant:**
   - The blue pencil rule strikes grammatically severable unreasonable parts but won't rewrite.
   - R2 § 184 lets a court narrow the covenant if the promisee acted in good faith.

**Case: Valley Medical Specialists v. Farber** (Ariz. 1999)
- **Facts:** A pulmonologist's covenant barred him, for 3 years, from practicing any medicine within 5 miles of any of the group's three offices (about 235 square miles). It had no exception for emergencies.
- **Issue:** Is a physician's covenant not to compete enforceable?
- **Holding:** Unenforceable. The group had a legitimate interest in referral sources, but not in "owning" patients. 3 years was too long. Patients see a pulmonologist about every 6 months, so a much shorter period would protect the group. "Any medicine" was too broad, and the area was too large. Patients' right to choose their doctor outweighed the group's interest. The court won't rewrite the covenant, and blue-penciling couldn't save it.
- **Takeaway:** A non-compete is unenforceable if it is broader than needed to protect a legitimate interest or if that interest is outweighed by hardship and injury to the public, and courts won't rewrite it.

**Rule: Freedom of contract and public policy.** Courts invalidate a contract on public policy grounds only in cases free from doubt, given the weighty societal interest in freedom of contract.

**Case: P.M. v. T.B.** (Iowa 2018)
- **Facts:** An infertile couple hired a gestational surrogate. The embryos came from the intended father's sperm and a donor egg, so the surrogate wasn't genetically related to the children. She was paid for IVF costs and expenses, and the contract stated that payment was for services, not for the child or for giving up parental rights. After the relationship fell apart, she refused to surrender the babies.
- **Issue:** Is a gestational surrogacy contract unenforceable as against public policy?
- **Holding:** The contract is enforceable in favor of the biological father. No statute prohibits it. The baby-selling statute exempts surrogacy, and the 72-hour adoption waiting period doesn't apply to a non-genetic carrier. Payment is for gestational services. Public policy invalidation requires a case "free from doubt," given the "weighty societal interest in freedom of contract." Ordinary defenses (fraud, duress, unconscionability) remain available in particular cases.
- **Takeaway:** A gestational surrogacy contract is enforceable for the intended biological father because no statute or clear public policy forbids it.

## Class 33 — Mistake

### Reading: CB 743–754 (Lenawee; BMW Financial)
**Rule: Mistake (R2 §§ 151–154, 157).**
- **Mistake** (R2 § 151): a belief not in accord with the facts that exist when the contract is made. A bad prediction about the future isn't a mistake (that's Class 34's territory). Regret isn't a mistake.
- **Mutual mistake** (R2 § 152): The contract is voidable by the adversely affected party if:
  1. both parties are mistaken,
  2. about a basic assumption on which the contract was made,
  3. that has a material effect on the agreed exchange, and
  4. the adversely affected party doesn't bear the risk under § 154.
- **Risk allocation** (R2 § 154): A party bears the risk of a mistake when:
  - (a) the agreement allocates it to them (an "as is" clause, for example);
  - (b) they knew they had limited knowledge but treated it as enough (conscious ignorance); or
  - (c) the court allocates it to them because that's reasonable in the circumstances.
- **Unilateral mistake** (R2 § 153): The § 152 elements must be met (minus "both"), plus either:
  - (a) enforcement would be unconscionable (meaning severe loss, much heavier than the other side's loss from avoidance); or
  - (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake (a "palpable" mistake, so it can't "snap up" an offer that's too good to be true) or caused it.
- **Fault** (R2 § 157): Carelessness doesn't bar relief unless it amounts to a failure of good faith and fair dealing.
- **Remedies:**
  - Rescission plus restitution. It's equitable and discretionary, and courts sometimes craft creative remedies.
  - Reformation when the writing fails to state what the parties actually agreed.

**Older test (now replaced):** *Sherwood v. Walker* (the "barren" cow Rose 2d of Aberlone, actually pregnant) asked whether the mistake went to the "very nature of the thing" (rescission allowed) or only to its quality or value (no rescission). *Lenawee* called this distinction "inexact" and adopted R2 § 152.

**Case: Lenawee County Board of Health v. Messerly** (Mich. 1982)
- **Facts:** The Pickleses bought a three-unit apartment building for $25,500 on a land contract. The contract said: "Purchaser has examined this property and agrees to accept same in its present condition." Days later, raw sewage surfaced. A previous owner had installed an illegal septic system, and the lot was too small for a legal one. The county condemned the property. Neither side knew of the problem.
- **Issue:** Can buyers rescind a land contract for mutual mistake when the property turned out to have an illegal septic system?
- **Holding:** There was a mutual mistake about a basic assumption (that the property was fit for habitation and rental income), and it materially affected the exchange. But rescission was denied. Rescission is discretionary, and under § 154(a) the "as is" clause put the risk of the unknown defect on the buyers.
- **Takeaway:** A mutual mistake about a basic assumption permits rescission only if the party seeking it didn't bear the risk, and an "as is" clause can place that risk on the buyer.

**Rule: Mistaken construction bids.** A bidder can get relief from a clerical mistake (not a mistake of judgment) if the mistake is material, the bidder used reasonable care, and the owner can be put back where it was (e.g., *Wil-Fred's*). Compare *Drennan* (Class 12).

**Case: BMW Financial Services v. Deloach** (Cal. Ct. App. 2017)
- **Facts:** BMW's collection agency, unaware that BMW had won a $114,677 default judgment, settled the account for $14,000. BMW itself had failed to flag the account as in litigation. BMW discovered the error and repudiated the settlement a month later.
- **Issue:** Can a creditor rescind a settlement made through its own unilateral mistake?
- **Holding:** No rescission. BMW bore the risk under § 154(c): it caused its own error, and the other side was innocent. Risk allocation isn't limited to cases where the party neglected a legal duty. Enforcing the settlement wasn't unconscionable under § 153(a). BMW's real loss was modest (the car sold for $25,000), and lost punitive (treble) damages don't count. BMW's delay in repudiating also weighed against it.
- **Takeaway:** A party that caused its own unilateral mistake bears the risk of it and can't rescind unless enforcement would be unconscionable.

# UNIT XI — Excuse: Changed Circumstances (Class 34)

## Class 34 — Impracticability and Frustration of Purpose

**Mistake vs. excuse:** Mistake is about facts that existed at signing. Impracticability and frustration are about events after signing. Both ask the same core question: Who bore the risk of a circumstance the contract didn't address?

**History:**
- **Paradine v. Jane** (1647): Strict liability. A lessee owed rent even though an army drove him off the land.
- **Taylor v. Caldwell** (1863): A music hall burned before the concerts. There's an implied condition that the specific thing needed for performance will continue to exist, so the parties were excused. The same applies to the death of a person needed to perform.
- **Krell v. Henry** (1903): A room rented to watch the coronation procession. When the procession was canceled, the renter was excused. This was the origin of frustration of purpose.
- **Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard** (Cal. 1916): Removing gravel would have cost 10 times the normal amount. This was the origin of impracticability: performance that is possible but only at excessive and unreasonable cost.

**Rule: Impracticability and frustration (R2 §§ 261, 265; UCC § 2-615).**
1. An event makes performance impracticable (extreme and unreasonable difficulty or expense) or substantially frustrates a party's principal purpose (the other party's performance becomes virtually worthless to it).
2. The non-occurrence of the event was a basic assumption on which the contract was made.
3. The party seeking excuse wasn't at fault.
4. That party didn't assume the risk, either in the contract's language or from the circumstances.

- Foreseeability points toward assumption of the risk, but it isn't decisive by itself.
- **Market changes don't count:** A rise or collapse in the market is "exactly the type of business risk which business contracts made at fixed prices are intended to cover" (UCC § 2-615 cmt. 4). Mere unprofitability isn't enough.
- The defenses rarely succeed. One empirical study found impracticability failed in 78% of cases and frustration in 84%.
- **Other R2 sections:** § 262 (death or incapacity of a person necessary for performance), § 263 (destruction of a thing necessary for performance), § 264 (government regulation or order). UCC § 2-613 covers the casualty to identified goods.

**Case: Hemlock Semiconductor Operations v. SolarWorld Industries Sachsen** (6th Cir. 2017)
- **Facts:** A long-term take-or-pay contract required the buyer (Sachsen) to buy fixed quantities of polysilicon at fixed prices through 2019, or pay anyway. The buyer's advance payments helped fund the seller's $4 billion plant expansion. After China subsidized its own production (allegedly illegally), the market price collapsed below the contract price. The buyer stopped paying.
- **Issue:** Does a collapse in market price excuse a buyer from a fixed-price take-or-pay contract under impracticability or frustration?
- **Holding:** No excuse. About $800 million was awarded to Hemlock. On impracticability, continued market conditions aren't a basic assumption. A fixed-price contract exists precisely to allocate the risk of price swings, whatever causes them. A third party's illegal conduct affecting the market changes nothing, and holding otherwise would invite a "flood" of similar claims. A sophisticated buyer could have negotiated a price-renegotiation clause. On frustration, the contract's purpose was a stable supply at a predictable price, and that purpose wasn't frustrated. "Mutual profitability" isn't the purpose. The liquidated damages clause (the full remaining contract price) wasn't a penalty, given the seller's huge reliance investment.
- **Takeaway:** A drop in market price doesn't make performance of a fixed-price contract impracticable or frustrate its purpose, whatever caused the drop.

**Rule: Frustration by regulation.** A later regulation that bars a lease's intended use can excuse performance, but not if a serviceable use consistent with the lease remains. The party claiming frustration bears the burden of proving its principal purpose was frustrated.

**Case: Mel Frank Tool & Supply, Inc. v. Di-Chem Co.** (Iowa 1998)
- **Facts:** A chemical distributor leased a warehouse for "storage and distribution." The lease required compliance with city ordinances. A new fire code later barred storing hazardous chemicals in the building without expensive upgrades. The tenant moved out and stopped paying rent.
- **Issue:** Did a new fire code barring hazardous storage frustrate the purpose of a warehouse lease?
- **Holding:** No frustration. A later regulation that bars the lease's intended use can excuse rent. But the tenant isn't excused if a serviceable use consistent with the lease's use clause remains. A use that is less profitable, or even unprofitable, isn't substantial frustration. Di-Chem offered no evidence of what share of its inventory was hazardous. It could still store non-hazardous chemicals and food additives. As the party with superior knowledge of what it stored, it bore the risk.
- **Takeaway:** A regulation that restricts a tenant's use doesn't frustrate the lease's purpose if a serviceable use consistent with the lease remains.

# UNIT XII — Modifications (Class 35)

## Class 35 — Changed Agreements and Modifications

### Reading: CB 791–821 (Alaska Packers; Kelsey-Hayes; Brookside Farms)
**Rule: Pre-existing duty rule.** A promise to do what you're already obligated to do isn't consideration. So a one-sided promise of more pay for the same work is unenforceable. The modern justification: the rule prevents coerced modifications.

**Ways around the rule:**
- **R2 § 89(a):** A modification is binding without consideration if it's fair and equitable in light of circumstances not anticipated when the contract was made, and the contract isn't fully performed on either side.
- **R2 § 89(c):** It's binding to the extent justice requires because of material reliance on it (a focused version of § 90).
- **New consideration:** Any new or different duty, however small, is consideration.
- **Mutual rescission plus a new contract:** The parties tear up the old deal and make a new one. Courts are skeptical when it's a disguise for a one-sided change.

**Case: Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico** (9th Cir. 1902)
- **Facts:** Fishermen signed on in San Francisco for $50 plus 2¢ per salmon. At a remote Alaska cannery, with the short season just beginning and no replacements available, they stopped work and demanded $100. The superintendent signed new contracts, while saying he had no authority to change them. Back in San Francisco, the company paid the original wages.
- **Issue:** Is a promise of extra pay for the same work already owed enforceable?
- **Holding:** Unenforceable. The promise of more pay rested only on the men's promise to do "the exact services, and none other" they already owed. That's no consideration. They took advantage of the employer's necessity. (Their claim that the nets were rotten, which might have been an unanticipated circumstance, was rejected on the facts.)
- **Takeaway:** A promise of extra pay for performing a duty already owed is unenforceable for lack of consideration.

**Rule: Modification under UCC § 2-209.**
- (1) A modification needs no consideration. But it must be made in good faith: (a) an objectively reasonable commercial reason, such as avoiding a loss from unanticipated circumstances, and (b) honesty in fact, with no coercion by threatening breach without a genuine legal defense.
- (2) A no-oral-modification (NOM) clause in a signed agreement is enforceable. It lets the parties write their own private Statute of Frauds.
- (3) If the contract as modified falls within § 2-201 ($500 or more), the modification must satisfy the Statute of Frauds.
- (4) A modification that fails (2) or (3) can still operate as a waiver.
- (5) A waiver of an executory portion can be retracted on reasonable notice, unless retraction would be unjust because of material reliance.

**Rule: Duress and modifications.** A modification is itself a contract, so it's subject to duress and unconscionability. Threatening to breach unless the other side pays more can be a wrongful threat (R2 § 175, § 176).

**Case: Kelsey-Hayes Co. v. Galtaco Redlaw Castings Corp.** (E.D. Mich. 1990)
- **Facts:** Galtaco was the sole supplier of castings under a 3-year fixed-price requirements contract. Losing money, it demanded a 30% price increase, then another 30%, and threatened to stop shipping. Kelsey-Hayes couldn't find another supplier for 18–24 weeks, and a short interruption could shut down Ford's production line. Kelsey-Hayes agreed but protested strenuously. It later withheld about $2 million, roughly the amount of the increases.
- **Issue:** Was a price increase agreed to under a supplier's threat to stop shipping voidable for economic duress?
- **Holding:** Summary judgment denied. A jury could find economic duress: (1) a wrongful threat (threatening to breach unless paid more); (2) no reasonable alternative (it couldn't cover in time, and suing for damages was inadequate); and (3) adequate protest. The UCC's good faith test doesn't displace common-law duress. A modification made under duress doesn't supersede the original contract.
- **Takeaway:** A threat to breach unless paid more, leaving the other party no reasonable alternative, is economic duress that can invalidate a modification, even under the UCC.

**Case: Brookside Farms v. Mama Rizzo's, Inc.** (S.D. Tex. 1995)
- **Facts:** A written basil requirements contract had a 91,000-lb minimum and set prices of $3.80 per lb in season and $5.00 off season. It had a NOM clause and a no-waiver clause. The buyer orally asked for more stem removal at higher prices and promised to note the changes on its copy of the contract. The new prices then appeared on purchase orders, invoices, and checks, and the buyer paid them without protest. Later the buyer refused to pay for 3,041 lbs and refused to buy the minimum.
- **Issue:** Is an oral modification enforceable despite a no-oral-modification clause?
- **Holding:** The oral modification was enforceable despite the NOM and no-waiver clauses. The buyer was estopped: The buyer had promised to put the changes in writing, and Brookside reasonably relied. Under § 2-201(3)(c), the Statute of Frauds (and the private one created by the NOM clause) is satisfied for goods received and accepted. The buyer was the one that materially breached.
- **Takeaway:** An oral modification can be enforced despite a no-oral-modification clause through estoppel, waiver, or the received-and-accepted exception to the Statute of Frauds.

**Modification checklist:**
1. Common law or UCC?
2. **Common law:** Is there new consideration? If not, does § 89(a) (unanticipated circumstances, fair and equitable) or § 89(c) (reliance) apply?
3. **UCC:** Was it made in good faith? Was there a NOM clause, and is the Statute of Frauds satisfied? If not, does waiver or estoppel save it?
4. **Either:** Was it coerced? Check duress (wrongful threat, no reasonable alternative, protest).
5. **Parol evidence is no obstacle:** A later modification isn't barred by the PER.

## Must-know tests

Formation
- **Offer (R2 § 24):** Would a reasonable person believe their assent alone concludes the deal? Ads and quotes usually aren't offers, unless clear, definite, and nothing left to negotiate (*Izadi*, *Sateriale*).
- **Objective theory (Lucy, Ray):** reasonable meaning of words and acts. Secret intent and secret jokes don't matter.
- **Termination:** rejection or counteroffer, lapse, revocation (including indirect revocation, *Normile*), death or incapacity.
- **Unilateral contracts (R2 § 45):** beginning performance creates an option (*Cook*).
- **UCC formation (§ 2-204, § 2-207):** formation by conduct; gaps filled by the Code (*Harlow & Jones*).
- **Indefiniteness:** An agreement to agree on a material term fails at common law (*Walker*). The UCC fills gaps if the parties intended a contract.
- **Online:** clickwrap is generally fine. Browsewrap requires actual or constructive notice (*Long*). Shrinkwrap requires notice of terms and of how to reject (*DeFontes*).

Consideration
- **Bargained for (R2 § 71):** sought by the promisor in exchange. A condition of a gift isn't consideration (*Kirksey*, *Plowman*).
- Legal detriment suffices (*Hamer*). Adequacy isn't reviewed (*Batsakis*), unless it's grossly inadequate plus unfair circumstances (*Dohrmann*).
- Recitals don't create consideration (*Dougherty*, *Berryman*), except the R2 § 87(1) option rule.
- Past consideration isn't consideration (*Mills*), except under the material benefit rule (R2 § 86, *Webb*).

Promissory estoppel (R2 § 90)
- Promise + reasonably expected reliance + actual reliance + injustice without enforcement. The remedy can be limited to reliance damages.
- Family (*Greiner*, *Harvey*, *Wright*), employment (*Katz*), charity (*King*; § 90(2)), lending (*Aceves*), negotiations (*Pop's*).
- **Bids:** *Baird* (no estoppel for bargain offers) vs. *Drennan* (implied promise to hold the bid open; R2 § 87(2)).

Restitution
- Benefit conferred + knowledge and retention + unjust to keep without paying. No recovery for volunteers. Emergencies are the exception (*Pelo*). An owner who paid the general contractor isn't enriched (*Commerce*).

Statute of Frauds
- MY LEGS. Writing signed by the party to be charged. Writings may be pieced together (*Crabtree*).
- **Escapes:** part performance for land (*Beaver*); § 139 reliance (*Rice*); restitution.
- **UCC § 2-201:** a quantity term is required; merchant's confirmation; specially manufactured goods; admission; payment or goods accepted (*Buffaloe*).

Implied terms and good faith
- Implied reasonable/best efforts in exclusive deals (*Wood*; UCC § 2-306).
- Reasonable notice to end an indefinite contract (*Leibel*; UCC § 2-309(3)).
- **Satisfaction clauses:** objective for commercial fitness, subjective (honest) for personal taste (*Morin*).
- **Good faith (R2 § 205):** applies to discretion. It can't override express terms. No bad-faith rejection (*Locke*). No firing to avoid paying commissions (*Geysen*).
- **Warranties:** express (§ 2-313), merchantability (§ 2-314, merchant only), fitness for a particular purpose (§ 2-315, reliance on seller).

Interpretation and the PER (ambiguity)
- **Textualism:** four corners, patent ambiguity only. Contextualism: consider evidence first, then ask whether the term is reasonably susceptible to the proposed meaning (*Taylor*; R2 § 214(c)).
- **R2 § 201:** a shared meaning controls. Otherwise the innocent party's meaning controls. If neither side knew or should have known the other's meaning, no contract.
- **Frigaliment's evidence:** text → negotiations → trade usage (it binds a newcomer only if they knew it or it was notorious) → regulations → price → course of performance. The party claiming the narrower meaning bears the burden.
- **Reasonable expectations (*C & J*; R2 § 211(3)):** In form contracts, honor the adherent's objectively reasonable expectations. A term the drafter has reason to believe the adherent wouldn't accept isn't part of the deal.

Integration prong of the PER
- **Complete integration:** no contradicting and no adding. Partial: no contradicting, but consistent additional terms may be added.
- Is it integrated? Classical: four corners, and a facially complete writing is conclusive (*Thompson*). Modern: all circumstances, and a merger clause isn't decisive (R2 § 210 cmt. b).
- **Collateral term tests:** a distinct subject (*Thompson*); not ordinarily expected in the writing (*Mitchill*'s 3 conditions); naturally omitted (R2 § 216(2)); certainly would have been included (UCC § 2-202 cmt. 3, the most permissive).
- **Never barred:** interpretation, later agreements, an oral condition precedent, invalidity (fraud, duress, mistake), and reformation.
- **Fraud split:** the broad view admits it even against a merger clause (R2 § 214(d)). The narrow view bars fraud claims that contradict the writing (*Sherrodd*).
- **UCC:** usage and course of performance can cut down but not negate an express term (*Nanakuli*).

Conditions and breach
- **Express condition:** strict compliance (*Oppenheimer*). Excuses: forfeiture (§ 229, *J.N.A.*), waiver (§ 84), modification, prevention (§ 245). If in doubt, read the term as a promise (§ 227).
- **Constructive condition:** substantial performance is enough (*Jacob & Youngs*). Damages are cost to complete, or difference in value if cost is grossly disproportionate. Willfulness cuts against the breaching party (§ 241(e); *Roudis*).
- **Order:** simultaneous if possible (§ 234(1)). Otherwise the longer performance goes first (§ 234(2)).
- Material breach (§ 241 factors) lets the other side suspend. Total breach (§ 242) lets it terminate. Terminating wrongly makes you the breaching party (*Sackett*).
- **Anticipatory repudiation:** must be definite and unequivocal. A modification request doesn't count (*Flatt*). It can be retracted until the other side materially relies or communicates that it treats the repudiation as final (§ 256; § 2-611).

Defenses
- **Minors:** may disaffirm. Traditional rule is a full refund. Modern trend is an offset for use (*Dodson*). Minors are liable for necessaries in restitution.
- **Mental incapacity:** cognitive test, or affective test (plus the other side's reason to know). Judged at the time of contracting. Medical evidence required (*Sparrow*).
- **Duress (§ 175):** improper threat + no reasonable alternative + inducement. Bad-faith withholding of an admitted debt (*Totem*), or a threat to breach unless paid more (*Kelsey-Hayes*), qualifies.
- **Undue influence (§ 177):** susceptibility + excessive pressure (the 7 *Odorizzi* factors). It's not available to escape a bad bargain.
- **Misrepresentation (§ 164):** fraudulent or material + induced assent + justified reliance. Opinion counts if the speaker doesn't hold it, or the speaker is a fiduciary or expert, or the hearer is especially susceptible (§§ 159, 169; *Syester*). Nondisclosure counts under § 161(a)–(d), and home sellers must disclose known latent defects (*Hill*). A merger clause never shields fraud.
- **Unconscionability:** procedural + substantive, on a sliding scale, judged at formation (*Williams*; § 2-302; § 208).
- **Public policy:** non-competes must be ancillary, protect a legitimate interest, and be reasonable in time, area, and activity (§ 188; *Valley Medical*). Courts invalidate only in cases "free from doubt" (*P.M.*).
- **Mutual mistake (§ 152):** a basic assumption, with material effect, where the party didn't bear the risk (§ 154: agreement, conscious ignorance, or court allocation). An "as is" clause can allocate the risk (*Lenawee*).
- **Unilateral mistake (§ 153):** the § 152 elements plus either unconscionable enforcement or the other party knew, had reason to know, or caused the mistake. A party that caused its own error bears the risk (*BMW*).

**Excuse: changed circumstances**
- **Impracticability (§ 261; UCC § 2-615) and frustration (§ 265):** an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, no fault, and no assumption of the risk.
- Market price swings never count (*Hemlock*). Frustration requires that the other side's performance be virtually worthless. A remaining serviceable use defeats it (*Mel Frank*).

Modification
- **Common law:** the pre-existing duty rule (*Alaska Packers*), with exceptions for § 89(a) (unanticipated circumstances, fair and equitable), § 89(c) (reliance), and any new consideration.
- **UCC § 2-209:** no consideration needed, but good faith is required. NOM clauses are valid, the Statute of Frauds applies to the contract as modified, and a failed modification may still be a waiver (*Brookside*).
- Duress still applies to modifications. The victim should protest (*Kelsey-Hayes*).
- **UCC § 1-303 hierarchy:** express terms > course of performance > course of dealing > usage of trade.
