Contracts — Class 20: Implied Promises & Warranties (Part 1: Implied Terms & Good Faith)

1. Rules

Rule: Implied-in-Fact vs. Implied-in-Law Terms

  • Source: Textbook/casebook narrative, Chapter 6 introduction

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: A term may be "implied" in one of two senses — implied-in-fact, meaning the court finds it implicit in the parties' own words or conduct even though not literally expressed, or implied-in-law, meaning the court imposes the term by operation of a statute, common-law precedent, or its own judgment that implication is appropriate in the case, regardless of whether the parties actually agreed to it

  • Legal Question: What is the source and nature of a "term" that is part of an enforceable contract but was never expressly stated by the parties?

  • Requirements:

    1. Implied-in-fact — evidence that the parties' words, conduct, or the structure of their bargain support an inference that they intended the term

    2. Implied-in-law — a statute, precedent, or judicial policy determination justifying imposition of the term irrespective of actual party intent

  • Application: Courts use this framework to decide whether a missing term should be supplied at all, and if so, whether the source of authority is the parties' own (broadly construed) agreement or an external rule of law

  • Exceptions: The casebook cautions that the line between the two categories is often not easy to draw, since an implied-by-law term is frequently justified partly on the ground that it is consistent with what the parties probably intended

 

Rule: Wood v. Lucy — Implied Promise of Reasonable Efforts to Avoid an Illusory Contract

  • Source: Case holding, Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, 222 N.Y. 88 (1917)

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Where an agreement grants one party an exclusive right or exclusive agency and makes the other party's sole compensation dependent entirely on the first party's efforts, a promise to use reasonable efforts to bring about the contemplated results will be implied, because without it the writing would lack the "business efficacy" both parties must have intended

  • Legal Question: Does an agreement that does not expressly bind one party to any performance fail for lack of consideration/mutuality, or can a promise be implied to prevent that result?

  • Requirements:

    1. An exclusive grant or exclusive agency running from one party to the other

    2. Compensation to the granting party structured so that it can only materialize through the other party's efforts (e.g., a share of profits/revenues generated)

    3. Other terms of the writing (recitals, ancillary promises such as duties to account or obtain patents/trademarks) consistent with the existence of an underlying duty to perform

  • Application: Courts invoke this rule when a defendant argues that a contract is illusory or unenforceable because the plaintiff made no express binding promise, particularly in exclusive dealing, agency, or distribution arrangements

  • Exceptions: The rule does not apply where the parties have expressly and clearly provided that a party's efforts are optional, since courts will not imply a term contrary to an express agreement; the casebook notes debate (Eisenberg) over whether even such "illusory" arrangements should sometimes be enforced as a bargained-for "chance"

 

Rule: UCC § 2-306(2) — Best Efforts in Exclusive Dealing Contracts

  • Source: UCC statutory provision, discussed in notes following Wood v. Lucy

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: A contract for exclusive dealing in goods imposes, unless otherwise agreed, an obligation by the seller to use best efforts to supply the goods and by the buyer to use best efforts to promote their sale

  • Legal Question: What obligation does a party under a sale-of-goods exclusive dealing arrangement bear once the arrangement is deemed "exclusive"?

  • Requirements:

    1. A contract for sale falling within Article 2

    2. An "exclusive dealing" arrangement between buyer and seller (not necessarily unqualifiedly exclusive on both sides — courts have found sufficient exclusivity even where certain other relationships or minimum-purchase carve-outs exist)

  • Application: Courts apply this provision to imply a best-efforts obligation and thereby defeat challenges that an exclusive dealing contract is illusory or unenforceable for indefiniteness/lack of mutuality

  • Exceptions: Applies only where the relationship is sufficiently "exclusive"; courts have split on how much exclusivity is required, but arrangements permitting some carve-outs (e.g., MDC Corp., Tigg Corp.) have still qualified

 

Rule: Interpretation of "Best Efforts" and "Reasonable Efforts" Clauses

  • Source: Textbook narrative summarizing case law, Notes following Wood v. Lucy

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Courts differ on the meaning of "best efforts," with a minority refusing to enforce such clauses as too vague, some treating them as equivalent to good faith, some imposing fiduciary-like obligations, but most defining "best efforts" in terms of reasonableness and diligence measured against the party's abilities, means, and the other party's expectations

  • Legal Question: Do "best efforts" and "reasonable efforts" clauses impose the same or different levels of obligation, and how is the standard measured?

  • Requirements: Not a fixed test — application depends on jurisdiction, but the prevailing formulation requires (1) good faith, (2) action in light of one's own capabilities and means, and (3) pursuit of all reasonable methods

  • Application: Used when a party is sued for allegedly failing to satisfy an express or implied efforts clause, to determine whether the effort exerted was legally adequate

  • Exceptions: A minority of courts still refuse to enforce "best efforts" clauses at all on vagueness grounds; Farnsworth's view (cited in the notes) treats "best efforts" as more exacting than good faith, whose core is honesty and fairness

 

Rule: UCC § 2-309(2) — Duration and Termination of Contracts for Successive Performances

  • Source: UCC statutory provision, applied in Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Where a contract provides for successive performances but is indefinite in duration, it is valid for a reasonable time, but unless otherwise agreed it may be terminated at any time by either party

  • Legal Question: How long does an indefinite-duration, ongoing sale-of-goods relationship last, and may either party end it unilaterally?

  • Requirements:

    1. A contract calling for successive performances (e.g., an ongoing dealer-distributor supply relationship)

    2. No agreed definite duration

  • Application: Courts apply this to distributorship, dealer, and requirements/output relationships governed by Article 2 to establish that the relationship is terminable at will, subject to § 2-309(3)'s notice requirement

  • Exceptions: Displaced if the parties have otherwise agreed on a fixed duration or termination mechanism

 

Rule: UCC § 2-309(3) — Reasonable Notification Required to Terminate

  • Source: UCC statutory provision (KRS 355.2-309(3) as applied in Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.)

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Termination of a contract by one party, except on the happening of an agreed event, requires that reasonable notification be received by the other party, and an agreement dispensing with notification is invalid if its operation would be unconscionable

  • Legal Question: What must a party do before terminating an at-will, ongoing contract for the sale of goods?

  • Requirements:

    1. Termination not triggered by an agreed-upon event (e.g., a specified default or minimum-sales failure)

    2. Reasonable notification must be given and actually received by the other party

  • Application: Used to evaluate whether a manufacturer's termination of a distributor/dealer was lawful; reasonableness is assessed by factors such as the terminated party's need to sell off inventory, recoup investment, and find a substitute arrangement, and is generally a question of fact

  • Exceptions: Does not apply where termination occurs upon an "agreed event" specified in the contract (though courts have split on whether specifying agreed events for termination forecloses the general reasonable-notice route entirely, compare Delta Services with Viking Supply); Comment to UCC § 1-309 notes the good-faith/notice obligation has no application to demand instruments that by their nature permit call at any time with or without reason

 

Rule: Leibel v. Raynor — Distributorship Agreements Governed by UCC Article 2

  • Source: Case holding, Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co., 571 S.W.2d 640 (Ky. Ct. App. 1978)

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: A distributorship agreement whose real nature, purpose, and intent is the sale of the manufacturer's goods to the dealer for resale is a "transaction in goods" governed by UCC Article 2, regardless of whether the parties label the relationship a franchise, dealership, or otherwise

  • Legal Question: Does Article 2 of the UCC apply to an oral exclusive dealer-distributorship arrangement, or is it purely a common-law personal-services/franchise relationship outside the Code?

  • Requirements: Courts look to (1) the real nature of the agreement, (2) its real purpose, and (3) what the parties really intended (the Buttorff test), distinguishing a true sale-of-goods relationship from a personal-services/commission-sales relationship

  • Application: Applied to determine whether Code gap-fillers (like § 2-309's notice requirement) govern a distributorship dispute

  • Exceptions: Does not apply where the relationship is truly one of personal services or commissioned sales agency rather than sale of goods (e.g., Buttorff, where the distributor was found to be a commissioned salesman)

 

Rule: Historical Common-Law Unenforceability of Indefinite Distributorship/Requirements Agreements

  • Source: Textbook narrative, Notes following Leibel and the Requirements/Output Contracts Comment

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: At common law, exclusive distributorship and requirements agreements were frequently held unenforceable as lacking consideration (the buyer/dealer's promise viewed as illusory), lacking mutuality of obligation, or being too indefinite/vague to enforce, as in E.I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Claiborne-Reno Co.

  • Legal Question: Why were early distributorship and requirements/output contracts frequently struck down before modern gap-filling doctrine developed?

  • Requirements: N/A (describes a historical judicial approach rather than a current test)

  • Application: Used as the doctrinal backdrop explaining why Wood, Leibel, and the UCC's gap-filler provisions were significant developments

  • Exceptions: Superseded in modern law by (1) demotion of "mutuality of obligation" to a mere corollary of consideration with no independent force, (2) Professor Corbin's consideration theory validating requirements contracts (Restatement (Second) § 77), and (3) UCC gap-filling provisions (e.g., §§ 2-306, 2-309) that supply the missing definiteness

 

Rule: Gap-Filling / "Off-the-Rack" Default Terms Rationale

  • Source: Textbook narrative, Notes following Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Many UCC provisions (e.g., §§ 2-308, 2-309, 2-310, 2-509, 2-513) supply implied "gap-filler" terms as a matter of law unless the parties otherwise agree, justified both on fairness grounds and on economic-efficiency grounds because such standardized default terms approximate what most parties would choose anyway and reduce the cost of individually bargaining over every contingency

  • Legal Question: Why does the law supply default contract terms instead of requiring parties to specify everything themselves?

  • Requirements: N/A (a policy rationale, not an operative legal test)

  • Application: Invoked to justify filling gaps such as place of delivery, time of payment, risk of loss, and termination notice when the parties' agreement is silent

  • Exceptions: Some UCC gap-filler obligations are mandatory and may not be varied even by express agreement (e.g., §§ 2-309(3), 2-719(3); see generally § 1-302); most gap-fillers, however, are merely default rules displaceable by the parties' express agreement

 

Rule: Ayres & Gertner "Penalty Default Rules" Theory

  • Source: Textbook narrative citing Ian Ayres & Robert Gertner, Filling Gaps in Incomplete Contracts, 99 Yale L.J. 87 (1989), in Notes following Leibel

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Where contractual incompleteness results from one party's informational advantage and strategic behavior, courts should consider adopting "penalty default rules" — default terms that penalize the party who withheld information, thereby creating an incentive for knowledgeable parties to reveal information during bargaining

  • Legal Question: How should courts choose which default rule to supply when contractual gaps arise from strategic non-disclosure rather than simple oversight?

  • Requirements: Identification of (1) contractual incompleteness and (2) an informational asymmetry combined with strategic behavior by the advantaged party

  • Application: An academic framework offered as an alternative/supplement to the "hypothetical bargain" approach to selecting default rules

  • Exceptions: Contrasts with the more common "hypothetical bargain" default-rule approach (reflecting what parties probably would have agreed to), and with relational-contract theories emphasizing repeated dealings (Feinman)

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 204 — Supplying an Omitted Essential Term

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Contracts, cited in Notes following Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Statement: Where the parties to a bargain sufficiently defined to be a contract have not agreed with respect to a term essential to determining their rights and duties, a term which is reasonable in the circumstances is supplied by the court

  • Legal Question: What term should a court supply when the parties' agreement is silent on an essential matter, such as the due date for repayment of a loan?

  • Requirements:

    1. An otherwise sufficiently definite bargain/contract exists

    2. The parties have not agreed as to a term essential to determining their rights and duties

  • Application: Cited as the basis for the majority rule that a loan made without an agreed due date is payable within a reasonable time (Barnes v. Michalski)

  • Exceptions: Applies only to fill an essential gap, not to override a term the parties did address

 

Rule: UCC § 1-304 — Good Faith Obligation in Performance and Enforcement

  • Source: UCC statutory provision, discussed in Section B introduction

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: Every contract or duty within the scope of the UCC imposes an obligation of good faith in its performance and enforcement

  • Legal Question: Does the UCC impose a freestanding duty of good faith on contracting parties beyond their express terms?

  • Requirements: Applies automatically to every contract or duty governed by the UCC; no separate bargaining or express adoption required

  • Application: Serves as the statutory anchor for good-faith claims regarding performance of sale-of-goods contracts (price-setting, requirements/output quantity adjustments, exercise of discretion, etc.)

  • Exceptions: Per the Official Comment to revised § 1-304, the section does not itself create an independent cause of action for failure to perform or enforce in good faith; it directs courts toward interpreting contracts in their commercial context rather than creating a separately breachable duty of fairness

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 205 — Duty of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 205

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: Every contract imposes upon each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and enforcement, with good faith performance emphasizing faithfulness to an agreed common purpose and consistency with the justified expectations of the other party

  • Legal Question: What is the general common-law (non-UCC) source of the implied duty of good faith?

  • Requirements: Applies to "every contract," without need for express incorporation

  • Application: Cited by courts (e.g., in Seidenberg) as the doctrinal parallel to UCC § 1-304 for non-sale-of-goods contracts

  • Exceptions: Comment a limits the doctrine's aim to faithfulness to the agreed common purpose and the other party's justified expectations, not an open-ended fairness override

 

Rule: UCC § 1-201(b)(20) — Definition of Good Faith

  • Source: UCC statutory definition, Article 1 (revised 2001)

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: Good faith means honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing

  • Legal Question: What substantive content does "good faith" have under the UCC?

  • Requirements:

    1. Subjective honesty in fact

    2. Objective observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing

  • Application: Courts apply both prongs together when assessing a party's conduct in performing or enforcing a sale-of-goods contract (e.g., price-setting, requirements/output adjustments)

  • Exceptions: Not all states have adopted this expanded, unified definition; some jurisdictions retain the older, more minimal "honesty in fact" standard, at least for non-merchant parties, and Article 5 retains a narrower provision

 

Rule: "Fruit of the Contract" Concept of Good Faith

  • Source: Case holding, Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co., 188 N.E. 163 (N.Y. 1933), and commentators (Burton, Patterson)

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: In every contract there is an implied covenant that neither party shall do anything which will have the effect of destroying or injuring the right of the other party to receive the fruits of the contract, which is what is meant by the implied obligation of good faith and fair dealing

  • Legal Question: What conduct does the implied covenant of good faith actually prohibit?

  • Requirements: A party's conduct must be assessed against whether it destroys or injures the other party's ability to receive the benefits reasonably expected under the contract

  • Application: Widely adopted formulation courts use to frame good-faith claims; Professor Burton's related theory frames bad faith as recapturing "foregone opportunities" the party should have understood were precluded by the contract in light of applicable business practices or course of dealing

  • Exceptions: Some courts (per Van Alstine's study) have moved toward a "new textualist" approach giving near-absolute priority to express terms, rendering the implied good-faith duty largely irrelevant in many disputes

 

Rule: Seidenberg's Three Applications of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith

  • Source: Case holding, Seidenberg v. Summit Bank, 348 N.J. Super. 243 (App. Div. 2002)

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing has been applied in three general ways: (1) to include terms and conditions necessary to give the contract "business efficacy" that were not expressly set forth, (2) to allow redress for bad-faith performance of an agreement even when no express term has been breached, and (3) to permit inquiry into a party's exercise of discretion expressly granted by the contract's terms

  • Legal Question: In what distinct doctrinal situations can a good-faith claim arise?

  • Requirements: Plaintiff must show the claim fits one (or more) of the three categories and, in the latter two, must show bad faith or improper motive

  • Application: Courts use this taxonomy to sort good-faith claims (e.g., Bak-A-Lum and Onderdonk as gap-filling; Sons of Thunder as bad-faith performance; Wilson and R.J. Gaydos as discretion-policing)

  • Exceptions: None of the three applications may be used to override or contradict an express term of the contract

 

Rule: Good Faith Cannot Override an Express Contract Term

  • Source: Case holding, Wilson v. Amerada Hess Corp., 168 N.J. 236 (2001), and Sons of Thunder, Inc. v. Borden, Inc., 690 A.2d 575 (N.J. 1997), both applied in Seidenberg v. Summit Bank

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing cannot override an express term in a contract, but it can still require that a contracting party act in good faith when exercising discretion in performing its obligations or when exercising an express right to terminate

  • Legal Question: Can a party be liable for breach of the implied covenant even though it complied with every express term of the contract, including an express termination right?

  • Requirements:

    1. The defendant's challenged conduct must not directly contradict an express term

    2. The defendant must have exercised some discretion, or engaged in performance conduct, in a manner that defeats the plaintiff's reasonable contractual expectations

  • Application: Applied where a party (like Borden in Sons of Thunder) validly terminates under an express clause but is alleged to have acted in bad faith in its performance leading up to termination

  • Exceptions: If the plaintiff's claim in substance seeks to contradict or nullify an express term (rather than to police discretion or performance around it), the claim fails; courts remain divided on how strictly to police this line (see Beidel v. Sideline Software; Humantech v. Caterpillar)

 

Rule: Parol Evidence Rule Does Not Bar Good-Faith Claims

  • Source: Case holding, Seidenberg v. Summit Bank

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: Because the covenant of good faith and fair dealing is implied by operation of law in every contract, the parol evidence rule — which bars evidence of oral promises that tend to alter or vary an integrated written instrument — does not inhibit a plaintiff's ability to introduce evidence of the parties' expectations and purposes to establish a breach of that covenant

  • Legal Question: Does a plaintiff's reliance on facts and discussions outside the four corners of an integrated written contract, to prove a good-faith breach, run afoul of the parol evidence rule?

  • Requirements: The plaintiff must not be using the extrinsic evidence to contradict or add an express term to the contract, but rather to show the parties' reasonable expectations and purposes relevant to a duty implied by law

  • Application: Used to reject a trial court's dismissal of a good-faith claim on parol evidence grounds

  • Exceptions: The parol evidence rule may still apply, and could bar a claim, where the plaintiff's proffered evidence would directly contradict an express term of the writing

 

Rule: Bad Faith / Improper Motive Requirement

  • Source: Case holding, Wilson v. Amerada Hess Corp., as adopted and applied in Seidenberg v. Summit Bank

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: A party exercising discretion granted by a contract breaches the duty of good faith and fair dealing only if it exercises that discretion arbitrarily, unreasonably, or capriciously with the objective of preventing the other party from receiving its reasonably expected fruits under the contract; merely negligent, arbitrary, or unreasonable conduct causing economic disadvantage, without improper motive, is not a breach

  • Legal Question: What level of culpability must a plaintiff show to establish a breach of the implied covenant of good faith?

  • Requirements:

    1. Improper motive or bad faith intent aimed at depriving the other party of the fruits of the bargain

    2. Not satisfied by mere negligence, poor judgment, or unreasonableness alone

  • Application: Applied to price-setting discretion cases (Wilson; Mathis v. Exxon) and to performance/termination cases (Seidenberg; Deom v. Walgreen)

  • Exceptions: The exact contours of "bad faith" are deliberately left flexible and fact-specific (a question for the trier of fact), to avoid the covenant becoming an "all-embracing" override of freely negotiated contract terms

 

Rule: Unequal Bargaining Power Is a Factor, Not the Sine Qua Non

  • Source: Case holding, Sons of Thunder, Inc. v. Borden, Inc., discussed and applied in Seidenberg v. Summit Bank

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: While a plaintiff's unequal bargaining power or financial vulnerability may be a prominent feature supporting a good-faith claim, it is merely one factor among many to be considered and is not required to sustain such a claim; sophisticated parties represented by competent counsel may still bring a viable good-faith claim

  • Legal Question: Must a plaintiff show it was the economically weaker or less sophisticated party to prevail on an implied covenant claim?

  • Requirements: None mandatory — bargaining power/sophistication is weighed alongside other circumstances (expectations, discretion, bad faith) rather than serving as a threshold requirement

  • Application: Used in Seidenberg to reject the trial court's reliance on the plaintiffs' sophistication and equal bargaining power as a basis for dismissal

  • Exceptions: In cases like Sons of Thunder and Bak-A-Lum, disparate bargaining power and financial dependency were prominent and arguably outcome-relevant, so its evidentiary weight can vary by case

 

Rule: UCC § 2-306(1) and Comments — Requirements and Output Contracts

  • Source: UCC statutory provision and Official Comments, discussed in the Requirements and Output Contracts Comment

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: A term measuring quantity by the output of the seller or the requirements of the buyer means such actual output or requirements as may occur in good faith, except that no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to any stated estimate, or in the absence of a stated estimate to any normal or otherwise comparable prior output or requirements, may be tendered or demanded; the parties may also expressly build in a stated maximum or minimum

  • Legal Question: How is the "quantity" term determined and policed in a requirements or output contract, where the exact amount cannot be fixed in advance?

  • Requirements:

    1. Actual output/requirements must be tendered/demanded in good faith

    2. Any given quantity must not be unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate or, absent one, to normal/comparable prior output or requirements

  • Application: Used to test whether a substantial increase ("ballooning") or decrease in a buyer's or seller's quantity is enforceable

  • Exceptions: Displaced or refined where the parties have expressly agreed on a stated maximum or minimum quantity (Comment 3)

 

Rule: Consideration in Requirements Contracts (Corbin/Restatement (Second) § 77)

  • Source: Commentary (Professor Corbin) codified in Restatement (Second) § 77, discussed in the Requirements and Output Contracts Comment

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: A requirements buyer's promise is not illusory because the buyer commits either to buy its needed goods exclusively from the designated seller or not to buy at all, and each of these alternatives would independently have constituted sufficient consideration if bargained for separately, so the buyer's performance entails sufficient legal detriment to be valid consideration

  • Legal Question: How can a requirements contract be enforceable given that the buyer does not promise to buy any fixed quantity?

  • Requirements: The buyer's commitment must, at minimum, foreclose obtaining the goods elsewhere (exclusivity), even if the exact quantity is variable

  • Application: Used to answer the historical objection that requirements contracts lack consideration

  • Exceptions: An agreement that does not bind the buyer to any appreciable degree of exclusivity is still likely to be found invalid for lack of consideration/mutuality (e.g., Brooklyn Bagel Boys), though courts will sometimes find an implied promise of exclusivity from context (Essco Geometric; Pepsi-Cola v. Steak 'N Shake)

 

Rule: Good-Faith Reduction of Requirements to Zero

  • Source: Case holding, Empire Gas Corp. v. American Bakeries, Inc., 840 F.2d 1333 (7th Cir. 1988), discussed in the Requirements and Output Contracts Comment

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: A requirements buyer may reduce its level of purchases, even to zero, so long as it acts in good faith

  • Legal Question: Does a drastic or complete reduction in a requirements buyer's demand automatically constitute a breach?

  • Requirements: The reduction must be made in good faith (e.g., resulting from reasons genuinely beyond the buyer's control, such as loss of the underlying need)

  • Application: Majority/near-uniform rule applied across jurisdictions (common law and UCC) to test challenged reductions in demand

  • Exceptions: A minority approach (Simcala, Inc. v. American Coal Trade, Inc.) holds that a decrease disproportionate to a stated estimate is a breach under the plain meaning of UCC § 2-306(1) even if made in good faith; bad faith is found where the buyer procures goods more cheaply elsewhere or acts with intent to harm/eliminate the seller (Chemical Distributors v. Exxon)

 

Rule: Output Contract Termination Standard

  • Source: Case holding, Feld v. Henry S. Levy & Sons, Inc., 335 N.E.2d 320 (N.Y. 1975), discussed in the Requirements and Output Contracts Comment

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: Termination of production under an output contract is judged by a standard similar to that applied to a requirements buyer's reduction — bankruptcy or imperilment of the company's existence would justify termination, but mere reduced profitability would not

  • Legal Question: When may an output seller lawfully cease production/termination under an output contract?

  • Requirements: Termination must be justified by a genuine threat to the business's continued existence, not simply a desire for greater profit elsewhere

  • Application: Used to assess a seller's good faith in discontinuing an output line

  • Exceptions: None stated beyond the good-faith/necessity threshold

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 228 — Preference for the Objective Satisfaction Standard

  • Source: Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 228, applied in Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction, Inc.

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: If it is practicable to determine whether a reasonable person in the position of the obligor would be satisfied, an interpretation is preferred under which the condition of satisfaction occurs if such a reasonable person in the position of the obligor would be satisfied, rather than requiring the obligor's actual, subjective satisfaction

  • Legal Question: How should a contract clause conditioning performance on a party's "satisfaction" be interpreted — by what a reasonable person would find satisfactory, or by whether the obligor was actually, honestly satisfied?

  • Requirements: The objective standard is preferred whenever it is "practicable" to apply it, i.e., where the subject matter permits an outside, knowledgeable observer to judge satisfaction

  • Application: The objective standard is generally applied where the contract involves commercial quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility that other knowledgeable persons can judge (e.g., Morin's factory siding); the subjective good-faith standard is applied where the contract involves personal aesthetics or fancy (e.g., a commissioned portrait)

  • Exceptions: Comment a to § 228 indicates the subjective standard governs only where the agreement leaves no doubt that only honest dissatisfaction is meant; where a third party (e.g., an architect or engineer, per §§ 227 cmt b and 228 cmt b) is designated to judge satisfaction, courts show greater tolerance for a subjective standard because the third party is less likely to be influenced by the obligor's "selfish interests"

 

Rule: Morin — Categorization of Contracts for Satisfaction-Clause Purposes

  • Source: Case holding, Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction, Inc., 717 F.2d 413 (7th Cir. 1983) (Posner, J.), applying Indiana law

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: The reasonable-person (objective) standard is employed when the contract involves commercial quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility which other knowledgeable persons can judge, while the standard of good faith (subjective) is employed when the contract involves personal aesthetics or fancy, and even contract language referencing "artistic effect" will not necessarily place a case in the subjective category if the underlying purpose of the contract is fundamentally utilitarian/commercial

  • Legal Question: How does a court decide which category (objective or subjective) a given "satisfaction"/"acceptability" clause falls into when the contract's own language is ambiguous or boilerplate?

  • Requirements: Courts examine (1) the true nature and purpose of the underlying subject matter (utilitarian/commercial vs. aesthetic), (2) whether the specific language used (e.g., "artistic effect") was tailored to the deal or generic form-contract boilerplate, and (3) what allocation of risk the parties would likely have wanted (e.g., would the performing party have demanded a price premium to bear an unreasonable/unattainable standard)

  • Application: Applied to determine the correct jury instruction on the meaning of a "satisfaction"/"acceptability of workmanship" clause in a construction subcontract

  • Exceptions: Where the language and circumstances clearly show the parties intended to subject performance to the obligor's private aesthetic taste (e.g., the portrait-painting hypothetical, Gibson v. Cranage), the subjective good-faith standard controls even if the outcome seems unreasonable to an outsider

 

Rule: UCC § 1-309 Comment — Good Faith Does Not Limit Demand/Call Provisions

  • Source: Official Comment to UCC § 1-309, discussed in Notes following Seidenberg v. Summit Bank (lender liability discussion)

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Statement: The obligation of good faith has no application to demand instruments or provisions whose very nature permits call (e.g., of a loan) at any time with or without reason

  • Legal Question: Can a lender's express contractual right to call a loan "on demand" be limited by the implied duty of good faith?

  • Requirements: Applies where the instrument or provision is, by its terms, a true demand/on-call provision

  • Application: Cited by courts (e.g., Reger Development v. National City Bank) siding with lenders against "lender liability" claims that seek to restrict express demand/termination rights via the good-faith duty

  • Exceptions: Some courts (K.M.C. Co. v. Irving Trust Co.) have nonetheless applied the good-faith duty to limit even an express demand/termination right, especially where the lender also controlled the borrower's operating bank account — though this approach has been criticized by later courts as effectively overriding express contract terms

2. Case Narrative

Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon

Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, was a celebrated fashion designer in the early twentieth century — a "creator of fashions" whose personal endorsement of a product or design gave it real commercial value; manufacturers of dresses, millinery, and similar goods were willing to pay for the right to use her name and approval. Otis F. Wood ran a business set up to exploit exactly this kind of celebrity endorsement value. Sometime around April 1, 1915, the two entered into a written agreement: Lady Duff-Gordon gave Wood the exclusive right (subject to her approval) to place her endorsements on other manufacturers' designs, and also the exclusive right to market her own designs or license others to do so. In exchange, Wood would pay her one-half of all profits and revenues derived from any contracts he made on her behalf, and he separately promised to account for those profits monthly and to obtain any patents, copyrights, or trademarks needed to protect the arrangement. The exclusive arrangement was to run for at least a year from April 1, 1915, and continue year to year unless ended on ninety days' notice.

At some point after the agreement began, Lady Duff-Gordon started placing her own endorsements on fabrics, dresses, and millinery without telling Wood, cutting him out of the deals and keeping the resulting profits for herself, rather than running those opportunities through the exclusive arrangement and splitting the proceeds with him as promised. Wood believed this violated the exclusive agency he had bargained for. She, in turn, argued that the contract Wood was trying to enforce didn't actually bind Wood to do anything at all — nowhere did the writing say, in so many words, that Wood had to make any effort to market her designs or place her endorsements — so there was no real contract for her to have broken.

Wood sued Lady Duff-Gordon for damages resulting from her having placed her own endorsements and withheld the profits due him. The case reached the courts on the pleadings, with Lady Duff-Gordon arguing (by demurrer/motion for judgment on the pleadings) that the agreement, on its face, failed for lack of a binding, non-illusory promise on Wood's part.

 

Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co.

Raynor Manufacturing Co. was a manufacturer of garage doors, operators, and related parts. The appellant, referred to as Leibel, wanted to become a local dealer selling and installing Raynor's garage doors around Lexington, Kentucky. On or about March 1, 1974, Leibel and Raynor entered into an oral agreement establishing Leibel as the exclusive dealer-distributor of Raynor's garage doors within a 50-mile radius of Lexington: Raynor would sell and deliver its products to Leibel at the factory distributor price, and Leibel would sell, install, and service Raynor products exclusively within that territory.

In reliance on this oral arrangement, Leibel took on substantial financial commitments — he borrowed significant sums of money, made capital expenditures, purchased an inventory of doors and parts, rented storage and office space, hired personnel, and bought a service truck, tools, and equipment, all to build up the distributorship business. For about two years, Leibel operated as Raynor's Lexington-area dealer, but sales in the territory apparently declined during that period. On or about June 30, 1976, Raynor abruptly notified Leibel that, effective that date, their relationship was terminated — Raynor had already lined up a new dealer-distributor for the area (Helton Overhead Door Sales) and told Leibel he would now have to order any future doors, operators, and parts through that new distributor instead of directly from Raynor.

Leibel, having sunk substantial capital into building the distributorship on the strength of what he understood to be an ongoing exclusive relationship, felt he was entitled to more advance warning before being cut off and left with unsold inventory and unrecouped investment. Leibel filed a three-count complaint against Raynor; Count I asserted that Raynor breached by failing to give reasonable notice before terminating the oral, indefinite-duration dealer-distributor arrangement. Raynor moved for summary judgment on the theory that, since no duration had ever been agreed, the contract was terminable by either party at will, with no notice obligation at all.

 

Seidenberg v. Summit Bank

Richard Seidenberg and Eric Raymond were the founders and sole shareholders of two Pennsylvania-based businesses — Corporate Dynamics (formed 1971) and Philadelphia Benefits Corporation (formed 1985) — that marketed, consulted on, and sold health insurance benefit plans to employers. In 1997, Seidenberg and Raymond sold their stock in these two "brokerage firms" to Summit Bank, receiving 445,000 shares of common stock in Summit's parent, Bancorp Corporation, in exchange, and placing an additional 49,500 shares into escrow (to be held until December 12, 2001) as security against any undisclosed liabilities that might later surface. As part of the same transaction, Seidenberg and Raymond entered into employment agreements under which they stayed on as executives running the day-to-day operations of the brokerage firms, and were also slated to run the daily operations of any other employee-benefits insurance business Summit might acquire in the future. Their employment agreements expressly contemplated that Summit and the two men would "work together" to formulate joint marketing programs giving the brokerage firms access to Summit's broader market resources, subject to applicable regulatory constraints. The two men also agreed to take reduced base salaries in exchange for bonuses tied to the anticipated growth of the brokerage firms once they had access to Summit's resources and customer base.

According to Seidenberg and Raymond, that promised cooperation never materialized in the way they expected. They alleged that Summit failed to build a close working relationship between the entities, failed to create an effective cross-selling structure to generate customer leads, failed to introduce the brokerage firms to Summit's own vendors as a way to expand their customer base, failed to pursue existing relationships that could easily have been converted into new brokerage clients (which the pleadings called "low hanging fruit"), withheld information from them that they needed to give full advice on employee benefits, unreasonably delayed an agreed direct-mail marketing campaign, thwarted an agreed joint marketing campaign, and failed to tell them about Summit's pursuit of another acquisition that (they claimed) should have fallen within their own area of responsibility. Because their compensation and their sense of long-term job security (their employment agreements had a five-year minimum term and were to continue until each turned 70, absent termination by Summit) depended on the brokerage firms actually growing through this promised collaboration, they felt Summit's underperformance directly harmed their expected income and job tenure. They came to believe that Summit had never genuinely intended to grow the business together with them at all — that Summit's real plan from the outset was simply to acquire the profitable brokerage business and, eventually, find its own people to run or grow it, cutting Seidenberg and Raymond out. In December 1999, Summit did in fact terminate both men from their positions.

Seidenberg and Raymond sued Summit Bank. After the parties settled all disputes over the express written terms of the transaction and employment agreements, the only remaining claim was that Summit had breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in how it performed (and ultimately terminated) the relationship. Summit moved to dismiss that remaining claim for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.

 

Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction, Inc.

General Motors hired Baystone Construction, Inc. as the general contractor to build an addition to one of its Chevrolet manufacturing plants, located in Muncie, Indiana. Baystone, in turn, subcontracted with Morin Building Products Company to supply and install the aluminum exterior walls for the new addition. The subcontract specified that the exterior siding be "aluminum type 3003, not less than 18 B & S gauge, with a mill finish and stucco embossed surface texture to match finish and texture of existing metal siding" — in other words, plain, unpainted ("mill finish") aluminum meant to blend with the plant's existing walls. The contract also contained boilerplate language, carried over from a general-purpose form (and, for one clause, incorporated from GM's separate "Contract General Conditions" used across its projects), stating that all work was subject to final approval by the architect or GM's authorized agent, whose decision on matters of "artistic effect" would be final, and that any dispute over the "quality or fitness of materials or workmanship" would be decided strictly by the owner (GM), based on a requirement that all work be "first class in every respect."

Morin went ahead and put up the aluminum walls. But when GM's representative viewed the finished siding in bright sunlight from an acute angle, it did not present a uniform appearance — the mill-finish aluminum showed some variation and striping in how it reflected light. GM's representative rejected the work on that basis. Baystone then had Morin's siding torn down and hired a different subcontractor to install replacement siding, which GM did approve. Notably, mill-finish aluminum sheet is, by definition in the trade, understood to have "a nonuniform finish which may vary from sheet to sheet and within a sheet, and may not be entirely free from stains or oil" — meaning some degree of visual non-uniformity may have been essentially unavoidable given the type of material specified in the contract. Baystone then refused to pay Morin the remaining $23,000 balance of the subcontract price, on the theory that Morin's work had failed to satisfy the contractually required standard of approval.

Morin sued Baystone for the unpaid $23,000 balance of the contract price. The dispute centered on what legal standard should govern GM's rejection of Morin's siding — whether GM's actual (subjective) dissatisfaction, however seemingly unreasonable, was enough to justify withholding payment, or whether the rejection had to be measured against what a reasonable person would have found acceptable.

3. Cases

Case Name: Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, New York Court of Appeals, 1917, Opinion by Cardozo, J.

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Lady Duff-Gordon was a well-known fashion designer whose endorsement had commercial value; Wood ran a business organized to exploit endorsement-based marketing

    • The parties' written agreement, signed around April 1, 1915, gave Wood the exclusive right (subject to her approval) to place her endorsements on others' designs, and the exclusive right to market her own designs or license others to do so, for at least one year, renewable yearly absent 90 days' notice

    • Lady Duff-Gordon's sole compensation was one-half of "all profits and revenues" derived from contracts Wood made

    • Wood separately promised to account monthly for money received and to obtain patents, copyrights, and trademarks as necessary to protect the arrangement

    • The written agreement recited that Wood possessed "a business organization adapted to the placing of such indorsements as" Lady Duff-Gordon approved

    • Lady Duff-Gordon placed her own endorsements on fabrics, dresses, and millinery without Wood's knowledge and kept the resulting profits, allegedly in violation of the exclusive arrangement

    • Nowhere did the written agreement expressly state, in so many words, that Wood was obligated to use any effort to market her designs or place her endorsements

  • Procedural Posture

    • New York Supreme Court, Special Term (New York's trial court of general jurisdiction is styled the "Supreme Court"): denied Defendant Lady Duff-Gordon's motion for judgment in her favor on the pleadings, ruling effectively for Plaintiff Wood

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Lady Duff-Gordon sought review in the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of New York on the grounds that the agreement lacked a binding, non-illusory promise by Wood and was therefore unenforceable for want of consideration/mutuality

    • Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of New York: reversed the Special Term's order and granted judgment on the pleadings in favor of Defendant Lady Duff-Gordon, holding that Wood was not bound to any performance under the writing

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Wood sought review in the New York Court of Appeals on the grounds that a promise of reasonable efforts should fairly be implied from the whole writing, making the agreement enforceable

    • New York Court of Appeals: Plaintiff Wood is the appellant; Defendant Lady Duff-Gordon is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether an agreement granting Wood the exclusive right to market Lady Duff-Gordon's designs and endorsements, in which Wood did not expressly promise to make any efforts on her behalf, was nevertheless an enforceable contract because a promise to use reasonable efforts should be implied?

  • Holding: The New York Court of Appeals held that a promise by Wood to use reasonable efforts to place Lady Duff-Gordon's endorsements and market her designs is fairly to be implied from the whole writing, making the agreement a valid, non-illusory, enforceable contract

  • Rule: Wood v. Lucy — Implied Promise of Reasonable Efforts to Avoid an Illusory Contract: where an agreement grants one party an exclusive right or agency and makes the other party's compensation depend entirely on that party's efforts, a promise to use reasonable efforts will be implied to give the agreement "business efficacy"

  • Reasoning: The New York Court of Appeals reasoned as follows:

    • Formalism has given way to a broader view under which a promise, though not stated "in so many words," may nonetheless be fairly implied where the whole writing is "instinct with an obligation, imperfectly expressed"

    • The exclusive nature of the grant supports implication: Lady Duff-Gordon gave up her own right to place endorsements or market her designs for at least a year except through Wood, and acceptance of an exclusive agency was itself an assumption of its duties; the parties could not have intended that one party be "placed at the mercy of the other"

    • The recital that Wood possessed a business organization "adapted to" placing such endorsements implies that his organization would actually be used for that purpose

    • Because Lady Duff-Gordon's sole compensation was one-half of "all profits and revenues" resulting from Wood's efforts, she could never receive anything unless he made efforts; without an implied promise the transaction could not have the "business efficacy" both parties must have intended (quoting Bowen, L.J., in The Moorcock)

    • Wood's additional express promises — to account monthly for profits and to obtain patents, copyrights, and trademarks as necessary — would be valueless if he had no underlying duty to try to market the designs, reinforcing that his promise to pay half the profits was, in substance, a promise to use reasonable efforts to bring profits and revenues into existence

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Wood argued that the written agreement, read as a whole, fairly implied his obligation to use reasonable efforts to market Lady Duff-Gordon's designs and place her endorsements, and that Lady Duff-Gordon breached that agreement by placing her own endorsements without his knowledge and withholding the resulting profits owed to him

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Lady Duff-Gordon argued that the written agreement did not, in its express terms, bind Wood to do anything at all, so the "contract" was illusory and unenforceable for lack of consideration and mutuality of obligation

  • Disposition: The New York Court of Appeals reversed the judgment of the Appellate Division and affirmed the order of the Special Term (denying the defendant's motion for judgment on the pleadings), with costs, allowing Wood's damages suit to proceed

  • Importance: This is the canonical case for implying a promise of reasonable/best efforts to prevent an exclusive dealing arrangement from being illusory; it is the acknowledged genesis of UCC § 2-306(2)'s best-efforts obligation for exclusive dealing contracts, and it is a foundational example of implication grounded in "business efficacy" that raises the recurring question of whether such implied terms are truly implied-in-fact or implied-in-law

 

Case Name: Leibel v. Raynor Manufacturing Co., Kentucky Court of Appeals, 1978, Opinion by Howerton, J.

  • Document: CB pp 485-496

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Leibel and Raynor Manufacturing Co. entered into an oral agreement, on or about March 1, 1974, making Leibel the exclusive dealer-distributor of Raynor's garage doors, operators, and parts within a 50-mile radius of Lexington, Kentucky

    • Raynor agreed to sell and deliver its products to Leibel at the factory distributor price; Leibel agreed to sell, install, and service Raynor products exclusively in that territory

    • In reliance on the agreement, Leibel borrowed substantial sums, made capital expenditures, purchased inventory, rented storage and office space, hired personnel, and bought a service truck, tools, and equipment

    • After two years of declining sales, Raynor notified Leibel on or about June 30, 1976, that the relationship was terminated effective that date, and that a new dealer-distributor (Helton Overhead Door Sales) had already been appointed for the area

    • Leibel would now be required to order any future doors, operators, and parts from the new dealer-distributor rather than directly from Raynor

    • There was no formal written agreement setting a fixed duration for the relationship

  • Procedural Posture

    • Circuit Court (unspecified Kentucky county, Kentucky's trial court of general jurisdiction): granted Defendant-Appellee Raynor Manufacturing's motion for summary judgment dismissing Count I of the complaint, on the theory that the oral agreement was of indefinite duration and therefore terminable at will without any notice requirement

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Leibel sought review in the Kentucky Court of Appeals on the grounds that reasonable notification was required before termination of the ongoing exclusive dealer-distributorship relationship

    • Kentucky Court of Appeals: Plaintiff Leibel is the appellant; Defendant Raynor Manufacturing Co is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether an oral, indefinite-duration exclusive dealer-distributorship agreement for the sale of garage doors could be terminated by the manufacturer without any notice, or whether the manufacturer was required to give the distributor reasonable notice of termination?

  • Holding: The Kentucky Court of Appeals held that the parties' distributorship agreement was governed by UCC Article 2 because its true nature was a contract for the sale of goods, and that under KRS 355.2-309 (UCC § 2-309) reasonable notification of termination was required, making summary judgment improper without a factual determination of reasonableness

  • Rule: Leibel v. Raynor — UCC Governs Distributorship Agreements/Reasonable Notice to Terminate: a distributorship agreement whose real nature is the sale of goods falls under UCC Article 2 regardless of its label, and under UCC § 2-309, such an indefinite-duration agreement is valid for a reasonable time and terminable at will only upon reasonable notification, with a notice-dispensing agreement invalid if unconscionable

  • Reasoning: The Kentucky Court of Appeals reasoned as follows:

    • Applying the Buttorff test, the court looked to the real nature, purpose, and intent of the agreement, concluding that Leibel was a dealer-distributor buying and reselling goods, not a commissioned salesman providing personal services

    • Distributorship agreements increasingly involve substantial dealer investment, and without a good-faith/reasonable-notice requirement, a manufacturer's unrestricted at-will termination right could severely damage a distributor who has built inventory and capital investment in reliance on the arrangement

    • KRS 355.2-309(2)-(3) governs: an indefinite-duration contract for successive performances is valid for a reasonable time and terminable at will, but termination requires reasonable notification, with Comment 8 explaining the purpose is to allow the terminated party time to seek a substitute arrangement

    • Reasonable notice depends on the facts and circumstances (e.g., time to sell off inventory or find a substitute arrangement) and therefore presents a jury question, not one resolvable on summary judgment as a matter of law

  • Plaintiff's/Appellant's Arguments: Leibel argued that he was entitled to reasonable notice before termination because the parties' oral relationship was a distributorship agreement subject to UCC Article 2's implied notice requirement, and that he had invested substantial capital in reliance on the ongoing relationship

  • Defendant/Appellee's Arguments: Raynor Manufacturing argued that because the oral agreement had no fixed duration, it was terminable at will by either party with no notice obligation at all, and that the UCC did not apply to this type of relationship

  • Disposition: The Kentucky Court of Appeals vacated the summary judgment and remanded the case for a factual determination of whether the notice actually given was reasonable under the circumstances

  • Importance: Establishes that modern distributorship/dealer arrangements are generally treated as UCC Article 2 sale-of-goods contracts, which supplies the Code's gap-filler termination-notice rule (§ 2-309) and thereby resolves the older common-law indefiniteness/mutuality problems that doomed cases like Du Pont v. Claiborne-Reno; illustrates an implied-by-law term supplied directly by statute rather than inferred from the parties' actual intent

 

Case Name: Seidenberg v. Summit Bank, Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division, 2002, Opinion by Clarkson S. Fisher, Jr., J.S.C. (temporarily assigned)

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Seidenberg and Raymond sold their two Pennsylvania health-benefits brokerage firms (Corporate Dynamics and Philadelphia Benefits Corporation) to Summit Bank in 1997, receiving Bancorp Corporation stock, with additional shares held in escrow until December 12, 2001, as security against undisclosed liabilities

    • Under related employment agreements, Seidenberg and Raymond stayed on as executives running the brokerage firms and were also to run any other employee-benefits business Summit later acquired; the agreements expressly required Summit and plaintiffs to "work together" on joint marketing programs

    • Plaintiffs took reduced salaries in exchange for anticipated bonuses tied to growth of the brokerage firms fueled by access to Summit's resources

    • Plaintiffs alleged Summit failed to build a close working relationship, failed to create cross-selling structures, failed to introduce vendors, failed to pursue "low hanging fruit" relationships, withheld needed information, delayed a direct-mail campaign, thwarted a joint marketing campaign, and failed to disclose pursuit of another acquisition

    • Plaintiffs' employment agreements had a five-year minimum term and were to continue until each plaintiff reached age 70 absent termination by Summit

    • Summit terminated plaintiffs from their positions in December 1999

    • The parties had settled all disputes over the express terms of their written agreements before this appeal, leaving only the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing claim

  • Procedural Posture

    • Superior Court of New Jersey, Law Division (following transfer from the Chancery Division after partial settlement of the equity claims): granted Defendant-Appellee Summit Bank's motion to dismiss the second amended complaint under R. 4:6-2(e) for failure to state a claim, on the ground that the allegations really sought to enforce oral promises outside the written agreements in violation of the parol evidence rule

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiffs-Appellants Seidenberg and Raymond sought review in the Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division on the grounds that the Law Division misapprehended the scope of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing and erroneously applied the parol evidence rule

    • Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division: Plaintiffs Seidenberg and Raymond are the appellants; Defendant Summit Bank is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether plaintiffs' second amended complaint alleging breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in the performance and termination of their business relationship with Summit Bank stated a claim upon which relief could be granted?

  • Holding: The Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division held that the complaint stated a viable claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, that equal bargaining power is only one factor and not dispositive, that the parol evidence rule does not bar the claim, and that plaintiffs adequately alleged bad faith/ill motive to survive dismissal

  • Rule: Seidenberg v. Summit Bank — Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing: the covenant, present in every contract, prohibits either party from destroying or injuring the other's right to receive the fruits of the contract, and may be applied to supply missing terms, to redress bad-faith performance absent an express breach, or to police bad-faith exercise of contractual discretion, provided the plaintiff shows bad faith or improper motive and the claim does not override an express term

  • Reasoning: The Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division reasoned as follows:

    • The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is contained in all New Jersey contracts, mandating that neither party destroy or injure the other's right to receive the fruits of the contract

    • Equal bargaining power and sophisticated representation, though relevant, are not the sine qua non of a good-faith claim; disparate bargaining power was prominent in Sons of Thunder and Bak-A-Lum but is not a required element

    • The parol evidence rule does not apply because the duty of good faith is implied by operation of law rather than an oral term plaintiffs sought to add to vary the writing, so evidence of the parties' expectations and purposes is properly considered

    • While the covenant cannot override an express term, it can require good faith in a party's exercise of contractual discretion (including a termination right) or in its performance of obligations

    • Plaintiffs' allegations that Summit failed to develop the promised collaborative marketing relationship and effectively never intended to grow the business jointly, intending instead only to acquire and later replace plaintiffs, could establish bad faith in both performance and termination if proven

    • Bad faith/improper motive is required, but that fact-intensive determination should be resolved after discovery, not on a motion to dismiss

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Seidenberg and Raymond argued that Summit's failure to develop joint marketing, cross-selling, lead generation, and information sharing, along with delaying and thwarting agreed marketing campaigns and withholding disclosure of a related acquisition, frustrated their reasonably expected compensation and continued employment, and reflected that Summit never truly intended to collaborate but only to acquire and later replace them

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Summit Bank argued that the parties had equal bargaining power and sophisticated counsel such that any additional obligations should have been reduced to express terms, and that plaintiffs' claims were in substance an attempt to enforce oral promises outside the fully integrated written agreements, barred by the parol evidence rule

  • Disposition: The Superior Court of New Jersey, Appellate Division reversed the Law Division's dismissal and remanded the case for further proceedings, including discovery

  • Importance: Provides the leading taxonomy of the three doctrinal uses of the implied covenant of good faith (gap-filling, bad-faith-performance, and discretion-policing), clarifies that equal bargaining power does not defeat a good-faith claim, holds that the parol evidence rule does not bar such claims, and reinforces that bad faith/improper motive (not mere unreasonableness) is the touchstone of liability

 

Case Name: Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction, Inc., United States Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit, 1983, Opinion by Posner, Circuit Judge

  • Document: CB pp 496-517

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • General Motors hired Baystone Construction, Inc. to build an addition to a Chevrolet plant in Muncie, Indiana; Baystone subcontracted with Morin Building Products Company to supply and erect the aluminum exterior walls

    • The subcontract specified "aluminum type 3003, not less than 18 B & S gauge, with a mill finish and stucco embossed surface texture to match finish and texture of existing metal siding"

    • The contract stated that all work was subject to final approval by the architect or GM's authorized agent, whose decision on "artistic effect" would be final, and that disputes over "quality or fitness of materials or workmanship" would be decided strictly by the owner based on a "first class in every respect" standard, with custom in the trade expressly excluded from consideration

    • Morin installed the siding, but viewed in bright sunlight from an acute angle it did not present a uniform finish, and GM's representative rejected it

    • Baystone removed Morin's siding and hired a replacement subcontractor, whose work GM approved (though there was evidence the replacement siding produced a similar striped effect under the same viewing conditions)

    • Mill-finish aluminum sheet is, by trade definition, "sheet having a nonuniform finish which may vary from sheet to sheet and within a sheet, and may not be entirely free from stains or oil"

    • Baystone refused to pay Morin the remaining $23,000 balance of the contract price

  • Procedural Posture

    • U.S. District Court for the District (Indiana, unspecified division): entered judgment for Plaintiff-Appellee Morin Building Products Co following a jury verdict, based on a jury instruction that applied an objective reasonable-person standard to General Motors' rejection of the siding

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Baystone Construction sought review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit on the grounds that the jury instruction applied the wrong legal standard, arguing the satisfaction clause should be judged by GM's actual (subjective, good-faith) satisfaction rather than an objective reasonableness standard

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit: Defendant Baystone Construction is the appellant; Plaintiff Morin Building Products Co is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether the contractual "satisfaction"/"acceptability" language governing General Motors' approval of Morin's aluminum siding should be judged by an objective reasonable-person standard or by General Motors' actual subjective (good-faith) satisfaction?

  • Holding: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit held that this contract fell into the "commercial quality/operative fitness" category for which the objective reasonable-person standard applies, rather than the "personal aesthetics/fancy" category calling for a subjective standard, and affirmed the jury instruction and judgment for Morin

  • Rule: Morin — Objective vs. Subjective Satisfaction Standard (Restatement (Second) § 228): where practicable, a satisfaction condition is preferably judged by whether a reasonable person in the obligor's position would be satisfied, applying the objective standard to matters of commercial quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility, and the subjective good-faith standard to matters of personal aesthetics or fancy

  • Reasoning: The Seventh Circuit reasoned as follows:

    • A reasonableness requirement is read into a contract to approximate what the parties would have expressly agreed for an unforeseen contingency, not out of paternalism, and is inappropriate only where there are no objective standards available to guide the court

    • The applicable test (from Indiana Tri-City Plaza Bowl) uses the objective standard for commercial quality/operative fitness/mechanical utility judged by other knowledgeable persons, and the good-faith standard for personal aesthetics or fancy

    • Although the contract referenced "artistic effect," the building was a utilitarian factory, not intended as a thing of beauty, and specifying unpainted mill-finish aluminum — inherently non-uniform by trade definition — shows aesthetics were secondary to function and cost

    • The "artistic effect" and "quality or fitness" clauses were generic boilerplate from a general-purpose form contract (not drafted specifically for this deal), casting doubt on whether the parties truly intended to subject Morin's rights to GM's aesthetic whim

    • If a perfect, arguably unattainable finish had truly been required, Morin would likely have demanded a compensating price premium to bear that risk, and if uniform finish had genuinely mattered to GM, GM could have specified painted siding instead of mill finish

    • Deference to the experienced Indiana federal district judge's assessment of unsettled state law was appropriate, supporting the conclusion that this was a contract where the buyer could not unreasonably withhold approval

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Morin argued that mill-finish aluminum is inherently non-uniform by trade definition, that GM's rejection based on lack of absolute uniformity viewed from an acute angle in bright sunlight was unreasonable given the strictly utilitarian purpose of a factory building, and that the objective reasonableness standard applied by the district court was correct and was satisfied on the evidence

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: Baystone argued that the express contract language subjected "artistic effect" and "acceptability" to GM's own final decision and satisfaction, so GM's actual dissatisfaction — so long as honest — should control and defeat Morin's claim, warranting a new trial applying a subjective good-faith standard rather than the objective reasonableness standard the jury was instructed on

  • Disposition: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit affirmed the district court's judgment for Morin

  • Importance: Leading case interpreting "satisfaction"/"acceptability" clauses under Restatement (Second) § 228's objective/subjective dichotomy; illustrates Judge Posner's efficiency-based reasoning for why default interpretive rules should track probable party intent even while claiming not to be "paternalistic," and shows by contrast (the portrait-painting hypothetical) that a truly aesthetic/personal-judgment contract would still be governed by a subjective good-faith standard

4. Definitions

Implied-in-Fact Term

  • Definition: A contract term that a court finds to be implicit in the parties' own words or conduct, even though not literally or expressly stated by them

  • Significance: Used to describe the reasonable-efforts obligation implied against Wood in Wood v. Lucy, grounded in the structure and recitals of the parties' actual writing

  • Distinction: Contrasted with an implied-in-law term, which is imposed by the court through operation of statute, precedent, or policy rather than found in the parties' own agreement, though the casebook notes the line between the two is often blurry

 

Implied-in-Law Term

  • Definition: A term that a court holds should be "implied by law" — made part of the parties' obligations by operation of a rule of law (statute, precedent, or policy) rather than by the parties' own agreement, even broadly construed

  • Significance: Explains the source of terms like the UCC § 2-309 reasonable-notice requirement applied in Leibel, which arises from statute rather than the parties' oral agreement

  • Distinction: Contrasted with implied-in-fact terms; often justified partly by its apparent consistency with what the parties probably would have wanted, blurring the line between the two categories

 

Business Efficacy

  • Definition: The quality of a contract that makes it workable and capable of producing the practical result the parties intended, invoked (per The Moorcock, quoted by Cardozo in Wood v. Lucy) to justify implying a term without which the transaction "could not have such business efficacy as both parties must have intended"

  • Significance: The central justification for implying Wood's reasonable-efforts promise, and later echoed by the Seidenberg court as one of the three grounds for applying the implied covenant of good faith

  • Distinction: Distinct from "good faith" — business efficacy justifies supplying a missing term to make the deal function, whereas good faith more broadly polices how existing obligations and discretion are performed

 

Illusory Promise

  • Definition: A purported promise that does not actually bind the promisor to any performance, because the promisor retains complete discretion whether to perform, and therefore cannot serve as consideration

  • Significance: The defense raised (unsuccessfully) in Wood v. Lucy — that Wood's "promise" was illusory because he was not expressly bound to make any efforts — and the underlying historical objection to early requirements/output and distributorship contracts

  • Distinction: Distinguished from a promise conditioned on the promisor's honest satisfaction (which is not illusory, since it requires actual good-faith performance) and from a promise limited by an implied reasonable/best-efforts obligation (which supplies the missing binding commitment)

 

Best Efforts / Reasonable Efforts

  • Definition: Contractual obligations requiring a party to exert effort toward a specified goal (such as marketing a product or supplying/purchasing goods), interpreted by most courts as requiring diligence and reasonableness measured against the party's own capabilities, means, and the other party's expectations

  • Significance: The obligation implied in Wood v. Lucy ("reasonable efforts") and expressly codified for exclusive dealing contracts in UCC § 2-306(2) ("best efforts")

  • Distinction: Some authorities (e.g., Farnsworth) treat "best efforts" as a more exacting standard than "good faith," whose core is honesty and fairness, though courts disagree on whether "best" and "reasonable" efforts differ in practice

 

Requirements Contract

  • Definition: A contract in which the seller agrees to supply all of a given type of goods that the buyer will "require" during the contract term, with the buyer's needed quantity left flexible rather than fixed

  • Significance: Discussed at length in the Requirements and Output Contracts Comment; historically challenged as illusory/lacking mutuality, now validated via Corbin's consideration theory (Restatement (Second) § 77) and UCC § 2-306

  • Distinction: The mirror image of an output contract, where instead the buyer agrees to take all of the seller's output of a commodity

 

Output Contract

  • Definition: A contract in which the buyer agrees to purchase all of the seller's "output" of a given commodity during the contract term

  • Significance: Governed by the same UCC § 2-306 good-faith quantity principles as requirements contracts; illustrated by Feld v. Henry S. Levy & Sons regarding good-faith termination of production

  • Distinction: Distinguished from a requirements contract by which party's quantity obligation is variable (seller's output vs. buyer's requirements)

 

Mutuality of Obligation

  • Definition: The now-largely-discredited doctrine requiring that both parties to a contract be bound to some performance, historically used to invalidate agreements (like early distributorship and requirements contracts) where one party appeared to retain unlimited discretion

  • Significance: The basis for striking down agreements like the one in E.I. Du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Claiborne-Reno Co., and the objection raised against Wood in Wood v. Lucy

  • Distinction: Modern courts and commentators treat mutuality as a mere corollary of the consideration requirement with no independent force, rather than a freestanding doctrinal requirement

 

Good Faith (UCC Definition)

  • Definition: Under UCC § 1-201(b)(20), honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing

  • Significance: Governs performance and enforcement of every UCC contract per § 1-304, and underlies the good-faith limits on requirements/output quantity adjustments and price-setting discretion (e.g., Mathis v. Exxon)

  • Distinction: Combines a subjective element (honesty in fact) with an objective element (reasonable commercial standards); some states retain only the older, narrower "honesty in fact" standard for non-merchants

 

Fruits of the Contract

  • Definition: The benefits or expected gains a party reasonably anticipates receiving from a contract, which the implied covenant of good faith protects against destruction or injury by the other party

  • Significance: The organizing concept from Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co., invoked repeatedly in Sons of Thunder and Seidenberg to frame what conduct the implied covenant prohibits

  • Distinction: Distinguished from a breach of an express term — a party can deprive the other of the contract's fruits through bad-faith performance or discretion even without violating any specific express provision

 

Default Rules / Gap-Fillers

  • Definition: Standardized contract terms supplied by law (e.g., by the UCC) when the parties' own agreement is silent, designed to approximate the term the parties would likely have chosen and to reduce the cost of individualized bargaining

  • Significance: Explains provisions like UCC §§ 2-308, 2-309, 2-310, 2-509, and 2-513, discussed in the notes following Leibel

  • Distinction: Distinguished from mandatory UCC rules that cannot be varied even by express agreement (e.g., §§ 2-309(3), 2-719(3)), and from "penalty default rules" (Ayres & Gertner), which are deliberately chosen to be unattractive defaults so as to induce disclosure by an informationally advantaged party

 

Satisfaction Clause (Objective vs. Subjective Standard)

  • Definition: A contract provision conditioning one party's performance or payment obligation on that party's (or a third party's) "satisfaction" with the other party's performance, interpreted either objectively (whether a reasonable person would be satisfied) or subjectively (whether the party was actually, honestly satisfied)

  • Significance: The central interpretive issue in Morin Building Products Co. v. Baystone Construction, Inc., governed by Restatement (Second) § 228

  • Distinction: The objective standard applies to matters of commercial quality, operative fitness, or mechanical utility judged by knowledgeable third persons; the subjective (good-faith) standard applies to matters of personal aesthetics or fancy, and is also more readily applied where an independent third party (rather than a self-interested contracting party) is designated to judge satisfaction
