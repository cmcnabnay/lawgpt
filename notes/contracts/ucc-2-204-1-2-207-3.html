Rule: UCC § 2-204: Formation in General

- Source: UCC

- Document: UCC §§ 2-204(1), 2-207(3)

- Statement: A contract for sale of goods can be formed in any manner that shows agreement, including conduct by both parties that acknowledges a contract's existence. No specific moment is required, and contracts are valid even with open terms if the parties intended to create a binding agreement.

- Legal Question: How can mutual assent be established in a contract for the sale of goods?

- Requirements:

1. Agreement must be shown through any form of expression or conduct by the parties.

2. The parties must have intended to enter a binding agreement.

3. There must be a reasonably certain basis for granting a remedy.

- Application: Courts use this rule to determine if an agreement exists when the details of the contract are not fully settled.

- Exceptions: The more terms left open by the parties, the less likely it is they intended to contract, but conduct may still confirm an agreement despite omissions.

 

Rule: UCC § 2-207(3): Additional Terms in Acceptance or Confirmation

- Source: UCC

- Document: UCC §§ 2-204(1), 2-207(3)

- Statement: Conduct by both parties recognizing a contract is sufficient to establish a contract for sale, even if written records do not confirm it. The contract terms include agreed-upon terms from the parties' writings and supplementary terms under UCC provisions.

- Legal Question: How do additional or different terms in acceptance affect the validity of a contract?

- Requirements:

1. There must be conduct indicating a contract exists.

2. The terms consist of those terms on which the writings agree, plus supplementary terms.

- Application: This rule allows parties to effectively form contracts based on their conduct, even if their writings differ.

- Exceptions: If the writings expressly limit acceptance to the original terms or if additional terms materially alter the contract, they do not become part of the agreement.

 

Distinctions

- Offer vs. Invitation to Make an Offer:

- An offer proposes specific terms that, upon acceptance, form a contract; an invitation invites the other party to make an offer.

- Acceptance vs. Counteroffer:

- Acceptance agrees to the terms of an offer, while a counteroffer proposes different terms, effectively rejecting the original offer.

- Common Law vs. UCC:

- Common Law requires a mirror image rule for acceptance, while UCC allows for acceptance even with additional or different terms under certain conditions.

 

Example: Facts → Application of rule → Result

Added Hypothetical (not in the reading): A buyer orders 100 widgets from a seller. The seller ships the widgets but includes a extra warranty clause in the shipment. The buyer accepts and pays for the widgets. → The seller's shipment operates as acceptance even with the additional warranty clause. The contract exists incorporating both the original and the new warranty terms, as the buyer’s acceptance and payment indicate mutual assent. → The buyer is bound by the warranty, which adds additional obligations.

 

Important Terms

Conduct

- Meaning: Actions by the parties that signal agreement or mutual assent.

- Why it matters: Establishes the foundation for a contract even when written documents are incomplete.

Indefiniteness

- Meaning: The concept that a contract may be valid even with open or undefined terms.

- Why it matters: Recognizes the parties' intent to form a binding contract despite not specifying every detail.

 

How the Rules Fit Together

UCC § 2-204 establishes the foundational principles of forming contracts through conduct, while § 2-207 clarifies how additional or different terms interact when a contract is accepted. Together, they emphasize the flexibility in contract formation under the UCC, allowing parties’ actions to constitute agreements even with incomplete terms.

 

What I Need to Know

1. UCC § 2-204 allows contracts formation through conduct, regardless of expressed terms or completeness.

2. UCC § 2-207 states that recognition of a contract through conduct can be valid, incorporating agreed terms plus supplementary UCC terms.

3. Indefiniteness does not invalidate contracts if parties intended to create an agreement.

4. Important distinctions include Offer vs. Invitation to Make an Offer and Acceptance vs. Counteroffer.

5. Key terminology includes Indefiniteness and Conduct.
