Contracts, Class 36: Expectation Damages: Calculations (CB pp. 893–922)

1. Rules

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 346(2)

  • Source: Restatement (Second) § 346(2), cited in the casebook narrative, together with MBM Fin. Corp. v. Woodlands Operating Co. (Tex. 2009) as described in the narrative

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A party hurt by an actionable breach is ordinarily entitled to some remedy, even if only nominal damages; nominal damages are "a small sum fixed without regard to the amount of loss"

  • Legal Question: What does a plaintiff recover after proving breach if there are no actual damages, or none that can be proved?

  • Requirements:

    1. A binding contract

    2. An actionable breach, meaning an unjustified failure to perform a contractual duty

    3. No actual damages, or none that can be proved

  • Application: This is the minimum a plaintiff who proves breach will get; in MBM, $1, $10, or even $100 could be nominal damages, but $1,000 was too much; the casebook notes that suits are rarely brought just to recover nominal damages unless the plaintiff wants vindication, as in a libel case, and that winning only nominal damages is usually a "Pyrrhic victory"

  • Exceptions: The amount must actually be nominal; a sum like $1,000 goes beyond nominal; nominal damages are not compensation, so any provable loss is handled under the expectation, reliance, or restitution measures instead

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 344

  • Source: Textbook narrative quoting Fuller & Perdue, The Reliance Interest in Contract Damages I, 46 Yale L.J. 52 (1936); the drafters adopted this framework in Restatement (Second) § 344

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Contract damages can protect three interests: the restitution interest, which prevents unjust enrichment by making the breaching promisor give up value the promisee conferred; the reliance interest, which puts the plaintiff "in as good a position as he was in before the promise was made"; and the expectation interest, which puts the plaintiff "in as good a position as he would have occupied had the defendant performed his promise"

  • Legal Question: Which of the plaintiff's interests is a contract damages award meant to protect?

  • Requirements:

    1. Restitution: the plaintiff, relying on the promise, conferred value on the defendant

    2. Reliance: the plaintiff, relying on the promise, changed position, for example by paying to investigate title or by passing up other contracts

    3. Expectation: no reliance or enrichment is needed; the promise itself created the expectancy

  • Application: This framework is used to pick a measure of recovery; Fuller and Perdue rank the interests by how strongly they call for judicial intervention, with restitution strongest because it combines "unjust impoverishment with unjust gain," then reliance, then expectation; they admit it is "no easy thing to explain" why expectation is nonetheless the normal rule

  • Exceptions: Craswell (2000) and Slawson (2003) question whether the three-interest framework is useful; reliance and restitution recovery are covered in Chapter 11

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 347

  • Source: Restatement (Second) § 347 and the textbook narrative, citing Sampley Enters., Inc. v. Laurilla (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1981), Wright v. Stevens (Miss. 1984), and Sanders Oil & Gas, Ltd. v. Big Lake Kay Constr., Inc. (Tex. App. 2018)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: In a breach of contract suit, the court tries if possible to award damages that give the plaintiff the "benefit of the bargain," meaning the gain she would have realized had the agreement been fully performed; the protected expectation is the plaintiff's net expectation, which is the value of the defendant's promised performance minus the cost of the plaintiff's own promised return performance

  • Legal Question: What is the default measure of money damages for breach of contract?

  • Requirements:

    1. A binding contract and an actionable breach

    2. Proof of the value of the performance the plaintiff expected

    3. A deduction for whatever the plaintiff saved, or reasonably should have saved, because of the breach

  • Application: Expectation works as both a floor and a ceiling; awarding less is reversible error, as in Sampley, where it was error to limit a contractor to out-of-pocket expenses when its expected profit was proven with sufficient certainty; awarding more is also reversible error, as in Wright, where a $13,000 award for pool defects was error because the evidence supported less than $10,000; in the words of Sanders Oil, the plaintiff receives "only the actual damages incurred, not less, not more"

  • Exceptions: The award is limited by foreseeability, reasonable certainty, and mitigation; reliance or restitution recovery may be available instead (Chapter 11); the casebook says the law "is not always as rigid" as the general rule suggests and asks why expectation should be both the minimum and the maximum award

 

Rule: General measure of damages for total breach

  • Source: Textbook narrative quoting E. Allan Farnsworth, Contracts § 12.9 (4th ed. 2004), which explains the Restatement (Second) § 347 formula

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Damages for total breach equal loss in value plus other loss, minus cost avoided, minus loss avoided

  • Legal Question: How is the injured party's expectation turned into a dollar figure when that party terminates the contract and claims total breach?

  • Requirements:

    1. Loss in value: the value to the injured party of the performance that should have been received, minus the value to that party of what, if anything, was actually received

    2. Other loss: incidental and consequential damages, such as a broker's fee paid in a reasonable but failed attempt to find a substitute, or injury to person or property caused by the breach; this item is subject to limits such as unforeseeability

    3. Cost avoided: further spending the injured party saves because it no longer has to perform, such as the builder's cost of the remaining work

    4. Loss avoided: savings from salvaging or reallocating resources, such as reusing leftover materials or the net earnings from substitute employment; this is measured by what the injured party actually saved, even if another person could not have saved as much

  • Application: This is the formula behind the casebook's hypotheticals; the reading poses them without giving answers, and the figures below come from applying the formula to the stated facts

    • Case 1 (owner breaches a $200,000 building contract): loss in value is the $200,000 price minus the $70,000 paid, or $130,000; other loss is $0; cost avoided is the $180,000 estimated total cost minus the $95,000 already spent, or $85,000; loss avoided is the $10,000 of resold materials; damages are $130,000 − $85,000 − $10,000 = $35,000

    • Case 2 (employee wrongfully discharged after 6 months of a 2-year, $50,000-per-year contract): loss in value is the $100,000 total salary minus the $25,000 paid, or $75,000; other loss is the $1,000 agency fee, an incidental damage; cost avoided is not quantified in the facts; loss avoided is the $45,000 the new job pays over the 12 months left on the contract, since she was rehired 6 months after discharge; damages are $75,000 + $1,000 − $45,000 = $31,000

    • Case 3: see the construction contract rule below; it gives the same $35,000 as Case 1

  • Exceptions: The two subtractions, cost avoided and loss avoided, apply only when the injured party terminates and claims total breach; for partial breach see the next rule; the narrative notes the formula works easily when the breaching party owed money, and is much harder to apply when the breaching party owed a non-money performance such as building or services, because putting a "price tag" on a half-finished building is difficult

 

Rule: Measure of damages for partial breach

  • Source: Textbook narrative quoting Farnsworth, Contracts § 12.9, including the reference to the Vienna Convention

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: When the injured party does not terminate, stands ready to render any remaining return performance, and claims damages for partial breach, only the first two terms of the formula apply: loss in value plus other loss

  • Legal Question: How are damages measured when the injured party keeps the contract alive and claims only partial breach?

  • Requirements:

    1. The injured party has not terminated the contract

    2. The injured party stands ready to render any remaining return performance

    3. The claim is for partial breach

  • Application: The classic example is a buyer who accepts nonconforming goods; loss in value is the value to the buyer of conforming goods minus the value of the goods actually delivered

  • Exceptions: Cost avoided and loss avoided are not subtracted, because the injured party is not excused from its own performance; under the Vienna Convention, a buyer of nonconforming goods also has a right to price reduction, a remedy the common law does not recognize, as an alternative to partial breach damages

 

Rule: Contractor's damages for owner's breach of construction contract

  • Source: Textbook narrative describing alternative statements of the expectation measure, and the Case 3 hypothetical

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: When the owner breaches a construction contract, the builder's expectation damages are often stated as the builder's expected net profit on the entire contract plus the builder's unreimbursed expenses at the time of breach

  • Legal Question: How are a builder's damages calculated when the owner wrongfully terminates partway through the job?

  • Requirements:

    1. Expected net profit: contract price minus estimated total cost of performance

    2. Unreimbursed expenses at the time of breach: amounts spent minus amounts paid by the owner, reduced by any loss avoided such as resold materials

  • Application: In Case 3, net profit is $200,000 − $180,000 = $20,000; unreimbursed expenses are $95,000 − $70,000 = $25,000, minus $10,000 of resold materials, leaving $15,000; the total is $35,000, the same as the Farnsworth formula gives in Case 1, which illustrates the narrative's point that different formulations "should" produce identical results

  • Exceptions: This is another way of stating the § 347 measure, not a different measure; if the builder is the party in breach, the owner's measure is usually cost to complete (see the American Standard rules below)

 

Rule: Seller's damages for buyer's breach of contract to sell land

  • Source: Case holding in Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton (Mo. Ct. App. 2008), relying on Wooten v. DeMean and Leonard v. American Walnut Co.; Notes 1 and 5 following the case

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A seller's measure of damages for a buyer's breach of a contract to sell land with a structure on it is the difference between the purchase price and the fair market value of the property on the date of breach, meaning the date the sale should have been completed

  • Legal Question: How are benefit-of-the-bargain damages measured when a party to a land sale contract breaches?

  • Requirements:

    1. A breach of a contract to sell land

    2. Proof of the contract price

    3. Proof of market value at the time of breach, not at the time of trial (Reese v. Wong, Cal. Ct. App. 2001)

    4. A seller-plaintiff must show the market value was less than the contract price; a buyer-plaintiff must show it was more

  • Application: In Crabby's, the $290,000 contract price minus the $235,000 resale price gave about $55,000 for loss of bargain; in Lawson v. Menefee (Ky. Ct. App. 2004), the contract price was $265,000 and the market value $274,000, so the seller suffered no damages; in Horning v. Shilberg (Cal. Ct. App. 2005) and St. Lawrence Factory Stores (N.Y. App. Div. 2008), buyers lost for failure to prove, or credibly prove, that market value exceeded the contract price; Note 1 asks why contract price and market value are often close or identical

  • Exceptions: The contract–market difference is not the only measure; the plaintiff may also recover consequential and incidental damages, or reliance and restitution damages (The Father's House Int'l, Inc. v. Kurguz, Ohio Ct. App. 2016); when the seller breaches, jurisdictions following the English rule limit the buyer to restitution unless the seller acted in bad faith

 

Rule: Resale price as evidence of market value

  • Source: Case holding in Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton, following Hawkins v. Foster (Mo. Ct. App. 1995) and Hoelscher v. Schenewerk, and quoting Leonard v. American Walnut Co.; Note 3

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: "An essential element of the seller's case is proof of market value, and if he does resell within a reasonable time after the breach, the price obtained is some evidence of market value"; a resale about eleven and one-half months after breach was within a reasonable time

  • Legal Question: Can the price the seller later gets on resale prove the market value on the date of breach?

  • Requirements:

    1. The resale occurs within a reasonable time after the breach

    2. The resale is an arm's length transaction, not a sale the seller was compelled to make

  • Application: Conflicts in valuation evidence are for the fact finder to resolve, and the value found only has to be "within the range" of the evidence; examples include 1472 N. Milwaukee, Ltd. v. Feinerman (Ill. App. Ct. 2013), where a resale six months later properly established value, and Oltman Homes, Inc. v. Mirkes (Okla. Civ. App. 2008), where a resale 26 days later was "the best evidence of the market value"

  • Exceptions: Chris v. Epstein (N.C. Ct. App. 1994) treated a resale a full year later as irrelevant; Barry v. Jackson (Tex. App. 2010) warned that a resale more than one year after breach may be insufficient, particularly given the housing market upheaval that began in 2008, and placed the burden on the seller-plaintiff to prove the time was reasonable; a distress or compelled sale does not reflect fair market value

 

Rule: Fair market value

  • Source: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton, quoting Turner v. Shalberg, which in turn quotes Carter v. Matthey Laundry & Dry Cleaning Co. (Mo. 1961)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Fair market value is "the price which property will bring when it is offered for sale by an owner who is willing but under no compulsion to sell and is bought by a buyer who is willing or desires to purchase but is not compelled to do so"

  • Legal Question: Does a sale price reflect fair market value, or was the sale a compelled sale?

  • Requirements:

    1. A willing seller under no compulsion to sell

    2. A willing buyer under no compulsion to buy

  • Application: The court distinguished a seller who is highly motivated or "badly wants" to sell, whose sale still counts as a market-value sale, from a seller who is compelled to sell, whose sale does not

  • Exceptions: Compulsion means something like the situation in Carter, where the sale was made under a liquidation plan that had to be completed within one year under the tax code and the property was also threatened with condemnation; financial motivation alone is not compulsion

 

Rule: Owner-opinion rule

  • Source: Note 3 following Crabby's, citing RWH Homebuilders, LP v. Black Diamond Dev. LLP (Tex. App. 2015)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Courts usually let a property owner testify to the property's market value even if the owner is not qualified as a real estate expert; appraisers and others qualified by education, training, or experience may also testify

  • Legal Question: Who may give evidence of market value for purposes of the contract price minus market value formula?

  • Requirements: The owner must give a factual basis for the opinion (RWH Homebuilders)

  • Application: This is one way to satisfy the burden of proving market value; Note 3 asks what disadvantages owner testimony may have compared with expert testimony

  • Exceptions: An owner's opinion without a factual basis is not enough under RWH Homebuilders

 

Rule: Financing contingency as a condition of the buyer's duty

  • Source: Case holding in Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton, quoting Howard v. Youngman (Mo. Ct. App. 2002)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: "A provision in a real estate contract that makes the contract contingent upon the buyer's obtaining financing is a condition"; because it protects the buyer, it is a condition of the buyer's duty but not of the seller's, and if the condition does not occur, "the buyer is ipso facto excused from performance"

  • Legal Question: What is the effect of a buyer's financing contingency, and whose duty does it condition?

  • Requirements:

    1. A contract clause making the buyer's duty contingent on obtaining financing on stated terms

    2. Non-occurrence of the condition

    3. Reasonable diligence by the buyer in seeking financing, when the clause requires it

  • Application: The buyer raises it to argue that the contract terminated automatically and that refusing to close was therefore not a breach; in Crabby's, the court noted that the buyers never applied for a loan on the contract's terms ($232,000, at least 15 years, at most 5.5%), which under Goldberg v. Charlie's Chevrolet evidences a failure to use reasonable diligence, but the court did not decide that question

  • Exceptions: Because the condition exists for the buyer's benefit, the buyer may waive it and go forward with the contract (see the next rule)

 

Rule: Waiver of a condition by conduct

  • Source: Case holding in Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton, quoting Howard v. Youngman, Pilla v. Estate of Pilla (Mo. Ct. App. 1985), and Keltner v. Sowell (Mo. Ct. App. 1996); Note 2 links this to Chapter 9 (Oppenheimer)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A party may waive any contract condition that exists in its favor, by oral agreement or by conduct, including provisions that require written communications; waiver is "an intentional relinquishment of a known right," and when it is implied from conduct there must be "a clear, unequivocal, and decisive act of party showing such purpose, and so consistent with intention to waive that no other reasonable explanation is possible"

  • Legal Question: Did a party give up the protection of a condition in its favor through its words or conduct?

  • Requirements:

    1. The condition exists for the benefit of the waiving party

    2. The party intentionally relinquished a known right

    3. If waiver is implied from conduct, the acts were clear, unequivocal, and decisive

    4. The conduct is so consistent with an intent to waive that no other reasonable explanation is possible

  • Application: After the 30-day automatic termination date had passed, the Crabby's buyers signed an amendment extending closing, providing for assignment to Paragon, and taking a $1,373.54 repair credit, all while stating that "all other terms and conditions of the contract remain unchanged"; they also signed a possession rider, took a key, moved the utilities into their name, and applied for restaurant licenses; this conduct waived the entire financing contingency, not just the automatic termination provision

  • Exceptions: The intent of the party charged with waiver is controlling; conduct that is equivocal or has another reasonable explanation does not establish waiver

 

Rule: English rule

  • Source: Textbook narrative in Note 4 following Crabby's, citing In re Standard Jury Instructions-Cont. & Bus. Cases (Fla. 2013)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: When the seller breaches a land sale contract, the buyer is limited to restitution of payments made on the purchase price unless the seller breached in "bad faith"

  • Legal Question: Can a buyer recover expectation damages when a seller fails to convey?

  • Requirements:

    1. The seller breaches a contract to convey land

    2. The seller acted in good faith, for example by contracting in ignorance of a cloud on title discovered later

  • Application: The rule developed when title searches were extremely difficult and sellers often could not deliver marketable title because of defects they did not know about; Florida has long recognized it

  • Exceptions: A bad faith breach gets expectation damages; American commentators have generally criticized the rule and it is losing ground to the American rule; its appeal is that it adjusts the remedy to the breaching party's willfulness, an approach some modern commentators favor (Thel & Siegelman, 2009)

 

Rule: American rule

  • Source: Textbook narrative in Note 4 following Crabby's, citing Donovan v. Bachstadt (N.J. 1982), Beard v. S/E Joint Venture (Md. 1991), and SCI Cal. Funeral Servs., Inc. v. Five Bridges Found. (Cal. Ct. App. 2012)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Expectation damages are generally awarded for any unexcused failure to convey land, whether the seller acted in good faith or bad faith

  • Legal Question: Can a buyer recover expectation damages when a seller fails to convey?

  • Requirements:

    1. The seller fails to convey as the contract requires

    2. The failure is not excused by one of the recognized categories of legal excuse

  • Application: The buyer recovers the contract price minus market value difference, as the purchasers did in Donovan; Beard notes that a diminishing number of American courts follow the English rule

  • Exceptions: Nonperformance that falls within a recognized category of legal excuse

 

Rule: Limitations on consequential and incidental damages

  • Source: Note 5 following Crabby's (textbook narrative); see also Lukaszewski and American Standard, both relying on the contemplation of the parties

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: "Other loss" damages, often called consequential or incidental damages, are recoverable as part of expectation damages but are subject to three limits: foreseeability, reasonable certainty, and the duty to mitigate

  • Legal Question: When may a plaintiff recover losses beyond the core loss in value?

  • Requirements:

    1. Foreseeability: at the time of contracting, the breaching party had reason to foresee the harm as a probable result of breach

    2. Reasonable certainty: the amount is not speculative

    3. Mitigation: the damages could not have been avoided or minimized by reasonable efforts

  • Application: In Crabby's, the trial court's $95,547.30 award included about $40,000 beyond the roughly $55,000 contract–market difference, for real estate and personal property taxes, utilities, and mortgage interest accruing between the breach and the resale; Note 5 asks whether those items meet the three limits

  • Exceptions: Later sections of the chapter, outside this reading, cover each limitation in detail

 

Rule: UCC § 2-708(1)

  • Source: UCC § 2-708(1), discussed in Note 6 following Crabby's

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A seller's damages for the buyer's nonacceptance or repudiation are "the difference between the market price at the time and place for tender and the unpaid contract price together with any incidental damages provided in this Article (§ 2-710), but less expenses saved in consequence of the buyer's breach"

  • Legal Question: How are a seller's market-based damages measured when a buyer of goods breaches?

  • Requirements:

    1. A contract for the sale of goods

    2. The buyer does not accept or repudiates

    3. Proof of market price at the time and place for tender, with some flexibility in that proof under § 2-723

  • Application: This is the goods counterpart of the land-sale contract–market formula

  • Exceptions: Expenses the seller saved because of the breach are subtracted; a seller may use resale damages under § 2-706 instead

 

Rule: UCC § 2-713

  • Source: UCC § 2-713, discussed in Note 6 following Crabby's

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A buyer's damages for the seller's nondelivery or repudiation are "the difference between the market price at the time when the buyer learned of the breach and the contract price together with any incidental and consequential damages provided in this Article (§ 2-715), but less expenses saved in consequence of the seller's breach"

  • Legal Question: How are a buyer's market-based damages measured when a seller of goods fails to deliver?

  • Requirements:

    1. A contract for the sale of goods

    2. The seller fails to deliver or repudiates

    3. Proof of market price at the time the buyer learned of the breach

  • Application: The buyer's counterpart to § 2-708(1); note that it is measured at a different time (when the buyer learned of the breach) and allows consequential as well as incidental damages

  • Exceptions: Expenses saved are subtracted; a buyer may use cover under § 2-712 instead

 

Rule: UCC § 2-706

  • Source: UCC § 2-706, discussed in Note 6 following Crabby's

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A seller who complies with § 2-706 may recover from a breaching buyer the difference between the contract price and the seller's resale price

  • Legal Question: May a seller measure damages by an actual substitute sale instead of a hypothetical market price?

  • Requirements:

    1. The buyer breaches

    2. The seller resells in compliance with § 2-706

  • Application: The drafters believed the market measure often failed to capture the actual loss, because the injured party usually reacts by making a substitute contract at a price other than market value

  • Exceptions: Available only if the seller complies with the section's provisions, which are explored in Section E of the chapter, outside this reading

 

Rule: UCC § 2-712(1)

  • Source: UCC § 2-712(1), discussed in Note 6 following Crabby's

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: When the seller breaches, the buyer may "cover" by buying substitute goods and measure damages as the difference between the cost of those goods and the contract price

  • Legal Question: May a buyer measure damages by an actual substitute purchase?

  • Requirements:

    1. The seller breaches

    2. The buyer buys substitute goods

  • Application: The buyer's counterpart to seller's resale; it replaces the hypothetical market price with the actual replacement transaction

  • Exceptions: Further explored in Section E of the chapter, outside this reading

 

Rule: Employer's damages for employee's breach of employment contract

  • Source: Case holding in Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski (Wis. 1983), relying on Walsh v. Fisher, Lommen v. Danaher, Pleasure Time, Inc. v. Kuss, Roth v. Speck, and 61 A.L.R.2d 1008

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: An employer may recover damages from an employee who fails to perform an employment contract; those damages include "the cost of obtaining other services equivalent to that promised but not performed, plus any foreseeable consequential damages"

  • Legal Question: What may an employer recover when an employee under a fixed-term contract quits without legal justification?

  • Requirements:

    1. A fixed-term employment contract binding the employee

    2. An unjustified resignation, meaning a breach

    3. Losses that necessarily flow from the breach

    4. Losses proven to a reasonable certainty

    5. Losses within the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made

  • Application: The Board recovered the $1,026.64 more per year it had to pay the replacement therapist; Note 1 observes that courts will almost never order an employee to specifically perform, so this damages measure is likely the only useful remedy when an employee walks off the job

  • Exceptions: The rule applies only to fixed-term contracts, not at-will employment, which the employee may end freely; the employer's recovery is limited by its duty to mitigate; the employee's nonperformance may be excused, for example by incapacity; compare the wrongfully discharged employee's measure described in the narrative, where loss in value is "the excess of salary originally promised over the salary actually paid," minus loss avoided through substitute employment

 

Rule: Expectation measured by the bargained-for performance

  • Source: Case holding in Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Expectation damages are measured by what the injured party bargained for, not by the objective value of the substitute performance it was forced to accept; extra value that is "imposed upon" the injured party by the breach "cannot be characterized as a benefit" that offsets damages

  • Legal Question: Does a more valuable substitute performance offset the cost of obtaining it?

  • Requirements:

    1. The injured party bargained for a specific performance at a specific price

    2. The breach forced the injured party to accept a different, more expensive substitute

    3. The injured party neither expected nor wanted the extra value

  • Application: The court of appeals and Lukaszewski focused on the replacement's greater objective value under the salary schedule; the Wisconsin Supreme Court rejected that focus because the Board bargained for a therapist with Lukaszewski's education and experience at her salary

  • Exceptions: An employer may not hire the most qualified and most expensive replacement available and recover the entire salary difference; the duty to mitigate applies

 

Rule: Duty to mitigate damages

  • Source: Case holding in Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski, citing Kuhlman, Inc. v. G. Heileman Brewing Co. (Wis. 1978); textbook narrative and Note 5 following Crabby's

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: An injured party must take all reasonable steps to mitigate damages and cannot recover damages that could have been avoided or minimized by reasonable efforts; an employer "must attempt to obtain equivalent services at the lowest possible cost"

  • Legal Question: Did the injured party act reasonably to limit its loss, and how much is the recovery reduced if it did not?

  • Requirements:

    1. The injured party took reasonable steps to avoid or minimize loss

    2. For a replacement employee, the employer hired the least expensive qualified replacement available

  • Application: The Board searched immediately, only one qualified person applied, and it hired her with "no alternative," so it properly mitigated; in the narrative, mitigation is one of the deductions taken from the total loss; reasonable mitigation costs are recoverable as incidental damages even if the attempt fails

  • Exceptions: The reading says mitigation is covered more fully later in the chapter

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 262

  • Source: Note 2 following Lukaszewski, citing Restatement (Second) § 262, Restatement (Second) § 261 Comment d, and Baptist Mem'l Hosp.-N. Miss., Inc. v. Lambert (Miss. Ct. App. 2015); the Lukaszewski dissent

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: In a personal service contract, the death or incapacity of a person necessary for performance may excuse nonperformance, and performance may be impracticable if it would involve undue risk of injury to a person

  • Legal Question: Does an employee's illness or health risk excuse her failure to perform a fixed-term employment contract?

  • Requirements:

    1. A personal service contract

    2. Death or incapacity of a person necessary for performance, or a performance that would involve undue risk of injury to a person

    3. A factual finding that nonperformance actually resulted from that condition

  • Application: Lukaszewski argued she resigned for health reasons; the trial court found otherwise, and that finding was not against the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence, so it stood; the dissent would excuse her whether her medically certifiable hypertension was caused by the Board or "self induced"; Note 2 asks whether her feeling of unfair treatment should have to meet an objective reasonableness test

  • Exceptions: A condition that the employee's own attempt to repudiate created did not excuse her in the majority's view; if the employee quit merely for a better job, there is no excuse, a point the dissent also concedes

 

Rule: Prejudgment interest on liquidated claims

  • Source: Note 3 following Lukaszewski, citing Blair Constr., Inc. v. McBeth (Kan. 2002)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Unless the agreement provides for interest on breach, as a promissory note does, a prevailing party usually recovers prejudgment interest only if the claim was for a "liquidated" sum at the time of breach; a claim is liquidated "when both the amount due and the date on which such amount is due are fixed and certain or when the same become definitely ascertainable by mathematical calculation"

  • Legal Question: Is the plaintiff entitled to interest for the period between the accrual of the claim and the judgment?

  • Requirements:

    1. Either a contract term providing for interest on breach, or

    2. A liquidated claim, meaning the amount and due date are fixed and certain or definitely ascertainable by mathematical calculation

  • Application: Blair held that prejudgment interest is discretionary and that a good faith dispute over the amount does not necessarily bar it; Note 3 asks whether the Lukaszewski claim, a fixed $1,026.64 salary difference, was liquidated

  • Exceptions: Unliquidated claims usually do not earn prejudgment interest

 

Rule: Postjudgment interest

  • Source: Note 3 following Lukaszewski, citing 28 U.S.C. § 1961 and N.Y. Civ. Prac. Law §§ 5003, 5004

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: A successful party will usually accrue postjudgment interest under local law at least from the date judgment is entered, and perhaps from the date of the verdict

  • Legal Question: Does a judgment earn interest after it is entered?

  • Requirements: A judgment, or in some jurisdictions a verdict, in the party's favor

  • Application: This compensates the prevailing party for not having use of the funds while the case runs, as in Lukaszewski, which took from the 1978 breach to a 1983 decision

  • Exceptions: The governing local statute controls when interest starts

 

Rule: General rule of damages for breach of a construction contract

  • Source: Case holding in American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman (N.Y. App. Div. 1981), citing Chamberlain v. Parker, Hadley v. Baxendale, Restatement (First) § 346, and Bellizzi v. Huntley Estates; Note 1 citing Nippo Corp. and Calamari & Perillo § 14.29

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: The injured party may recover damages that are "the direct, natural and immediate consequence of the breach and which can reasonably be said to have been in the contemplation of the parties when the contract was made"; "In the usual case where the contractor's performance has been defective or incomplete, the reasonable cost of replacement or completion is the measure"

  • Legal Question: What is the owner's measure of damages when a contractor's performance is defective or incomplete?

  • Requirements:

    1. A defective or incomplete performance by the contractor

    2. Damages that are the direct, natural, and immediate consequence of the breach

    3. Damages within the parties' contemplation at the time of contracting

    4. A reasonable cost of replacement or completion

  • Application: Schectman was liable for the $90,000 the jury found as the reasonable cost of removing the structures that remained above grade; under Calamari & Perillo, an abandoning builder owes the reasonable cost of completion plus delay damages; Note 2 gives two rationales: the owner can spend the award to obtain the bargained-for performance, and market value may miss "idiosyncratic" value, as with a personal residence or family farm (Muris on subjective value); the Groves and Chamberlain principle is that "a man may do what he will with his own," even erecting "a monument to his caprice or folly," and the contractor cannot argue that its performance would not have benefited the owner

  • Exceptions: The economic waste rule and the incidental covenant rule (below); "Disparity in relative economic benefits is not the equivalent of 'economic waste'"; the fact that the work turned out more burdensome than expected does not alter the rule

 

Rule: Economic waste exception

  • Source: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman, discussing Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent (N.Y.) and Restatement (First) § 346(1)(a)(ii) and Illustration 2

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: "When, however, there has been a substantial performance of the contract made in good faith but defects exist, the correction of which would result in economic waste," damages are measured as the difference between the value of the property as built and its value had performance been properly completed

  • Legal Question: When does diminution in value replace cost of completion as the measure?

  • Requirements:

    1. Substantial performance

    2. Made in good faith, with the breach unintentional

    3. Defects whose correction would involve "unreasonable economic waste," usually defects that cannot be fixed or cannot be repaired without substantially tearing down the structure

    4. A cost of correction that is "grievously out of proportion" to the significance of the default, with omissions of "trivial or inappreciable importance"

  • Application: In Jacob & Youngs, pipe of brands other than the specified Reading brand but of the same quality was installed, and the owner got only the nominal difference in value; American Standard refused to apply the exception because completion required only "doing what was promised and left undone," not undoing work; Groves v. John Wunder Co. awarded the $60,000 cost of grading even though the land was worth only $12,160 graded

  • Exceptions: The exception is unavailable to a contractor who breached intentionally or did not substantially perform; Schectman's unfinished work cost $90,000 and he denied he was obliged to do it, so he was not a "transgressor whose default is unintentional and trivial"

 

Rule: Incidental covenant exception

  • Source: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman, discussing Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Min. Co. (Okla.) and Avery v. Fredericksen & Westbrook; Note 4 discussing Maute's study of Peevyhouse

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: "Where, however, the breach is of a covenant which is only incidental to the main purpose of the contract and completion would be disproportionately costly, courts have applied the diminution in value measure even where no destruction of the work is entailed"

  • Legal Question: Can diminution in value apply even though completing the work would not require tearing anything down?

  • Requirements:

    1. The breached covenant is only incidental to the main purpose of the contract

    2. Completion would be disproportionately costly

  • Application: In Peevyhouse, a coal mining lessee failed to perform restorative work, and diminution in value applied because the main purpose of the lease was recovering coal, which is contrary to Groves; American Standard distinguished it because the grading and removal were not incidental to the plaintiffs' purpose of "achieving a reasonably attractive vacant plot for resale" and were part of the consideration for conveying the plant; Maute's research shows that the Peevyhouses gave up $3,000 in exchange for the promise to restore, received a $300 judgment, netted nothing after costs, and as of 1995 still lived on the unrestored land

  • Exceptions: Peevyhouse distinguished building and construction cases; Bannum, Inc. v. 2210 Adams Place (D.C. 2010) limited damages to diminution in value and distinguished American Standard because the plaintiff had not specifically paid for restoration

 

Rule: Restatement (Second) § 348(2)

  • Source: Restatement (Second) § 348(2), in Note 5 following American Standard

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: If the loss in value to the injured party is not proved with sufficient certainty, damages may be measured by (a) the diminution in market value or (b) the reasonable cost of completing performance or remedying the defects, if that cost "is not clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value to him"

  • Legal Question: How are damages measured when the loss in value cannot be proved with sufficient certainty?

  • Requirements:

    1. Loss in value is not proved with sufficient certainty

    2. For cost of completion, the cost is not clearly disproportionate to the probable loss in value to the injured party

  • Application: Note 5 asks how this compares to American Standard, which held that "disparity in relative economic benefits" alone does not trigger diminution in value; the Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 39, Illustration 5, favors cost of repair in a Peevyhouse or American Standard type scenario because those costs were factored into the price the parties set

  • Exceptions: Cost of completion is unavailable when it is "clearly disproportionate" to the probable loss in value

 

Rule: Diminution in market value on efficiency grounds

  • Source: Note 3 following American Standard, citing Posner, Economic Analysis of Law (9th ed. 2014), and Emery v. Caledonia Sand & Gravel Co. (N.H. 1977); Note 6 citing Farnsworth (1970) and Linzer (1981); Schwartz & Scott (2008); Chomsky (1991)

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Statement: Posner argues that cost-to-restore damages overcompensate an owner who does not actually restore the property, and that an owner who truly wanted restoration could have sued for specific performance; Emery rejects the idea that an owner who "pockets" the recovery is unjustly enriched, reasoning that the owner has simply given up restoration

  • Legal Question: Should the cost-to-restore remedy depend on whether the plaintiff will actually use the award to restore?

  • Requirements: This is a policy debate, not a black-letter test; the relevant factors are whether the plaintiff will restore, whether the plaintiff has subjective value in the property, and whether the plaintiff specifically paid for restoration

  • Application: The Groves plaintiff did not restore, which Posner uses as his example; American Standard's plaintiffs resold for only $3,000 below full market value, and Note 3 asks whether the court gave that fact too little weight; this view is a corollary of "efficient breach," covered later in the chapter

  • Exceptions: Several alternatives have been proposed: Farnsworth would let the fact finder pick any "not unreasonable" compromise between the two measures; Linzer would award specific performance and leave the parties to negotiate a buyout; Schwartz & Scott call economic waste and windfall objections unsound; Chomsky favors cost to complete generally but admits it sometimes overcompensates

 

2. Case Narrative

Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

Crabby's, Inc. is a Missouri corporation owned by Fred and Carolyn Billingsly. For several years it owned and operated Crabby's restaurant in Joplin, Missouri. It is the plaintiff and the seller. The defendants are the buyers: James Hamilton and Paragon Ventures, L.L.C., a business Hamilton and Richard Worley set up to run a restaurant. In 2003 the Billingslys listed the restaurant and its real property with Dee Kassab of Pro 100 Realty for $325,000. They turned down a $275,000 offer. Hamilton, working through Kent Eastman, an agent at the same realty firm, then offered $290,000, and the seller accepted on May 17, 2003. Hamilton later assigned his interest to Paragon but stayed on the contract as an individual buyer. The contract was contingent on the buyers obtaining a conventional loan of $232,000, payable over at least 15 years at no more than 5.5% interest. The buyers had to use reasonable diligence to get it, and if they did not give the seller a written loan commitment within 30 days, the contract would "automatically terminate."

The buyers never delivered a loan commitment within 30 days. They applied only to the Bank of Joplin, and only for a much larger package of $340,000: $225,000 on the real estate over 15 years, $65,000 on equipment over 7 years, and a $50,000 revolving line of credit, all at prime plus 1.5%. The bank approved it. On June 10, 2003, the buyers' agent received a title commitment showing sales tax liens on the property. The original June 30 closing was pushed to July 14 because of appraisal requirements. The parties then agreed that the buyers would take a $1,373.54 credit instead of further repairs. A second extension, dated July 18 (the opinion also describes the buyers signing an amendment on July 17), moved closing to August 1, assigned the contract to Paragon, and stated that all other terms "remain unchanged." The buyers also signed a rider letting them take possession as tenants from July 21 to clean. They took a key, put the utilities in their own name, and as late as July 25 were applying for restaurant licenses.

Just before closing everything was ready. The title company had the documents, the bank had the financing in place, and the seller's lender, U.S. Bank, had agreed to accept $266,000 and release its lien. The seller would have netted only $1,757.72 after paying off its debts. The tax liens were paid off the morning of August 1, as the parties had planned. On July 30, however, the buyers wrote that they would not close. They complained that "fixtures" had been taken: two used TVs, a couple of mirrors, stereo speakers, and a computerized cash register, none of which was on the list of personal property included in the sale. They also cited the tax liens. They said nothing about financing, and they did not show up on August 1. On August 5, Paragon offered to buy a different building at 520 Main Street in Joplin for a restaurant. That seller accepted the next day, and the deal closed on September 22, 2003, for $170,000.

Crabby's realtor kept trying to sell the restaurant but got no offers until May 2004, when J and A Cafe of Kansas, L.L.C. offered $235,000. That sale closed on July 15, 2004, about eleven and a half months after the failed closing. Crabby's then sued Hamilton and Paragon for breach of contract. It sought the $55,000 difference between the $290,000 contract price and the $235,000 resale price, plus the real estate and personal property taxes, utilities, and mortgage interest it paid while carrying the property between the two dates.

Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

The plaintiff is the Handicapped Children's Education Board, a public education employer in Wisconsin. The defendant is Elaine Lukaszewski, a speech and language therapist the Board employed. In January 1978 the Board hired her for the spring term and assigned her to the Lightfoot School in Sheboygan Falls, about 45 miles from her home in Mequon. She commuted daily instead of moving. During that spring the Board offered her a contract to stay at Lightfoot for the 1978–79 school year at $10,760, and she accepted. She had a history of high blood pressure dating to 1976 and was taking blood pressure medication.

In August 1978, before the school year began, the Wee Care Day Care Center, located near her home in Mequon, offered her a job at $13,000 a year. She decided to take it and told Thomas Morrelle, the Board's director of special education, that she meant to resign. He told her to submit a resignation letter for the Board to consider. At its August 21 meeting the Board refused to release her. On August 24 the Board's lawyer wrote to her ordering her back to work. The lawyer also wrote to Wee Care threatening legal action if it interfered with her contract, and sent a copy to the Department of Public Instruction. She left Wee Care and returned to Lightfoot for the fall term, but she resented how she had been treated and still had misgivings about the job.

On September 8, 1978, she met with Morrelle about her feelings and came out of the meeting very upset. She made a same-day appointment with her doctor and left school. Dr. Ashok Chatterjee found her agitated and nervous, with blood pressure readings up to 180/100. In a letter dated September 11, he wrote that her condition would not improve unless the cause was removed and that driving long distances in her agitated state would be dangerous. She never went back. In a September 13 letter she resigned, citing the doctor's statement and saying she would not risk her health or an accident. Shortly afterward she reapplied to Wee Care and was rehired.

The Board immediately looked for a replacement. Only one qualified person applied. That applicant had less education but more teaching experience than Lukaszewski, and under the union salary schedule she had to be paid $1,026.64 more per year. With no alternative, the Board hired her. In December 1978 the Board sued Lukaszewski for breach of contract and sought the $1,026.64 extra salary for the 1978–79 year.

American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

The plaintiffs are American Standard, Inc. and its co-plaintiffs (the opinion refers to them only as "plaintiffs"). Until 1972 they ran a pig iron plant on a 26-acre parcel along the Niagara River in Tonawanda, New York. The site had housed various industrial operations under several owners since the 1870s. It held industrial and office buildings, a 60-ton blast furnace, large lifts, hoists, and other equipment for moving and storing ore, plus railroad tracks, cranes, diesel locomotives, and other equipment. The defendant, Schectman, is a demolition and excavating contractor.

After deciding to close the plant, the plaintiffs entered a contract with Schectman on August 3, 1973. They agreed to convey the buildings, other structures, and most of the equipment to him. In return he would pay $275,000, remove the equipment, demolish the structures, and grade the property as specified. The contract required him to remove all foundations, piers, headwalls, and other structures, including those underground and not visible and whether or not shown on the attached map, to about one foot below the specified grade lines. The plaintiffs wanted that work done to prepare the land for sale.

Schectman left the job unfinished. The property deviated substantially from the required grade lines, and walls, foundations, and other structures still stood above grade. He maintained throughout that the contract did not require him to remove all the subsurface foundations. The plaintiffs later sold the property for $183,000, only $3,000 below its full fair market value. They sued Schectman for breach and sought the cost of completing the promised work, which their expert estimated at $110,500.

Case Name: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Crabby's, Inc. (the seller) contracted on May 17, 2003, to sell its Joplin, Missouri restaurant property to Hamilton for $290,000; Hamilton later assigned to Paragon Ventures, L.L.C. but stayed on the contract (together, the buyers)

    • A financing contingency made the contract contingent on a $232,000 conventional loan for at least 15 years at no more than 5.5%; the buyers had to use reasonable diligence, and the contract would "automatically terminate" if no written loan commitment was furnished within 30 days

    • The buyers never furnished a commitment within 30 days and never applied for a loan on the contract's terms; they applied only to the Bank of Joplin, for a $340,000 package at prime plus 1.5%, which was approved

    • After the 30-day deadline passed, the buyers signed amendments extending closing to August 1, assigning to Paragon, and taking a $1,373.54 repair credit, all stating "all other terms and conditions of the contract remain unchanged"; they also signed a possession rider, took a key, switched the utilities, and applied for restaurant licenses as late as July 25

    • Everything was in place for an August 1 closing, including financing and payoff of the tax liens that morning; on July 30 the buyers repudiated, citing removed "fixtures" not on the sale list and the tax liens, but not financing; they did not appear at closing

    • Within days Paragon contracted for a different building at 520 Main Street for $170,000

    • The seller resold to J and A Cafe for $235,000, with the sale closing on July 15, 2004, eleven and one-half months after the breach

    • Carolyn Billingsly answered "We did" to the compound question "you were compelled to sell it, I mean, you wanted to sell it bad; true?"

  • Procedural Posture:

    • Missouri trial court: entered judgment for Plaintiff Crabby's, Inc. against Defendants Hamilton and Paragon Ventures, L.L.C. for $95,547.30, after a bench trial without a jury

    • Appeal of right: Defendants-Appellants Hamilton and Paragon Ventures, L.L.C. sought review in the Missouri Court of Appeals on the grounds that they could not have breached because the contract automatically terminated under its financing contingency, or alternatively that the trial court misinterpreted "reasonable diligence," and that the judgment lacked substantial evidence of the property's fair market value on the date of breach because the $235,000 resale was too remote in time and was a distress sale

    • Missouri Court of Appeals: Hamilton and Paragon Ventures, L.L.C. (defendants below) are the appellants; Crabby's, Inc. (plaintiff below) is the appellee

  • Issue: Whether the buyers breached despite the financing contingency, and whether a resale eleven and one-half months after breach, made by a seller who "wanted badly" to sell, is substantial evidence of the property's fair market value on the date of breach

  • Holding: The Missouri Court of Appeals held that the buyers waived the entire financing contingency through clear, unequivocal, and decisive conduct and therefore breached; it also held that the July 15, 2004 resale occurred within a reasonable time and was not a compelled sale, so it was substantial evidence of fair market value on the date of breach

  • Rule:

    • Financing contingency as a condition of the buyer's duty: A buyer's financing contingency is a condition of the buyer's duty only, and its non-occurrence excuses the buyer

    • Waiver of a condition by conduct: A party may waive a condition in its favor by conduct, if the conduct is a clear, unequivocal, and decisive act so consistent with intent to waive that no other reasonable explanation is possible

    • Seller's damages for buyer's breach of contract to sell land: The seller recovers the contract price minus the property's fair market value on the date the sale should have been completed

    • Resale price as evidence of market value: A resale within a reasonable time after breach is some evidence of market value on the date of breach

    • Fair market value: Fair market value is the price agreed between a willing seller under no compulsion to sell and a willing buyer under no compulsion to buy

  • Reasoning:

    • The appeals court reasoned that because the contingency protected the buyers, it conditioned only their duty, and the buyers could waive it

    • The effective date was May 17, 2003, so the contract would have terminated 30 days later, but the buyers' later conduct was inconsistent with termination: the amendment extending closing and confirming that all other terms remained unchanged, the possession rider and key, the utilities transfer, and the license applications

    • The buyers never sought a loan on the contingency's terms, which evidences a lack of reasonable diligence; the appeals court did not need to decide that point, because that failure combined with the July 17 conduct showed waiver of the entire contingency, not merely the automatic termination clause

    • On valuation, the buyers cited no Missouri authority and relied only on Chris v. Epstein from North Carolina; the appeals court followed its own decision in Hawkins v. Foster, which approved a resale a little over eleven and one-half months after breach, and the buyers did not distinguish it

    • The "distress sale" argument failed on the record: Billingsly's answer "We did" responded to the "wanted to sell it bad" half of the compound question, not to "you were compelled," which would have called for "We were"

    • Being highly motivated to sell differs from being compelled to sell; Carter involved a liquidation deadline under the tax code plus a threat of condemnation, and the seller here faced nothing like that

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments:

    • The buyers' conduct after contracting waived the financing contingency provisions

    • The buyers' conduct showed a clear and unequivocal intent to waive all of the contingency's financing terms, not just automatic termination

    • Hawkins v. Foster controls and makes an eleven-and-one-half-month resale reasonable

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • The contract automatically terminated as a matter of law when no written loan commitment was furnished within 30 days, so refusing to close could not be a breach

    • Alternatively, even if automatic termination was waived, their inability to get financing on the contingency's terms relieved them, and the trial court misinterpreted "reasonable diligence"

    • A resale eleven and one-half months after breach is too remote as a matter of law, relying on Chris v. Epstein

    • The resale was a distress sale because the seller was "compelled" to sell, so under Turner and Carter it could not reflect fair market value

  • Disposition: The Missouri Court of Appeals denied both points and affirmed the trial court's $95,547.30 judgment for the seller

  • Importance: This is the casebook's lead example of measuring expectation damages in a land sale case as the contract price minus market value at the time of breach; it shows how market value is proved, including through a later resale and the limits on that evidence (time and compulsion); it also ties back to Chapter 9 on waiver of express conditions; Note 5 uses the gap between the roughly $55,000 contract–market difference and the $95,547.30 total award to introduce "other loss" (taxes, utilities, mortgage interest) and the foreseeability, certainty, and mitigation limits; the Notes extend the analysis to the English and American rules and to the UCC counterparts (§§ 2-706, 2-708, 2-712, 2-713)

 

Case Name: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • Lukaszewski, a speech and language therapist, accepted a 1978–79 contract with the Board at $10,760 to work at Lightfoot School in Sheboygan Falls, about 45 miles from her Mequon home

    • In August 1978 she accepted a $13,000 job at Wee Care Day Care Center near her home; the Board refused to release her and its lawyer threatened her and Wee Care with legal action, so she returned to Lightfoot

    • She had hypertension dating to 1976; after a September 8 meeting with Morrelle she left school upset, and her doctor found readings up to 180/100 and advised that driving long distances while agitated was dangerous

    • She resigned in writing on September 13, citing her health, and soon went back to work at Wee Care

    • The trial court found her medical excuse was "a result of the stress condition she had created by an attempted repudiation of her contract," not the product of harassment by the Board

    • The Board immediately searched for a replacement; the only qualified applicant had more experience and less education and, under the union salary schedule, cost $1,026.64 more per year; the Board hired her with "no alternative"

  • Procedural Posture:

    • Circuit Court for Ozaukee County, Wisconsin: ruled after a bench trial that Lukaszewski breached her contract and awarded the Board $1,249.14, consisting of $1,026.64 for breach of contract and $222.50 in costs

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Lukaszewski sought review in the Wisconsin Court of Appeals on the grounds that her resignation was excused because she resigned for health reasons and that the Board suffered no net loss because the replacement was a proportionately more valuable teacher

    • Wisconsin Court of Appeals: in an unpublished decision, affirmed the finding that Lukaszewski breached but reversed the damage award, holding that the Board suffered no damage because it obtained a proportionately more valuable teacher for the higher salary

    • Petition for review: the Board, Plaintiff below and now Petitioner, sought review in the Supreme Court of Wisconsin on the grounds that the court of appeals erred in reversing the damage award

    • Supreme Court of Wisconsin: the Board (plaintiff below) is the petitioner; Lukaszewski (defendant below) is the respondent; the court granted the Board's petition for review

  • Issue: Whether Lukaszewski breached her employment contract despite her claimed health reasons, and if so, whether the Board suffered recoverable damages when it had to pay a more experienced replacement a higher salary

  • Holding: The Supreme Court of Wisconsin held that the trial court's finding of breach was not against the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence, and that the Board suffered damages for the loss of its bargain equal to the $1,026.64 extra it had to pay the replacement

  • Rule:

    • Employer's damages for employee's breach of employment contract: An employer recovers the cost of obtaining equivalent services plus any foreseeable consequential damages

    • Restatement (Second) § 347: Damages are measured by the parties' expectations, giving full compensation for the loss of the bargain for losses necessarily flowing from the breach, proven to a reasonable certainty, and within the parties' contemplation

    • Expectation measured by the bargained-for performance: Extra value imposed on the injured party by a forced substitute is not a benefit that offsets damages

    • Duty to mitigate damages: The injured party must take all reasonable steps to mitigate, so an employer must try to obtain equivalent services at the lowest possible cost

    • Restatement (Second) § 262: Incapacity or undue risk to health can excuse performance of a personal service contract, but only if the fact finder finds that nonperformance actually resulted from it

  • Reasoning:

    • The Supreme Court of Wisconsin held that excusing Lukaszewski would have required a factual finding that she resigned for health reasons; the trial court's oral and written decisions found otherwise, and that finding was not against the great weight and clear preponderance of the evidence

    • Wisconsin has long allowed employers to recover damages from employees who fail to perform (Walsh v. Fisher)

    • The extra $1,026.64 necessarily flowed from the breach and was within the parties' contemplation

    • The court of appeals and Lukaszewski wrongly focused on the objective value of the services received instead of the services bargained for; the Board expected a therapist with her education and experience at her salary and "neither expected nor wanted" a more experienced, more expensive one

    • Any added value from the replacement's experience was "imposed upon" the Board, so it was not a benefit

    • An employer cannot hire the most expensive replacement and recover the difference; but the Board searched immediately, had one qualified applicant, and hired the least expensive qualified replacement available, so it properly mitigated

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments:

    • The Board lost the benefit of its bargain because the breach forced it to pay $1,026.64 more for a replacement

    • It mitigated by searching immediately and hiring the only qualified applicant

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • Her resignation was justified because she resigned for health reasons, and the uncontroverted evidence showed the job endangered her health

    • The Board suffered no net loss because the salary schedule reflects value, so paying more meant getting a proportionately more valuable teacher; awarding damages would put the Board in a better position than performance would have

  • Disposition: The Supreme Court of Wisconsin affirmed the part of the court of appeals' decision upholding the finding of breach, reversed the part reversing the damage award, and thereby reinstated the trial court's damages; Justice Day dissented, arguing that medically certifiable symptoms, whether caused by the Board or "self induced," justified ending the contract, so there was no breach and he would not reach damages

  • Importance: This case shows how expectation damages work when the breaching party owed a non-money performance (services): the measure is the cost of substitute performance, the expectation is defined by the bargain and not by objective market value, and mitigation limits recovery to the cheapest qualified substitute; it shows the employer's side of employment damages, while Case 2 shows the employee's side; it ties to Chapter 8 (impracticability and incapacity) through the excuse issue and the dissent; the Notes raise why specific performance is almost never ordered against employees, which fields use fixed-term contracts, and prejudgment and postjudgment interest

 

Case Name: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

  • Document: CB pp 893-922

  • Course: Contracts

  • Facts

    • The plaintiffs closed their pig iron plant on a 26-acre Niagara River parcel in Tonawanda in 1972

    • On August 3, 1973, they agreed to convey the buildings, structures, and most of the equipment to Schectman, a demolition and excavating contractor, in exchange for $275,000 plus his promise to remove the equipment, demolish the structures, and grade the property as specified

    • As interpreted, the contract required removal of all foundations, piers, headwalls, and other structures, including subsurface ones not shown on the map, to about one foot below grade

    • Schectman left substantial deviations from grade and walls, foundations, and structures above grade, and maintained throughout that the contract did not require removing all subsurface foundations

    • The plaintiffs' expert estimated the cost of completion at $110,500; the jury returned a $90,000 verdict, reflecting its finding that the reasonable cost of removing the structures above grade was $90,000

    • Schectman offered proof that leaving the foundations at grade rather than one foot below made no difference in value, and that the plaintiffs sold the land for $183,000, only $3,000 below full fair market value

  • Procedural Posture:

    • Supreme Court of New York, Trial Term (New York's trial court of general jurisdiction): entered judgment for the plaintiffs on a $90,000 jury verdict after refusing Schectman's requested charge on difference in value between what the property is worth as the contractor left it and what it would have been worth if the contractor had fully performed, and excluding his proof of the property's value and its $183,000 resale

    • Appeal of right: Defendant-Appellant Schectman sought review in the Appellate Division of the New York Supreme Court on the grounds that the trial court erred in refusing to charge diminution in value as the measure and in rejecting his proof that the plaintiffs suffered no loss, because under Jacob & Youngs he had substantially performed with omissions of trivial importance and the cost of completion was grossly out of proportion to the good to be attained

    • Appellate Division of the New York Supreme Court: Schectman (defendant below) is the appellant; American Standard, Inc. and its co-plaintiffs (plaintiffs below) are the appellees

  • Issue: Whether, when a contractor fails to complete promised grading and removal work, the owner's damages are measured by the reasonable cost of completion or by the difference in the property's value with and without the promised performance

  • Holding: The Appellate Division held that the cost of completion, not diminution in value, was the proper measure, so the requested charge was properly denied and the value evidence properly excluded

  • Rule:

    • General rule of damages for breach of a construction contract: When the contractor's performance is defective or incomplete, the measure is the reasonable cost of replacement or completion, covering damages that are the direct, natural, and immediate consequence of the breach and within the parties' contemplation

    • Economic waste exception: When a contract is substantially performed in good faith but has defects whose correction would cause economic waste, damages are measured by diminution in value

    • Incidental covenant exception: Diminution in value may apply where the breached covenant is only incidental to the contract's main purpose and completion would be disproportionately costly

  • Reasoning:

    • The Appellate Division reasoned that in the usual case of defective or incomplete performance, the reasonable cost of completion is the measure under Hadley and Chamberlain

    • The economic waste exception in Jacob & Youngs applies to good faith substantial performance where correction would be grievously disproportionate, usually requiring the work to be torn down; here completion required only "doing what was promised and left undone," not undoing work done in good faith

    • Following Groves, "disparity in relative economic benefits is not the equivalent of 'economic waste'"; an owner may do what it wants with its own property, and a contractor who has been paid cannot argue that its performance would not have benefited the owner

    • Peevyhouse was distinguishable because the grading and removal were not incidental to the plaintiffs' purpose of producing a reasonably attractive vacant plot for resale and were part of the consideration for conveying a valuable plant

    • The cost of completion was what the parties could reasonably have contemplated at the time of contracting; that completion would add little to sale value, contrary to the plaintiffs' expectations, does not excuse the default

    • Schectman could not claim substantial performance when the work left undone cost $90,000, and he was not a good faith transgressor because he denied any obligation to do the removal instead of trying to complete it

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments:

    • The contract required removing all structures to about one foot below grade, and Schectman did not do it

    • The measure is the reasonable cost of completion, estimated by their expert at $110,500

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • The plaintiffs suffered no loss because the property's value is the same whether the foundations are at grade or one foot below, and they sold for only $3,000 less than full market value

    • Cost-of-completion damages give the plaintiffs a windfall at his expense

    • Under Jacob & Youngs he substantially performed, the omissions were of "trivial or inappreciable importance," and the cost of completion was "grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained," so diminution in value is the proper measure

    • The contract did not require him to remove all subsurface foundations, a position the trial court rejected

  • Disposition: The Appellate Division unanimously affirmed the judgment and order, with costs

  • Importance: This is the casebook's main vehicle for the choice between cost to complete and diminution in value when a contractor breaches; know the general cost-to-complete rule, the two exceptions (economic waste under Jacob & Youngs and incidental covenant under Peevyhouse), the good faith and substantial performance prerequisites, and the Groves/Peevyhouse split; the Notes frame the policy debate: subjective or idiosyncratic value (Muris), Posner's efficiency and overcompensation critique against Emery's "pocketing" answer, Restatement (Second) § 348(2)'s "clearly disproportionate" test, the Restatement (Third) of Restitution's view that repair costs were priced into the deal, Maute's account of what really happened to the Peevyhouses, and the Farnsworth and Linzer alternatives; be ready to discuss whether the $3,000 resale shortfall should have mattered and whether a business plaintiff has any idiosyncratic value

 

4. Definitions

Nominal damages

  • Definition: "A small sum fixed without regard to the amount of loss" (Restatement (Second) § 346(2)), awarded for breach when there are no actual damages or none that can be proved

  • Significance: The minimum remedy for any actionable breach; MBM held that $1, $10, or $100 can be nominal but $1,000 is too much

  • Distinction: These are symbolic, unlike compensatory expectation, reliance, or restitution damages

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Restitution interest

  • Definition: The plaintiff's interest in recovering value she conferred on the breaching defendant while relying on the promise, which prevents the defendant's unjust enrichment

  • Significance: Fuller and Perdue say it presents "the strongest case for relief" because it combines unjust impoverishment with unjust gain

  • Distinction: It focuses on the defendant's gain, while reliance focuses on the plaintiff's loss

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Reliance interest

  • Definition: The plaintiff's interest in being restored to the position she was in before the promise was made, by undoing harm caused by relying on it, such as expenses incurred or opportunities given up

  • Significance: Second in Fuller and Perdue's ranking; often used to measure damages, as covered in Chapter 11

  • Distinction: It looks backward to the pre-contract position, while the expectation interest looks forward to the position after performance

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Expectation interest

  • Definition: The promisee's interest in the value of the expectancy the promise created, meaning being put in as good a position as if the defendant had performed

  • Significance: The normal rule of contract damages under Restatement (Second) § 347, serving as both floor and ceiling of recovery

  • Distinction: It requires neither reliance by the promisee nor enrichment of the promisor

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton; Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski; American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Benefit of the bargain

  • Definition: The gain the plaintiff would have realized had the agreement been fully performed; another name for expectation damages

  • Significance: Lukaszewski uses the phrase to reject an objective-value offset, and Crabby's uses it to describe the contract price minus market value measure

  • Distinction: It is measured by what was bargained for, not by the objective market value of the substitute performance received

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton; Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Specific performance

  • Definition: "An equitable remedy compelling performance of a contract on the precise terms agreed upon by the parties," a form of injunctive decree ordering the defendant to perform

  • Significance: Covered in Chapter 11; in most breach cases only money damages are sought or awarded; courts almost never order employees to specifically perform; Linzer proposes it as an alternative in cost-to-complete cases

  • Distinction: A money judgment for an unpaid price is described as a kind of specific performance because it orders exactly what was promised

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Total breach

  • Definition: A breach for which the injured party terminates the contract, refuses to render further return performance, and claims damages

  • Significance: It triggers all four terms of Farnsworth's formula, including the subtractions for cost avoided and loss avoided

  • Distinction: In a partial breach the injured party keeps performing and recovers only loss in value plus other loss

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Partial breach

  • Definition: A breach for which the injured party does not terminate, stands ready to render any remaining return performance, and claims damages

  • Significance: Only loss in value and other loss apply

  • Distinction: There are no deductions for cost avoided or loss avoided, unlike total breach

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Loss in value

  • Definition: The value to the injured party of the performance that should have been received, minus the value to that party of what, if anything, was actually received

  • Significance: The first and core term of the § 347 formula; easy to compute when the breaching party owed money, and hard when it owed a non-money performance or the loss involves personal satisfaction

  • Distinction: It is measured by value to the injured party, which can differ from market value, as Lukaszewski shows

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski; American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Other loss

  • Definition: Loss caused by the breach other than loss in value; it includes incidental and consequential damages

  • Significance: Added to damages in both total and partial breach claims, subject to foreseeability, certainty, and mitigation; Farnsworth says the label "should not … be critical" because "all loss, however characterized, is recoverable"

  • Distinction: It is separate from the value of the promised performance itself

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Incidental damages

  • Definition: Additional costs incurred after the breach in a reasonable attempt to avoid loss, even if the attempt fails, such as a broker's or employment agency fee

  • Significance: The $1,000 agency fee in Case 2 is an example; incidental damages are expressly allowed under UCC §§ 2-708, 2-710, 2-713, and 2-715

  • Distinction: They are post-breach avoidance costs, while consequential damages are harms the breach causes downstream

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Consequential damages

  • Definition: Losses that follow from the breach beyond the loss in value, such as injury to person or property, sometimes called "special" damages

  • Significance: Recoverable only if foreseeable, reasonably certain, and not avoidable; the carrying costs in Crabby's (taxes, utilities, mortgage interest) are labeled "consequential" or "incidental"

  • Distinction: They are distinct from incidental damages; UCC § 2-713 lets buyers recover them, while § 2-708(1) mentions only incidental damages for sellers

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton; Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Cost avoided

  • Definition: Further spending the injured party saves because termination relieves it of the rest of its own performance

  • Significance: Subtracted in a total breach claim; in Case 1 it is the $85,000 of remaining construction cost

  • Distinction: It is the cost of not performing, while loss avoided is the gain from redeploying resources

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Loss avoided

  • Definition: Savings the injured party gets by salvaging or reallocating resources it would otherwise have devoted to the contract, such as reselling materials or earning wages in substitute employment

  • Significance: Subtracted in a total breach claim; it is measured by what was actually saved, even if unusually favorable

  • Distinction: It is closely tied to mitigation, but it counts actual savings rather than savings that should reasonably have been made

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Mitigation of damages

  • Definition: The injured party's duty to take all reasonable steps to avoid or minimize loss; damages that reasonable efforts would have avoided are not recoverable

  • Significance: It limited the Board in Lukaszewski to hiring the least expensive qualified replacement, and it is one of the three limits on other loss

  • Distinction: Mitigation reduces recovery for losses that could reasonably have been avoided, while loss avoided reflects losses that were actually avoided

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski; Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Fair market value

  • Definition: The price property will bring when offered by an owner willing but under no compulsion to sell and bought by a buyer willing but not compelled to buy

  • Significance: The second term of the land-sale formula; in Crabby's it was measured on the date of breach, not the date of trial

  • Distinction: It differs from a distress sale price; a highly motivated seller is not a compelled seller

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Distress sale

  • Definition: A sale made under compulsion, such as a tax-code liquidation deadline or threatened condemnation, which therefore does not reflect fair market value

  • Significance: The buyers in Crabby's tried, and failed, to characterize the $235,000 resale this way

  • Distinction: It differs from a sale by a seller who merely "wanted badly" to sell

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Arm's length transaction

  • Definition: A sale between independent parties dealing on ordinary market terms

  • Significance: A resale can serve as evidence of market value only if it is at arm's length and within a reasonable time after breach (Note 3)

  • Distinction: It contrasts with compelled or distress sales

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Financing contingency

  • Definition: A real estate contract clause making the buyer's duty contingent on obtaining financing on specified terms; it is a condition of the buyer's duty only

  • Significance: Non-occurrence ipso facto excuses the buyer, but the buyer may waive it

  • Distinction: It is a condition, not a promise; it protects the buyer and does not condition the seller's duty

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

Waiver

  • Definition: "An intentional relinquishment of a known right"; when implied from conduct, it requires a clear, unequivocal, and decisive act consistent only with an intent to waive

  • Significance: The Crabby's buyers waived the entire financing contingency by extending closing, taking possession, switching utilities, and seeking licenses

  • Distinction: Waiver can apply even to provisions requiring written communications, and the intention of the party charged with waiver is controlling

  • Case: Crabby's, Inc. v. Hamilton

 

English rule

  • Definition: The traditional rule limiting a buyer to restitution of payments made when a land seller breaches, unless the seller acted in bad faith

  • Significance: It grew out of the historical difficulty of title searches; it is increasingly criticized and losing ground, though Florida still follows it

  • Distinction: It contrasts with the American rule, which gives expectation damages regardless of the seller's good faith

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

American rule

  • Definition: The rule awarding the buyer expectation damages for any unexcused failure by the seller to convey land, regardless of good or bad faith

  • Significance: It is consistent with the general contract principle of expectation recovery absent legal excuse, and it is gaining adherents

  • Distinction: Unlike the English rule, it does not adjust the remedy for the seller's willfulness

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Cover

  • Definition: Under UCC § 2-712(1), a buyer's purchase of substitute goods after the seller breaches, with damages measured by the cost of cover minus the contract price

  • Significance: It replaces the hypothetical market price with the buyer's actual substitute transaction

  • Distinction: It is the buyer's counterpart to the seller's resale under § 2-706, and an alternative to the § 2-713 market measure

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Seller's resale

  • Definition: Under UCC § 2-706, a seller's resale of the goods after the buyer breaches, with damages measured by the contract price minus the resale price

  • Significance: It reflects the drafters' view that substitute transactions often measure loss better than market price

  • Distinction: It is the UCC analogue to using resale as evidence of market value in land cases, but under the UCC the resale price is itself the measure

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Cost to complete

  • Definition: The reasonable cost of replacing defective work or completing unfinished work

  • Significance: The usual measure of the owner's damages when a contractor breaches; $90,000 was awarded in American Standard

  • Distinction: It is often far larger than diminution in value; it is limited by the economic waste exception, the incidental covenant exception, and the "clearly disproportionate" test in § 348(2)

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Diminution in value

  • Definition: The difference between the property's market value as delivered and what its value would have been had the contract been properly performed

  • Significance: Applied under the economic waste and incidental covenant exceptions; Posner favors it on efficiency grounds

  • Distinction: It may undercompensate an owner whose subjective or idiosyncratic value is not reflected in market value

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Economic waste

  • Definition: In this doctrine, correcting defects in a substantially performed contract at a cost grievously out of proportion to the benefit, usually because doing so would require substantially tearing down the structure

  • Significance: It triggers the diminution in value measure under Jacob & Youngs; American Standard held that "disparity in relative economic benefits" alone is not economic waste

  • Distinction: Schwartz & Scott call the "economic waste" objection a fallacy

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Substantial performance

  • Definition: Performance that falls short only in respects of "trivial or inappreciable importance," made in good faith

  • Significance: A prerequisite, together with an unintentional breach, for a contractor seeking the diminution in value measure "as an instrument of justice"

  • Distinction: Schectman's $90,000 of undone work and his intentional position that he had no obligation to do it ruled it out

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Idiosyncratic value

  • Definition: Subjective or nonpecuniary value the owner places on property that market value does not capture, as with a personal residence or family farm

  • Significance: A rationale for cost-to-complete damages; Muris found that courts often take it into account without saying so; it is less likely when the plaintiff is a business

  • Distinction: It is the main gap that makes diminution in market value undercompensate

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Efficient breach

  • Definition: The idea, as previewed in this reading, that remedies should not discourage breach when performance would be economically wasteful, as in Posner's argument against cost-to-restore damages

  • Significance: Introduced in Note 3 following American Standard; treated later in the chapter

  • Distinction: It contrasts with the view in Emery and Groves that an owner may insist on its bargained-for performance whatever its market value

  • Case: American Standard, Inc. v. Schectman

 

Liquidated claim

  • Definition: A claim whose amount due and due date are "fixed and certain" or "definitely ascertainable by mathematical calculation"

  • Significance: Usually required for prejudgment interest unless the contract provides for interest

  • Distinction: It differs from a liquidated damages clause, which is not covered in this reading; a good faith dispute over the amount does not necessarily make a claim unliquidated (Blair)

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Prejudgment interest

  • Definition: Interest on the damages for the period between the accrual of the cause of action and the judgment

  • Significance: It compensates for lost use of the money over long litigation, such as Lukaszewski's 1978 to 1983 span; it is discretionary under Blair

  • Distinction: Postjudgment interest runs from the judgment, or perhaps the verdict, and is generally available by statute

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Postjudgment interest

  • Definition: Interest that accrues under local law at least from the date judgment is entered, and perhaps from the date of the verdict

  • Significance: Usually available to the successful party, for example under 28 U.S.C. § 1961 and N.Y. CPLR §§ 5003 and 5004

  • Distinction: Unlike prejudgment interest, it does not depend on whether the claim was liquidated

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Fixed-term employment contract

  • Definition: An employment contract binding the employee to the employer for a stated period of time

  • Significance: Only these contracts trigger the Lukaszewski damages rule against employees

  • Distinction: At-will employment can be ended by the employee without liability, and the reading notes no movement toward restricting employees' right to quit

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

At-will employment

  • Definition: An employment relationship either party may end without a fixed term

  • Significance: Employer terminations face some limits, but no comparable limits restrict employee terminations

  • Distinction: It contrasts with fixed-term contracts, where an employee who quits early is in breach

  • Case: Handicapped Children's Education Board v. Lukaszewski

 

Owner-opinion rule

  • Definition: The rule letting a property owner testify to the property's market value without being qualified as an expert

  • Significance: One way to prove market value under the contract–market formula; RWH Homebuilders requires a factual basis for the opinion

  • Distinction: It differs from expert appraisal testimony and from resale price evidence

  • Case: None (general terminology)
