Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group: Reading Notes (Civ Pro, Class 29: Removal)

1. Rules

Rule: Time-of-filing rule

  • Source: Case holding. Majority opinion (Scalia, J.) in Grupo Dataflux, relying on Mollan v. Torrance, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 537, 539 (1824) ("the jurisdiction of the court depends upon the state of things at the time of the action brought") and Conolly v. Taylor, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 556, 565 (1829)

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: Every challenge to subject-matter jurisdiction based on diversity of citizenship is measured against "the state of facts that existed at the time of filing." This applies "whether the challenge be brought shortly after filing, after the trial, or even for the first time on appeal." Under Conolly, "Where there is no change of party, a jurisdiction depending on the condition of the party is governed by that condition, as it was at the commencement of the suit"

  • Legal Question: At what point in time does a court decide whether diversity jurisdiction exists?

  • Requirements:

    1. The action is based on diversity of citizenship (28 U.S.C. § 1332)

    2. The parties' citizenship is judged as of the date the action was filed

    3. A later change in a continuing party's citizenship does not count

  • Application: A party uses it to challenge, or a court raises it to test, whether the parties were diverse when the complaint was filed (or when the case was removed). If diversity was missing then, dismissal is required even after years of litigation and a jury verdict. The Court applies the rule "regardless of the costs it imposes"

  • Exceptions:

    • A change in the parties, as opposed to a change in a continuing party's citizenship, can cure the defect. Dropping a dispensable nondiverse party under Rule 21 is the long-recognized exception (Horn v. Lockhart; Newman-Green; Caterpillar)

    • The Court "has never approved a deviation" beyond that and refused to create a new efficiency-based exception

    • Distinction: the dissent agreed the rule is "generally, … altogether sound" and disagreed only about multimember associations whose membership changes after filing

 

Rule: Citizenship of a partnership

  • Source: Case holding. Carden v. Arkoma Associates, 494 U.S. 185, 192–195 (1990), described in Grupo Dataflux as "the accepted rule"

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: For diversity purposes, a partnership "is a citizen of each State or foreign country of which any of its partners is a citizen"

  • Legal Question: How is a partnership's citizenship determined for diversity jurisdiction?

  • Requirements:

    1. Identify every partner of the partnership

    2. Determine each partner's citizenship (state or foreign country)

    3. Treat the partnership as a citizen of every one of those states and countries

  • Application: Atlas was a Texas limited partnership, but two of its partners were Mexican citizens when the suit was filed. That made Atlas a Mexican citizen, and also a citizen of Delaware and Texas because of its other partners. Atlas's own complaint had called it simply "a Texas citizen," which shows how a party can miss this rule

  • Exceptions: The reading states none

    • The majority treated the partnership as a single continuing party ("the single artificial entity created under Texas law") even though its membership changed. A change in membership changes the partnership's citizenship but not the identity of the party

    • The dissent would treat a change in membership like a change in the parties

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)

  • Source: Statute, as applied in Grupo Dataflux. The dissent calls it "the complete-diversity requirement of § 1332(a)"

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: Federal courts have diversity jurisdiction over state-law suits between diverse parties. The diversity must be complete, so that no plaintiff shares citizenship with any defendant

  • Legal Question: When can a federal court hear a state-law claim based on the parties' citizenship?

  • Requirements:

    1. The suit is between diverse parties, such as a citizen of a state and a "citizen or subject of" a foreign state, as Atlas pleaded

    2. Diversity is complete, meaning no overlap in citizenship across the "v."

    3. Diversity exists at the time of filing (time-of-filing rule)

  • Application: Atlas pleaded jurisdiction under § 1332(a) as a suit "between a Texas citizen and a citizen or subject of Mexico." Diversity actually failed because Atlas, through its partners, was also Mexican

  • Exceptions: The dissent called Atlas's initial failure to meet complete diversity a "statutory defect" that could be excused, as the removal defect was in Caterpillar. The majority treated the absence of diversity under § 1332 as a jurisdictional defect that cannot be excused

 

Rule: Aliens on both sides of a diversity action

  • Source: Case holding. Mossman v. Higginson, 4 U.S. (4 Dall.) 12, 14 (1800), cited in Grupo Dataflux

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: When aliens are on both sides of the case, "the requisite diversity" is absent

  • Legal Question: Is there diversity jurisdiction when a foreign citizen is on each side of the suit?

  • Requirements:

    1. At least one plaintiff is a foreign citizen, including by attribution through a partner

    2. At least one defendant is a foreign citizen

  • Application: Dataflux was a Mexican corporation, and Atlas was a Mexican citizen through its two Mexican partners. With aliens on both sides, diversity was lacking at filing

  • Exceptions: The reading states none. It does not matter that other parties, or other partners, are citizens of U.S. states

 

Rule: Timing of challenges to subject-matter jurisdiction

  • Source: Case holding and the Federal Rules. Capron v. Van Noorden, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 126 (1804), and Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1) and 12(h)(3), cited in Grupo Dataflux

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: "Challenges to subject-matter jurisdiction can of course be raised at any time prior to final judgment," including after trial or for the first time on appeal

  • Legal Question: How late in a case can a party object that the court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction?

  • Requirements: The objection is raised before final judgment

  • Application: Dataflux moved to dismiss on November 18, 2000. That was after the October 27 jury verdict against it but before judgment was entered, and the motion was timely

  • Exceptions: The reading states none for this rule. The dissent noted the "irony" of letting a defendant that "incorrectly conced[ed] federal subject-matter jurisdiction in its answer" wait until the jury ruled against it and then start over. The rule nonetheless allows that, and the majority accepted it

 

Rule: Rule 21 dismissal of a dispensable nondiverse party

  • Source: Fed. R. Civ. P. 21 and case law. Horn v. Lockhart, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 570, 579 (1873), and Newman-Green, Inc. v. Alfonzo-Larrain, 490 U.S. 826, 832 (1989), as described in Grupo Dataflux

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: "Rule 21 invests district courts with authority to allow a dispensable nondiverse party to be dropped at any time, even after judgment has been rendered." Courts of appeals also have authority to cure a jurisdictional defect by dismissing a dispensable nondiverse party

  • Legal Question: Can a court save diversity jurisdiction by dropping the party who destroys diversity?

  • Requirements:

    1. A party destroys complete diversity

    2. That party is dispensable, not indispensable. In Horn's words, "their interests are severable and a decree without prejudice to their rights may be made"

    3. The court drops the party "on motion of any party or of its own initiative at any stage of the action and on such terms as are just"

  • Application: This is the established exception to the time-of-filing rule. The less-than-complete diversity that existed throughout the case "had been converted to complete diversity between the remaining parties to the final judgment." This is how both Newman-Green and Caterpillar were resolved

  • Exceptions:

    • It does not apply to indispensable parties

    • It requires an actual change in the parties. A change in the citizenship of a continuing party, such as partners leaving a partnership, does not qualify (Grupo Dataflux)

 

Rule: Cure of a statutory removal defect

  • Source: Case holding. Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, 519 U.S. 61 (1996), as reinterpreted in Grupo Dataflux

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: A statutory defect does not require dismissal "once there was no longer any jurisdictional defect." The statutory defect in Caterpillar was "Caterpillar's failure to meet the § 1441(a) requirement that the case be fit for federal adjudication at the time the removal petition is filed." The reason given was that "once a diversity case has been tried in federal court … considerations of finality, efficiency, and economy become overwhelming"

  • Legal Question: Must a judgment in a removed case be vacated because complete diversity was missing at removal, if the jurisdictional defect was cured before trial and judgment?

  • Requirements:

    1. The case was removed without complete diversity at the time of removal, a violation of § 1441(a)

    2. The jurisdictional defect was later cured, in Caterpillar by dismissal of the diversity-destroying party

    3. The case was tried to judgment while complete diversity existed

  • Application: This is the rule Atlas and the Fifth Circuit relied on. The majority confined Caterpillar's finality and efficiency reasoning to statutory defects and refused to extend it to jurisdictional defects

  • Exceptions:

    • It does not excuse an uncured jurisdictional defect. Caterpillar's argument "took as its starting point that subject-matter jurisdiction had been satisfied"

    • The dissent would not limit it to removal cases. It argued that excusing flaws in removal that would be fatal to original jurisdiction would be "odd"

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1441(a)

  • Source: Statute, quoted in the Grupo Dataflux dissent and discussed by the majority in describing Caterpillar

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: "Any civil action brought in a State court of which the district courts of the United States have original jurisdiction, may be removed by the defendant or the defendants" to federal district court. In diversity cases, there must be complete diversity at the time of removal

  • Legal Question: When may a defendant move a state-court action to federal court?

  • Requirements:

    1. The action was brought in state court

    2. Federal district courts would have original jurisdiction over it

    3. The defendant or defendants remove it

    4. For diversity-based removal, complete diversity exists at the time of removal

  • Application: The dissent stressed that removal jurisdiction "is totally dependent on satisfaction of the requirements for original jurisdiction." On that view, Caterpillar's tolerance of a removal defect should carry over to original-jurisdiction cases like Atlas's

  • Exceptions: A violation of § 1441(a)'s timing requirement is a statutory, not jurisdictional, defect that can be excused once jurisdiction exists at trial and judgment (Caterpillar as read by the majority)

 

Rule: One-year limit on diversity-based removal

  • Source: Statute, mentioned in the Grupo Dataflux dissent's discussion of Caterpillar. The reading does not give the section number

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: There is a "statutory requirement that a diversity-based removal take place within one year of a lawsuit's commencement"

  • Legal Question: How long after a state suit begins can a defendant remove it on diversity grounds?

  • Requirements:

    1. Removal is based on diversity

    2. Removal occurs within one year of commencement of the suit

  • Application: The Caterpillar defendant removed "with only a day to spare." Had it waited in state court for the settlement that produced complete diversity, the one-year limit would have barred removal. The dissent used this to argue that Caterpillar involved more pressure for early, defective filing than Atlas's case, where there was no "fast-running limitations period"

  • Exceptions: The reading states none

 

Rule: Post-filing change in citizenship of a continuing party

  • Source: Case holding. Grupo Dataflux majority, applying Conolly v. Taylor

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: A party's change in citizenship after filing cannot cure a lack of diversity that existed at filing. This includes a change in a partnership's citizenship caused by partners leaving, because "the purported cure arose not from a change in the parties to the action, but from a change in the citizenship of a continuing party"

  • Legal Question: Can a party's change in citizenship after filing, including a change in a partnership's membership, create diversity jurisdiction that was missing at filing?

  • Requirements:

    1. Diversity was lacking at filing

    2. The same party remains in the action, such as the same "single artificial entity"

    3. Only that party's citizenship changed

  • Application: The Mexican partners' withdrawal the month before trial did not cure the defect, because Atlas stayed a party throughout. The case must be dismissed

  • Exceptions:

    • Contrast this with dropping a party (Rule 21), which does cure

    • The dissent would treat "the multimember enterprise with partially changed membership" like multiparty litigation in which some original parties drop out, so that the change would cure

 

Rule: Post-verdict cure exception

  • Source: Fifth Circuit's proposed exception, built on Caterpillar. The Grupo Dataflux majority rejected it and the dissent endorsed it

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: Under this rejected exception, the time-of-filing rule would give way when (1) an action is filed or removed without meeting jurisdictional requirements, (2) no one raises the error until after a jury verdict or dispositive ruling, and (3) the defect is cured before that verdict or ruling. "If at any point prior to the verdict or ruling, the issue is raised, the court must apply the general rule and dismiss regardless of subsequent changes in citizenship"

  • Legal Question: Should finality, efficiency, and economy allow a cure after filing to save jurisdiction once a case has been tried?

  • Requirements:

    1. Constitutional or statutory jurisdictional requirements were not met at filing or removal

    2. No party or the judge raised the error until after a jury verdict or dispositive ruling

    3. The jurisdictional defect was cured before the verdict or ruling

  • Application: The Fifth Circuit used it to reverse the dismissal and order judgment for Atlas. The Supreme Court held it is not the law

  • Exceptions: The majority's criticisms explain why it was rejected:

    • It is "unsound in principle," because either the court has jurisdiction when the defect is identified or it does not. Whether a verdict has been reached is irrelevant

    • It is "certain to be ignored in practice" and of "indeterminate scope"

    • Judge Garza's point: "there is no difference in efficiency terms between the jury verdict and, for example, the moment at which the jury retires"

 

Rule: Rule 1

  • Source: Fed. R. Civ. P. 1, invoked in the Grupo Dataflux dissent

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Statement: The Federal Rules aim at "the just, speedy, and inexpensive determination" of cases

  • Legal Question: What purpose should guide the interpretation of court-made procedural rules?

  • Requirements: The reading states none beyond the purpose itself

  • Application: The dissent argued that court-made rules like the time-of-filing rule "should not become immutable at the instant of their initial articulation" and should stay "adjustable in light of experience." In its words, "salvage operations are ordinarily preferable to the wrecking ball"

  • Exceptions: The majority answered that a stable rule saves more over time than case-by-case salvage, because uncertainty breeds wasteful "collateral litigation" over jurisdiction

2. Case Narrative

Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

Atlas Global Group, L.P. is a limited partnership formed under Texas law. It is the respondent in the Supreme Court and was the plaintiff in the original suit. Grupo Dataflux is a Mexican corporation. It is the petitioner in the Supreme Court and was the defendant in the original suit. The opinion does not describe the underlying business relationship. It does not say what deal the two companies made, what Atlas did for Dataflux, when those dealings happened, or where. All it tells us is that Atlas believed Dataflux owed it money, on a contract theory and alternatively in quantum meruit, in an amount over $1.3 million.

The key real-world fact is who belonged to the Atlas partnership. Atlas thought of itself, and described itself, as "a Texas citizen." In fact, when it sued, two of its partners were Mexican citizens. Its other partners made it a citizen of Delaware and Texas as well. Then, in a transaction completed in September 2000 (the month before trial began in October 2000), the Mexican partners left the partnership. After that, no Mexican citizens remained among Atlas's partners. The opinion does not say why the Mexican partners left or whether their departure had anything to do with the lawsuit. The dissent notes that no one accused anyone of manipulation.

What went wrong between the parties, in plain terms, is that Atlas claimed Dataflux did not pay for what it owed under their arrangement. The opinion gives no further detail about the breakdown. In November 1997, Atlas sued Dataflux in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas for breach of contract and quantum meruit, seeking over $1.3 million. It relied on diversity jurisdiction as a suit between a Texas citizen and a citizen of Mexico.

3. Cases

Case Name: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

  • Document: 1005-13

  • Course: Civil Procedure (Class 29: Removal; Hubbard, Civil Procedure: An Integrated Approach, pp. 1005–13)

  • Facts

    • Atlas, a Texas limited partnership, sued Dataflux, a Mexican corporation, in November 1997 in the Southern District of Texas on state-law claims for breach of contract and quantum meruit, seeking over $1.3 million

    • Atlas alleged diversity jurisdiction under § 1332(a) as a suit "between a Texas citizen and a citizen or subject of Mexico"

    • At filing, two of Atlas's partners were Mexican citizens, which made Atlas a Mexican citizen (and also a Delaware and Texas citizen). Aliens were therefore on both sides

    • Pretrial motions and discovery took almost three years, and no one noticed the jurisdictional problem

    • The Mexican partners left Atlas in a transaction completed the month before trial

    • In October 2000 the parties consented to a jury trial before a Magistrate Judge. On October 27, after a 6-day trial, the jury awarded Atlas $750,000

    • On November 18, before judgment was entered, Dataflux moved to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Dataflux had earlier conceded jurisdiction in its answer, according to the dissent

    • After the dismissal, Atlas filed a new action but later dismissed it without prejudice

    • The parties litigated for more than 6½ years, including 3½ years over a jurisdictional defect Atlas conceded

  • Procedural Posture:

    • U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas: after a jury verdict for Plaintiff Atlas of $750,000 in a trial before a Magistrate Judge by consent, the Magistrate Judge granted Defendant Dataflux's motion to dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under Rules 12(b)(1) and 12(h)(3) before entry of judgment, because the parties were not diverse when the complaint was filed

    • Appeal of right: Plaintiff-Appellant Atlas sought review in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on the grounds that dismissal was inappropriate because the Mexican partners had left the partnership before trial, so diversity existed when the jury returned its verdict

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit: reversed and remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Atlas, holding under Caterpillar that the time-of-filing rule gives way when a jurisdictional defect existing at filing is not raised until after a verdict or dispositive ruling and is cured before that verdict or ruling

    • Petition for writ of certiorari: Defendant-Petitioner Dataflux sought review in the U.S. Supreme Court on the grounds that a post-filing change in citizenship cannot cure a lack of diversity existing at filing and that Caterpillar is a ruling limited to removal cases

    • U.S. Supreme Court: Dataflux (defendant below) is the petitioner; Atlas (plaintiff below) is the respondent; certiorari granted to address whether a party's post-filing change in citizenship can cure a lack of subject-matter jurisdiction that existed at filing in a diversity action

  • Issue: Whether a party's change in citizenship after filing, here the withdrawal of a partnership's Mexican partners, can cure a lack of diversity jurisdiction that existed when the action was filed, where the defect was not raised until after a jury verdict?

  • Holding: The U.S. Supreme Court held 5–4 that it cannot. The time-of-filing rule controls, and a change in the citizenship of a continuing party, including a change in a partnership's membership, does not cure a jurisdictional defect that existed at filing. Only a change in the parties, such as dropping a dispensable nondiverse party, does

  • Rule:

    • Time-of-filing rule: Diversity jurisdiction is measured by the facts at filing, and "where there is no change of party," jurisdiction is governed by the party's condition "as it was at the commencement of the suit"

    • Citizenship of a partnership: A partnership is a citizen of each state or foreign country of which any of its partners is a citizen

    • Aliens on both sides of a diversity action: Diversity is absent when aliens are on both sides of the case

    • Timing of challenges to subject-matter jurisdiction: Challenges to subject-matter jurisdiction may be raised at any time before final judgment

    • Rule 21 dismissal of a dispensable nondiverse party: Dropping a dispensable nondiverse party, even after judgment, cures a diversity defect

    • Cure of a statutory removal defect: A § 1441(a) defect from removal without complete diversity does not require dismissal once no jurisdictional defect remains at trial and judgment

    • Post-filing change in citizenship of a continuing party: A change in a continuing party's citizenship after filing cannot cure a lack of diversity that existed at filing

    • Post-verdict cure exception: The Fifth Circuit's exception allowing a cure before verdict to save jurisdiction when the defect is first raised after verdict is rejected

  • Reasoning:

    • The Supreme Court reasoned that the time-of-filing rule is nearly two centuries old, "hornbook law," and applied "regardless of the costs it imposes." It was uncontested that applying the rule required dismissal

    • Caterpillar "broke no new ground." Its defect was cured by dismissing the party who destroyed diversity, the long-established exception recognized in Horn v. Lockhart, Rule 21, and Newman-Green

    • Caterpillar's statement about "finality, efficiency, and economy" answered a different question: whether to excuse a statutory defect (§ 1441(a)'s requirement of complete diversity at removal) once jurisdiction was satisfied. It did not address curing a jurisdictional defect

    • Under Conolly, the only recognized cure is a "change of party." The Mexican partners' withdrawal changed Atlas's composition and citizenship, but Atlas, "the single artificial entity created under Texas law," remained the same party. The Court "decline[d] to do today what the Court has refused to do for the past 175 years"

    • The Fifth Circuit's exception would be "of indeterminate scope." Its cutoff at the verdict is "unsound in principle," because either the court has jurisdiction when the defect is identified or it does not. The line is also arbitrary in efficiency terms (Judge Garza), since huge costs are often spent long before trial

    • Answering the dissent's waste concern: if the parties expect the same outcome they will likely settle, using the verdict as a range, and even a retrial would be quick after three years of discovery

    • Overall, the dissent's approach is the wasteful one. Unsettled jurisdictional rules breed jurisdictional litigation. Atlas could have refiled immediately (it did, then dropped the new suit) and probably would have had a merits resolution by now. Instead the parties spent 3½ years litigating jurisdiction, compared with the one month the Magistrate Judge needed to apply the rule

    • "Uncertainty regarding the question of jurisdiction is particularly undesirable," and stability "weighs heavily against the approval of any new deviation"

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments:

    • Atlas, the plaintiff below and respondent, did not dispute that diversity was lacking at filing

    • Diversity existed when the jury returned its verdict because the Mexican partners had left the month before trial, so dismissal was inappropriate

    • The Court should "accept the very limited exception created by the Fifth Circuit," relying on Caterpillar's statement that once a diversity case has been tried, "considerations of finality, efficiency, and economy become overwhelming"

    • Caterpillar is only "technically" distinguishable, because dismissal of a party "was not at the heart of the Supreme Court's analysis"

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • Dataflux, the defendant below and petitioner, argued that the parties were not diverse when the complaint was filed, because Atlas's Mexican partners made it a Mexican citizen and aliens were on both sides

    • Under the time-of-filing rule, later changes in citizenship are irrelevant

    • Caterpillar is a ruling limited to removal cases

    • A change in a partnership's membership should be treated like an individual plaintiff changing her citizenship, not like a change in the parties

  • Disposition: The U.S. Supreme Court reversed the Fifth Circuit's judgment, leaving the dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction in place

  • Importance:

    • The case confirms the strict time-of-filing rule for diversity jurisdiction and limits the cure for a jurisdictional defect to a change in the parties (Rule 21), not a change in a continuing party's citizenship

    • It is in the Removal unit because it reinterprets Caterpillar v. Lewis. Caterpillar excuses only a statutory removal defect (§ 1441(a)) after the jurisdictional defect has been cured by dropping a party. It does not create a general exception for "finality and efficiency"

    • Distinguish jurisdictional defects from statutory defects. The first can never be waived or excused and can be raised before final judgment; the second can be overlooked once jurisdiction exists

    • It shows how partnership citizenship (Carden) can quietly defeat diversity. Always check every partner's citizenship, including foreign citizens, against the opposing party

    • Dissent (Ginsburg, J., joined by Stevens, Souter, and Breyer, JJ.), useful for policy arguments:

      • It would treat a change in a multimember entity's membership like a party dropping out

      • It found Caterpillar's efficiency logic equally applicable to the § 1332(a) defect

      • It doubted settlement would happen, since the parties twice passed up chances to settle

      • It noted the "irony" that Dataflux conceded jurisdiction in its answer yet got "an unmerited second chance" after losing

      • It saw no charge of manipulation and no race against a limitations period, unlike the one-year removal deadline in Caterpillar

      • It invoked Rule 1 to argue that procedural rules should stay "adjustable in light of experience"

    • Possible exam angles: a mid-case change in citizenship or membership (no cure) versus dropping a dispensable party (cure), and the timing of jurisdictional objections and the incentives it creates

4. Definitions

Time-of-filing rule

  • Definition: The principle that "the jurisdiction of the court depends upon the state of things at the time of the action brought," so diversity is measured by the facts that existed when the suit was filed

  • Significance: It is the rule that controlled the outcome. Atlas lacked diversity at filing, and later events could not change that

  • Distinction: It differs from the dissent's flexible approach and from the Rule 21 exception, which looks to the parties left at final judgment after a party is dropped

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Subject-matter jurisdiction

  • Definition: A court's authority to hear a category of case. Here, federal authority depended on diversity of citizenship under § 1332

  • Significance: A defect in it can be raised at any time before final judgment under Rules 12(b)(1) and 12(h)(3) and Capron v. Van Noorden, even by a party that earlier conceded it

  • Distinction: It differs from statutory procedural requirements such as § 1441(a)'s timing rule, which the majority treats as excusable once jurisdiction exists

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Diversity jurisdiction

  • Definition: Federal jurisdiction over state-law claims based on the parties' citizenship under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), for example a suit between a state citizen and a "citizen or subject of" a foreign state

  • Significance: It was Atlas's only basis for federal jurisdiction over its contract and quantum meruit claims

  • Distinction: The reading distinguishes it from removal jurisdiction, which depends on original jurisdiction under § 1441(a)

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Complete diversity

  • Definition: The requirement that no party on one side share citizenship with any party on the other side

  • Significance: It was lacking at filing in Grupo Dataflux (Mexican citizens on both sides) and at removal in Caterpillar (a Kentucky plaintiff and a Kentucky defendant)

  • Distinction: The majority treats its absence under § 1332 as jurisdictional. The dissent calls Atlas's initial failure a "statutory defect" comparable to the § 1441(a) defect in Caterpillar

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Alien

  • Definition: As used in the reading, a "citizen or subject" of a foreign country, such as Mexico. The reading does not define the term further

  • Significance: Aliens on both sides of a case destroy diversity (Mossman v. Higginson)

  • Distinction: A foreign corporation like Dataflux is an alien party directly. A U.S. partnership like Atlas can be an alien through its foreign partners

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Limited partnership

  • Definition: In the reading, a business entity "created under Texas law" that the majority calls a "single artificial entity," whose citizenship is that of each of its partners (Carden)

  • Significance: Because Atlas stayed the same party even as its partners changed, the change in citizenship was not a "change of party"

  • Distinction: The majority treats a partnership's membership change like an individual changing domicile. The dissent treats it like multiparty litigation in which some parties drop out

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Dispensable party

  • Definition: A party whose interests "are severable" so that "a decree without prejudice to their rights may be made" (Horn v. Lockhart)

  • Significance: A dispensable nondiverse party can be dropped under Rule 21 at any time, even after judgment or on appeal, to cure a diversity defect

  • Distinction: An indispensable party cannot be dropped this way. The reading uses that term but does not define it beyond contrast with Horn's test

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group (discussing Newman-Green and Caterpillar)

 

Rule 21

  • Definition: The Federal Rule providing that "parties may be dropped or added by order of the court on motion of any party or of its own initiative at any stage of the action and on such terms as are just"

  • Significance: It is the established vehicle for the one recognized cure of a diversity defect after filing

  • Distinction: It works by changing the parties, unlike a change in a continuing party's citizenship, which cannot cure

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Removal

  • Definition: A defendant's transfer of a civil action brought in state court to federal district court, available when federal courts would have original jurisdiction (§ 1441(a))

  • Significance: Caterpillar was a removal case, and the majority read its holding as excusing only a removal-statute defect

  • Distinction: Grupo Dataflux itself was filed originally in federal court, not removed. The dissent argued that the distinction should not matter

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group (discussing Caterpillar)

 

Statutory defect

  • Definition: In the majority's usage, a failure to comply with a statutory requirement other than subject-matter jurisdiction itself. In Caterpillar, this was § 1441(a)'s requirement that the case be fit for federal adjudication when removed

  • Significance: It can be excused for "finality, efficiency, and economy" once no jurisdictional defect remains

  • Distinction: The majority contrasts it with a jurisdictional defect, which cannot be excused. The dissent labeled Atlas's § 1332(a) problem a "statutory defect" too, blurring the line

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Jurisdictional defect

  • Definition: The absence of subject-matter jurisdiction, here the lack of diversity at filing

  • Significance: It requires dismissal unless cured by a change in the parties. Efficiency considerations cannot excuse it

  • Distinction: Contrast it with a statutory defect, and note the difference between curing it by dropping a party and attempting to cure it by a change in citizenship

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Ratio decidendi

  • Definition: As used in the reading, the essential rationale on which a decision rests. The majority acknowledged that Caterpillar's finality statement "unquestionably provided the ratio decidendi in Caterpillar"

  • Significance: The majority conceded that the statement was Caterpillar's core reasoning but limited it to the issue it addressed, the statutory defect

  • Distinction: The reading does not contrast it with another term

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Quantum meruit

  • Definition: The reading identifies it only as a state-law claim Atlas pleaded alongside breach of contract and does not define it

  • Significance: Together with the contract claim, it shows the suit was purely state-law, so federal jurisdiction depended entirely on diversity

  • Distinction: The reading pairs it with, but does not distinguish it from, breach of contract

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group

 

Dismissal without prejudice

  • Definition: As used in the reading, a dismissal that leaves the plaintiff free to bring the claim again. The reading does not define it further

  • Significance: Atlas filed a second action after the jurisdictional dismissal but later dismissed it without prejudice. The majority said pursuing that suit would likely have produced a merits resolution by now

  • Distinction: The reading does not discuss dismissal with prejudice

  • Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group
