1. Rules

Rule: Claim preclusion (bar) on the same claim or demand

  • Source: Case holding, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876), stating the first of the two effects of a judgment (casebook p. 755)

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: Where the second action is "upon the same claim or demand," a judgment "rendered upon the merits" is "an absolute bar to a subsequent action"; it binds the parties and those in privity with them "not only as to every matter which was offered and received to sustain or defeat the claim or demand, but as to any other admissible matter which might have been offered for that purpose"

  • Legal Question: When a second suit is on the same claim or demand as a prior suit, what effect does the prior judgment have?

  • Requirements:

    1. A prior judgment rendered upon the merits

    2. A second action upon the same claim or demand

    3. The same parties, or those in privity with them

  • Application: A party invokes it to bar the whole second action, not just particular issues; it covers grounds and defenses that were never raised, so in the promissory-note example a judgment on the note settles the instrument's validity and the amount due, even if defenses such as forgery, want of consideration, or payment really existed but were never proved; the claim "cannot again be brought into litigation between the parties in proceedings at law upon any ground whatever"

  • Exceptions: The judgment must be "upon the merits"; the "might have been offered" scope applies only "when applied to the demand or claim in controversy" and not when the second suit is on a different claim, where the issue-preclusion rule below governs instead; the Court calls the "every ground which might have been presented" formulation "strictly accurate" only in this same-claim setting

 

Rule: Issue preclusion (estoppel) on a different claim or demand

  • Source: Case holding, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876), stating the second effect of a judgment (casebook pp. 755–56)

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: Where the second action between the same parties is "upon a different claim or demand," the prior judgment "operates as an estoppel only as to those matters in issue or points controverted, upon the determination of which the finding or verdict was rendered"; the inquiry is always "the point or question actually litigated and determined in the original action, not what might have been thus litigated and determined"

  • Legal Question: When a second suit is on a different claim between the same parties, which matters from the prior judgment bind the parties?

  • Requirements:

    1. A prior judgment between the same parties (or privies)

    2. A second action upon a different claim or cause of action

    3. The point or question was actually in issue or controverted in the first action

    4. The point was actually determined

    5. The finding or verdict was rendered upon the determination of that point

  • Application: A party invokes it to stop the opponent from relitigating a specific issue rather than the whole claim; the court looks at what the first case actually decided, here by "reading the record of the lower court by the opinion and judgment" of the Supreme Court in the Smith appeal; in Cromwell this stopped the plaintiff from denying that the bonds were invalid against the county, but it did not stop him from proving that he was a bona fide holder for value

  • Exceptions: Matters that might have been litigated but were not are not precluded; the Court said no cases go so far as to exclude a "possible question" in a second action on a different demand, and dismissed contrary language as "loose remarks"; a finding that a party failed to prove a fact in the first case is not a determination that the fact is false, so it does not bar proving that fact as to a different instrument

 

Rule: Rationale for limiting issue preclusion to matters actually litigated

  • Source: Case holding/reasoning, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876) (casebook p. 756)

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: Because "[v]arious considerations, other than the actual merits, may govern a party in bringing forward grounds of recovery or defence in one action," a party acting on those considerations "ought not to be precluded from contesting in a subsequent action other demands arising out of the same transaction"

  • Legal Question: Why should a judgment on one demand not bar issues that could have been, but were not, raised, when the later demand arises from the same transaction?

  • Requirements:

    1. The later action is on a different demand

    2. The party did not raise the ground or defense in the earlier action

    3. The party's choice may have rested on non-merits considerations, such as "the smallness of the amount or the value of the property in controversy, the difficulty of obtaining the necessary evidence, the expense of the litigation, and his own situation at the time"

  • Application: Supports a party arguing that a small or strategically limited first suit should not decide everything in a larger later suit; in Cromwell the Court treated suits on different coupons and bonds as "different demands" even though they arose "out of the same transaction"

  • Exceptions: Does not apply when the second action is on the same claim or demand, where everything that might have been offered is barred; does not protect issues that were actually litigated and determined

 

Rule: Limited effect of a judgment by default

  • Source: Case reasoning, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876) (casebook p. 756)

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: "A judgment by default only admits for the purpose of the action the legality of the demand or claim in suit: it does not make the allegations of the declaration or complaint evidence in an action upon a different claim"

  • Legal Question: Does a default judgment establish the complaint's allegations for use in a later action on a different claim?

  • Requirements:

    1. A judgment entered by default

    2. A later action upon a different claim

  • Application: Used to resist issue preclusion based on a default, because nothing was actually litigated; the Court notes that a declaration may contain different, even inconsistent, statements of the cause of action in different counts, and "[i]t could hardly be pretended" that a default makes all of them evidence elsewhere

  • Exceptions: Within the same action, the default does admit the legality of the demand or claim in suit; the reading does not address the default judgment's claim-preclusive effect on the same claim beyond this

 

Rule: Preclusion binds parties and those in privity, including the real party in interest

  • Source: Case holding, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876) (casebook pp. 754–55), together with the casebook editor's bracketed note summarizing omitted portions of the opinion

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: A judgment concludes "parties and those in privity with them," and a person for whose "sole use and benefit" a prior action was prosecuted, and who owned the claim sued on, is treated as the same party as the nominal plaintiff in that action

  • Legal Question: Who is bound by a prior judgment for preclusion purposes, and can a person who was not the named plaintiff be bound?

  • Requirements:

    1. The person was a named party, or was in privity with a party

    2. For a non-named person to be bound as the real party in interest, proof that the person owned the claim sued on in the prior action and that the action was prosecuted for that person's sole use and benefit

  • Application: The County of Sac introduced evidence that Cromwell owned the coupons in Smith's suit and that the suit was prosecuted for Cromwell's sole use and benefit; the court below found Cromwell was the real party in interest, and the Supreme Court agreed in portions of the opinion omitted from the casebook, per the editor's note

  • Exceptions: The excerpt omits the Court's full privity analysis, so the attached reading does not set out further limits on who counts as in privity

 

Rule: Bona fide holder of authorized negotiable county bonds

  • Source: Case holding, Cromwell v. County of Sac, 94 U.S. 351 (1876) (casebook pp. 756–57), describing the substantive law the Court applied and the holding of the earlier Smith appeal

  • Document: 754-57

  • Statement: Negotiable county bonds whose issue was authorized by a county vote, and which recite on their face compliance with the authorizing law, are "valid obligations against the county in the hands of a bona fide holder taking them for value before maturity," even if fraud or illegality tainted their issue; but where the facts show "fraud and illegality in the inception of the bonds," the holder must show that he gave value

  • Legal Question: When can a holder of fraudulently issued negotiable county bonds or coupons still enforce them against the county?

  • Requirements:

    1. Negotiable instruments

    2. Issue authorized by a vote of the county

    3. A recital on their face of compliance with the law providing for their issue

    4. A holder who took them before maturity

    5. A holder who gave value, which the holder must prove once evidence of fraud or illegality in their inception appears

  • Application: This substantive rule is what made the preclusion question matter: the Smith judgment conclusively established that the bonds were void against the county except in the hands of holders for value before maturity, which left open whether Cromwell was such a holder of the different bonds and coupons now in suit

  • Exceptions: The bonds are void as against the county in the hands of parties who did not acquire them before maturity and give value; a holder's failure to show value for one bond or coupon "is not even presumptive, much less conclusive, evidence that he may not have given value for another and different bond or coupon"

2. Case Narrative

Cromwell v. County of Sac

Cromwell is the plaintiff, the holder of bonds and attached interest coupons issued by the County of Sac. The County of Sac, the defendant, is a county government in the State of Iowa. Cromwell sued the county to collect on four of its bonds and four interest coupons.

The story begins with a public construction project. The people of Sac County voted to authorize the county to issue $10,000 in bonds to pay for building a court-house. In 1860 the bonds were issued. They were payable to bearer in New York City, came due in 1868, 1869, 1870, and 1871, and carried 10% annual interest, paid through coupons attached to the bonds. The county judge issued the full $10,000 in bonds and delivered them to Meserey, a contractor he had hired to build the court-house. As soon as Meserey received the bonds, he gave one of them to the county judge as a "gratuity." The court-house was never built, by Meserey or by anyone else under the contract. The county was left owing on bonds for a building it never received, and the bond issue was tainted by fraud and illegality from the start.

The bonds and coupons then moved into other hands. Before maturity, Samuel C. Smith became the holder of twenty-five of the earlier-maturing coupons, and he sued the county on them. There was no finding that he ever paid value for them. Although Smith was the named plaintiff, Cromwell actually owned the coupons in that suit, and it was prosecuted for Cromwell's sole use and benefit. The county won: the court held the bonds void as against the county, and Smith (really Cromwell) recovered nothing.

Cromwell then went after a different set of instruments: four $1,000 bonds and four $100 coupons from the same bond issue. He was prepared to prove that he received these bonds and coupons before maturity and gave value for them. The opinion does not say exactly when Cromwell acquired them, and apart from the Iowa county and the New York place of payment, it does not say where the relevant transactions took place.

Cromwell sued the County of Sac to recover the amounts due on the four bonds and four coupons. The county defended by arguing that the earlier judgment in Smith's suit already settled the matter against him.

3. Cases

Case Name: Cromwell v. County of Sac

  • Document: 754-57

  • Course: Civil Procedure (Class 18, Preclusion; Hubbard, Civil Procedure: An Integrated Approach pp. 754–57)

  • Facts

    • The County of Sac, Iowa, by a vote of its people, authorized $10,000 in bonds to build a court-house; the bonds were issued in 1860, payable to bearer in New York City in 1868–1871, with 10% annual interest coupons attached

    • The county judge issued the bonds to Meserey, the building contractor, who immediately gave one bond to the county judge as a gratuity; the court-house was never built

    • In an earlier action, Samuel C. Smith sued the county on twenty-five earlier-maturing coupons from the same bonds, which he held before maturity; there was no finding that he gave value for them

    • In Smith's action the lower court held the bonds void as against the county; on writ of error the Supreme Court affirmed, holding that the facts were sufficient evidence of fraud and illegality in the bonds' inception to require the holder to show he gave value, and he had not done so

    • Cromwell was the actual owner of the coupons in Smith's action, and that action was prosecuted for his sole use and benefit

    • In the present action Cromwell sued on four different $1,000 bonds and four $100 coupons from the same issue

    • The county relied on the Smith judgment as an estoppel, together with proof connecting Cromwell to that action as the real party in interest

    • Cromwell offered to prove that he received the bonds and coupons in suit before maturity and for value, and that evidence was excluded

  • Procedural Posture:

    • Trial court (the opinion does not state its name and calls it only "the court below"): treated the Smith judgment as an estoppel against Cromwell, found Cromwell was the real party in interest in the Smith action, excluded Cromwell's offered evidence that he received the bonds and coupons before maturity for value, and entered judgment against Cromwell after trial (the opinion does not state whether the trial was to a jury or to the bench)

    • Review in the U.S. Supreme Court (the opinion does not state the vehicle for this case; it describes the earlier Smith case as coming up "on writ of error"): Cromwell, Plaintiff-Appellant (plaintiff below), sought review in the U.S. Supreme Court on the grounds that the Smith judgment did not estop him from proving he was a bona fide holder for value before maturity, that excluding his evidence was erroneous, and that evidence connecting him to the Smith action as the real party in interest was inadmissible

    • U.S. Supreme Court: Cromwell (plaintiff below) is the appellant; the County of Sac (defendant below) is the appellee; the opinion does not state how the case arrived beyond presenting questions about the judgment's estoppel effect and the admissibility of the evidence

  • Issue: Whether a judgment for the county in an earlier action on certain coupons, prosecuted for Cromwell's benefit, bars Cromwell's later action on different bonds and coupons from the same issue, either entirely as claim preclusion or as to his proof that he was a bona fide holder for value before maturity, when the earlier judgment rested on the holder's failure to show he gave value?

  • Holding: The U.S. Supreme Court held that because the second action was on a different claim or demand, the Smith judgment operated only as an issue-preclusive estoppel on matters actually litigated and determined; Cromwell was estopped from denying that the bonds were invalid as against the county except in the hands of holders for value before maturity, but he was not precluded from proving that he acquired these different bonds and coupons before maturity for value, because that point was never adjudicated

  • Rule:

    • Claim preclusion (bar) on the same claim or demand: A judgment on the merits is an absolute bar to a later action on the same claim or demand as to every matter offered and every admissible matter that might have been offered, binding parties and their privies

    • Issue preclusion (estoppel) on a different claim or demand: In a later action between the same parties on a different claim, the judgment is conclusive only as to points actually litigated and determined, on whose determination the finding or verdict was rendered, and not as to what might have been litigated

    • Rationale for limiting issue preclusion to matters actually litigated: A party may have non-merits reasons for not raising grounds in one action and should not be precluded from contesting other demands arising out of the same transaction

    • Limited effect of a judgment by default: A default judgment admits the legality of the demand only for the purpose of that action and does not make the complaint's allegations evidence in an action on a different claim

    • Preclusion binds parties and those in privity, including the real party in interest: A judgment binds the person for whose sole use and benefit the prior action was prosecuted and who owned the claim sued on

    • Bona fide holder of authorized negotiable county bonds: Authorized negotiable county bonds reciting compliance with law are valid against the county in the hands of a bona fide holder taking them for value before maturity, though evidence of fraud in their inception requires the holder to show value

  • Reasoning: The U.S. Supreme Court reasoned in steps

    • The U.S. Supreme Court first distinguished a judgment's effect as a bar against a second action on the same claim from its effect as an estoppel in an action on a different claim, and noted that both are loosely called "estoppel"

    • For the same claim, the judgment bars every ground that was or might have been presented, as the promissory-note example shows: a judgment on a note settles the instrument's validity and the amount due even if unraised defenses like forgery, want of consideration, or payment actually existed

    • For a different claim, the inquiry is limited to what was actually litigated and determined, and no cases extend preclusion to questions that merely might have arisen in the first action

    • Limiting preclusion this way is justified because litigants may forgo grounds for non-merits reasons, such as a small amount in controversy, difficulty obtaining evidence, litigation expense, or their personal situation, and should not lose other demands from the same transaction as a result

    • The same logic applies to a default judgment, which admits the claim's legality only for that action and does not turn the complaint's allegations, which may even be inconsistent across counts, into evidence elsewhere

    • Applying this, the U.S. Supreme Court treated the suit on different bonds and coupons as a different demand and read the Smith record in light of its own prior opinion to identify what was actually adjudged: (1) the bonds were void against the county in the hands of those who did not acquire them before maturity for value, and (2) that plaintiff, having not proved value, could not recover on those coupons

    • The fraud and illegality in issuing the bonds affected the coupons equally, so the finding of invalidity against the county estops Cromwell from asserting otherwise

    • The bonds were negotiable, authorized by county vote, and recited compliance with law, so they would be valid against the county in the hands of a bona fide holder taking them for value before maturity

    • The prior finding was only that the plaintiff had not made such proof in that case, and failing to show value for one bond or coupon is "not even presumptive, much less conclusive, evidence" about value given for a different one, so Cromwell should have been allowed to make the proof

    • Excluding Cromwell's evidence was therefore error

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Cromwell (plaintiff below, appellant here) argued as follows

    • He received the bonds and coupons in suit before maturity and for value, and he offered to prove it

    • The Smith judgment, on different coupons, should not foreclose that proof

    • The evidence connecting him to Smith's action as the real party in interest was inadmissible, which the opinion presents as one of the questions for decision; the Court's resolution of that question is in portions omitted from the casebook

    • The opinion notes that "counsel" stated the distinction between a judgment's effect as a bar on the same claim and as an estoppel on a different claim, but it does not say which side's counsel

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments: The County of Sac (defendant below, appellee here) argued as follows

    • The judgment for the county in Smith's earlier action on coupons from the same bonds estopped Cromwell from prosecuting this action

    • Cromwell was bound by that judgment because he owned the coupons in Smith's action and that action was prosecuted for his sole use and benefit

  • Disposition: The U.S. Supreme Court reversed the judgment and remanded the cause for a new trial; Justice Clifford's dissent is omitted from the casebook

  • Importance

    • Cromwell is the foundational statement of the two branches of preclusion: claim preclusion, which bars everything that was or might have been raised on the same claim, and issue preclusion, which binds only on points actually litigated and determined when the later claim is different

    • It answers the casebook's framing question: although the opinion calls both doctrines "estoppel," the key language is "same claim or demand" versus "different claim or demand," and "might have been offered" versus "actually litigated and determined"; issue preclusion is what applied here

    • It teaches the precise-scope inquiry for issue preclusion: a court must identify exactly what the first judgment decided, and a finding that a party failed to prove a fact is not a finding that the fact is false

    • It shows the policy behind narrow issue preclusion: litigants make non-merits strategic choices in small cases, and those choices should not cascade into larger later suits

    • It shows that preclusion can bind a non-named person who is the real party in interest

    • Exam angle: the Court treated suits on different coupons and bonds as "different demands" even though they arose "out of the same transaction," so be ready to identify how Cromwell defines what counts as the same claim; the attached reading does not provide any other test

4. Definitions

Claim preclusion (bar)

  • Definition: The effect of a judgment on the merits as an absolute bar to a later action on the same claim or demand, covering every matter that was offered and every admissible matter that might have been offered

  • Significance: It is the first of the two effects the Court described; the county's defense would have barred Cromwell's entire suit only if the new suit was on the same claim, and the Court found it was not

  • Distinction: It reaches unraised grounds ("might have been offered"), while issue preclusion reaches only points actually litigated and determined; claim preclusion bars the whole action, while issue preclusion bars only particular issues

 

Issue preclusion

  • Definition: The effect of a judgment in a later action between the same parties on a different claim, conclusive only as to "matters in issue or points controverted, upon the determination of which the finding or verdict was rendered"

  • Significance: This is the doctrine the Court actually applied; it estopped Cromwell on the bonds' invalidity against the county but left him free to prove he was a holder for value before maturity

  • Distinction: It is limited to what was actually litigated and determined, "not what might have been thus litigated and determined," in contrast to claim preclusion

 

Estoppel (as used in Cromwell)

  • Definition: The opinion's umbrella term for a judgment's preclusive effect, used for both the bar on the same claim and the issue-specific effect on a different claim

  • Significance: The casebook warns that the Court's use of "estoppel" for both doctrines makes it hard to tell which it means, so the reader must look at the surrounding language ("same claim" versus "different claim")

  • Distinction: Read "estoppel... upon the same claim" as claim preclusion, and "estoppel only as to those matters in issue" on a different claim as issue preclusion

 

Claim or demand / cause of action

  • Definition: The right sued upon in an action; in Cromwell, each separate set of coupons and bonds sued on was treated as a separate demand

  • Significance: Whether the second action is on the "same" or a "different" claim decides which preclusion doctrine applies; Cromwell's suit on different bonds and coupons was a different demand even though they arose from the same transaction

  • Distinction: The reading separates the "claim or demand" from the "transaction" it arises out of; different demands can arise from the same transaction, and the attached reading gives no further test for sameness

 

Judgment upon the merits

  • Definition: A judgment that decides the claim itself, which is the prerequisite the Court states for the absolute bar of claim preclusion

  • Significance: Only a judgment "rendered upon the merits" is a "finality as to the claim or demand in controversy"

  • Distinction: The reading does not define which judgments fall outside this category, apart from its separate discussion of the limited effect of default judgments

 

Judgment by default

  • Definition: A judgment entered without the claim being contested, which "only admits for the purpose of the action the legality of the demand or claim in suit"

  • Significance: The Court uses it to show that uncontested matters are not issue-preclusive in later actions on different claims, because the complaint's allegations do not become evidence elsewhere

  • Distinction: Unlike a contested judgment, it establishes no actually litigated points for issue preclusion in an action on a different claim

 

Privity / real party in interest

  • Definition: Privity is the relationship that makes a non-party bound by a judgment as if it were a party; a real party in interest is the person who actually owns the claim and for whose use and benefit the suit is prosecuted, even if someone else is the named plaintiff

  • Significance: Cromwell was bound by the judgment in Smith's suit because he owned those coupons and the suit was prosecuted for his sole use and benefit, so the parties in both suits were treated as the same

  • Distinction: This addresses who is bound, while claim and issue preclusion address what is precluded; the casebook omits the Court's full discussion of this point

 

Bona fide holder for value before maturity

  • Definition: A holder who took a negotiable instrument before it came due and gave value for it, and who can enforce authorized county bonds against the county despite fraud or illegality in their issue

  • Significance: This status was the open factual question; the Smith judgment determined only that the plaintiff there had not proved it, not that Cromwell lacked it as to the bonds and coupons in this suit

  • Distinction: A holder who did not acquire before maturity and give value takes bonds that are void as against the county when fraud or illegality tainted their inception

 

Negotiable instrument / bearer bond

  • Definition: A transferable written obligation to pay; the Sac County bonds were "payable to bearer," meaning payable to whoever held them

  • Significance: Their negotiability, together with the county vote and the recital of legal compliance on their face, is why a bona fide holder for value before maturity could enforce them despite the fraud

  • Distinction: Within the reading, the bonds (principal obligations) are distinguished from the coupons (interest obligations attached to them)

 

Coupon (interest coupon)

  • Definition: A detachable portion of a bond representing a periodic interest payment; here each was for $100, reflecting 10% annual interest on a $1,000 bond

  • Significance: Smith's suit was on earlier-maturing coupons, while Cromwell's suit was on different bonds and coupons, which made the second action a different demand; fraud in issuing the bonds "affected equally the coupons"

  • Distinction: Each coupon or bond is a separate demand for preclusion purposes, even though all came from the same bond issue

 

Writ of error

  • Definition: The procedural vehicle by which the earlier Smith case was brought to the U.S. Supreme Court for review

  • Significance: The Court read the Smith trial record together with its own opinion on the writ of error to determine what was actually adjudged, which is the key step in issue preclusion

  • Distinction: The reading does not define the writ further or state whether Cromwell's own case came up the same way

 

Counts (in a declaration)

  • Definition: Separate statements of the cause of action within a complaint; per the editor's note, a complaint may contain alternate or even inconsistent allegations in different counts

  • Significance: The Court used counts to show why a default judgment cannot make a complaint's allegations evidence in another proceeding, since the counts may contradict one another

  • Distinction: Allegations in counts are not findings; only matters actually litigated and determined carry issue-preclusive effect
