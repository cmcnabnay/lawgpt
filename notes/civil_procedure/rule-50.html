1. Rules

Rule: FRCP 50(a) — Judgment as a Matter of Law (Pre-Verdict)

  • Source: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, text of Rule 50(a)

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: If a party has been fully heard on an issue during a jury trial and the court finds that a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for that party on the issue, the court may resolve the issue against the party and grant judgment as a matter of law against the party on any claim or defense that, under the controlling law, can be maintained or defeated only with a favorable finding on that issue

  • Legal Question: Before the case goes to the jury, has the party with the burden on an issue produced legally sufficient evidence for a reasonable jury to find in its favor?

  • Requirements:

    1. The party against whom the motion is directed must have been "fully heard" on the issue during the jury trial

    2. The court must find that a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for that party on the issue

    3. The issue must be one that, under the controlling substantive law, is dispositive of a claim or defense (i.e., that claim/defense can be maintained or defeated only with a favorable finding on that issue)

  • Application: A party (typically the defendant, after the plaintiff rests, or either party at the close of all evidence) moves under Rule 50(a) asking the judge to take the issue away from the jury because the opposing party's evidence is so weak that no reasonable jury could find for them — the motion may be made at any time before the case is submitted to the jury, and it must specify the judgment sought and the law and facts entitling the movant to it

  • Exceptions: None stated in the text beyond the requirement that the motion be made before submission to the jury and that it specify the judgment sought with supporting law and facts

 

Rule: FRCP 50(b) — Renewed Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law After Trial (Formerly JNOV)

  • Source: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, text of Rule 50(b); textbook narrative explaining the historical JNOV terminology

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: If the court does not grant a Rule 50(a) motion, the court is deemed to have submitted the action to the jury subject to later deciding the legal questions raised by that motion, and the movant may, no later than 28 days after entry of judgment (or, if the motion addresses a jury issue not decided by a verdict, no later than 28 days after the jury was discharged), file a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law, which may include an alternative or joint request for a new trial under Rule 59

  • Legal Question: After the jury has already returned its verdict, should the judge nonetheless enter judgment as a matter of law because no reasonable jury could have found as this jury did — i.e., did the jury get it wrong (either by being unreasonable or by misunderstanding the law)?

  • Requirements:

    1. The movant must have first made a Rule 50(a) motion before the case was submitted to the jury that the court did not grant

    2. The renewed motion must be filed no later than 28 days after entry of judgment, or, if the motion addresses a jury issue not decided by a verdict, no later than 28 days after the jury was discharged

  • Application: In ruling on the renewed motion, the court may (1) allow judgment on the verdict if the jury returned one, (2) order a new trial, or (3) direct entry of judgment as a matter of law — this is the modern replacement for what used to be called a "judgment notwithstanding the verdict" (JNOV, from "judgment non obstante veredicto"); granting it means the judge disregards the jury's actual verdict on the ground that no reasonable jury could have found for the nonmoving party

  • Exceptions: A Rule 50(b) motion can only be made if the movant already made a Rule 50(a) motion before submission to the jury — the rule structures the post-verdict motion as a renewal of the earlier motion, not a freestanding new one

 

Rule: FRCP 50(c) — Granting the Renewed Motion; Conditional Ruling on a Motion for a New Trial

  • Source: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, text of Rule 50(c)

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: If the court grants a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law, it must also conditionally rule on any accompanying motion for a new trial by determining whether a new trial should be granted if the judgment is later vacated or reversed, and must state the grounds for that conditional ruling; conditionally granting the new-trial motion does not affect the judgment's finality, and if the judgment is reversed on appeal, the new trial must proceed unless the appellate court orders otherwise, while if the new-trial motion is conditionally denied, the appellee may assert that denial was error on appeal

  • Legal Question: If a court grants JMOL after the verdict, how should it simultaneously handle a linked motion for a new trial so the case is appeal-ready without a second round of trial-court proceedings?

  • Requirements:

    1. Applies only when the court grants a renewed Rule 50(b) motion

    2. The court must conditionally rule on any joined new-trial motion, stating grounds for granting or denying it

  • Application: This rule lets an appellate court reviewing a JMOL grant know immediately whether a new trial should follow if it reverses the JMOL, avoiding a remand purely to ask the trial court what it would have done about a new trial

  • Exceptions: The conditional ruling has no effect on the judgment's finality for appeal purposes — it only activates if the judgment is later vacated or reversed

 

Rule: FRCP 50(d) — Time for a Losing Party's New-Trial Motion

  • Source: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, text of Rule 50(d)

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: Any motion for a new trial under Rule 59 by a party against whom judgment as a matter of law is rendered must be filed no later than 28 days after entry of the judgment

  • Legal Question: How much time does the party who lost on JMOL have to separately move for a new trial?

  • Requirements: The motion must be filed no later than 28 days after entry of the judgment against that party

  • Application: Governs the party on the losing end of a JMOL ruling (as opposed to Rule 50(b), which governs the movant's own combined JMOL/new-trial request) and sets the deadline for that losing party's independent new-trial motion

  • Exceptions: None stated

 

Rule: FRCP 50(e) — Denying the Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law; Reversal on Appeal

  • Source: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, text of Rule 50(e)

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: If the trial court denies the motion for judgment as a matter of law, the prevailing party may, as appellee, assert grounds entitling it to a new trial should the appellate court conclude the trial court erred in denying the motion, and if the appellate court reverses the judgment, it may order a new trial, direct the trial court to determine whether a new trial should be granted, or direct the entry of judgment

  • Legal Question: What happens on appeal when a trial court denies JMOL and the appellate court disagrees with that denial?

  • Requirements: Applies where the trial court denied the JMOL motion and the losing movant appeals the denial

  • Application: Preserves the prevailing party's (appellee's) ability to argue for a new trial as a fallback position even though it won below, in case the appellate court reverses the denial of JMOL; gives the appellate court flexibility in structuring the remedy on reversal

  • Exceptions: None stated

 

Rule: JMOL vs. JNOV Terminology / Substantive Standard

  • Source: Textbook narrative preceding the Rule 50 text

  • Document: 627-28

  • Statement: A Rule 50(b) post-verdict motion for judgment as a matter of law (historically called JNOV) requires the judge to disregard a verdict the jury has already returned, on the ground that no reasonable jury could have found for the nonmoving party — not merely that the judge disagrees with the verdict

  • Legal Question: What is the substantive standard a judge applies when asked to override a jury's actual verdict, and how does that differ from simple disagreement with the outcome?

  • Requirements: The judge must conclude the jury was either unreasonable on the evidence or misunderstood the controlling law — mere disagreement with the verdict is not enough

  • Application: This framing explains why JMOL under 50(b) is treated as an exceptional remedy — courts invoke it only when satisfied that no reasonable jury could have reached the verdict rendered, reinforcing the deference ordinarily owed to jury fact-finding

  • Exceptions: None stated; this is a description of the standard's rigor rather than a carve-out

4. Definitions

Judgment as a Matter of Law (JMOL)

  • Definition: A ruling by the trial judge, available under Rule 50(a) before the case is submitted to the jury and under Rule 50(b) after the jury returns its verdict, that resolves an issue or claim against a party because a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for that party

  • Significance: It is the mechanism by which a judge can take a case (or an issue) away from the jury, either preemptively (50(a)) or by overriding an already-returned verdict (50(b))

  • Distinction: Under 50(a), the jury has not yet decided anything, so the judge is preventing the issue from ever reaching the jury; under 50(b), the jury has already decided, so the judge is disregarding that decision

 

Judgment Non Obstante Veredicto (JNOV)

  • Definition: The historical term (Latin for "judgment notwithstanding the verdict") for what is now called a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b)

  • Significance: Explains older case law and terminology students may encounter that predates the modern Rule 50(b) label; the substance is unchanged — the judge overrides the jury's verdict

  • Distinction: JNOV/Rule 50(b) is distinct from Rule 50(a) JMOL because it occurs after, not before, the jury has rendered a verdict

 

Renewed Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law

  • Definition: The Rule 50(b) motion filed after trial (within 28 days of entry of judgment or jury discharge) that "renews" an earlier Rule 50(a) motion the court did not grant, asking the judge to enter judgment as a matter of law notwithstanding the jury's verdict

  • Significance: A party cannot bring a Rule 50(b) motion without having first made a Rule 50(a) motion before submission to the jury — this preserves the issue and gives the non-movant fair notice and an opportunity to cure any evidentiary gap at trial

  • Distinction: Distinct from a Rule 59 motion for a new trial, though the two are often joined; JMOL asks for outright judgment in the movant's favor, while a new-trial motion asks only for another trial

 

Conditional Ruling (Rule 50(c))

  • Definition: A ruling the trial court must make on a joined new-trial motion whenever it grants a renewed Rule 50(b) motion, stating whether a new trial should be granted if the JMOL judgment is later vacated or reversed on appeal

  • Significance: Allows the appellate court, if it reverses the JMOL, to know immediately whether a new trial should proceed without remanding for the trial court to decide that question fresh

  • Distinction: The conditional ruling does not affect whether the underlying JMOL judgment is final and appealable — finality is preserved regardless of the conditional ruling's content
