1. Rules

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1)

  • Source: Statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1), as reprinted in the introductory note and quoted at the start of Hertz Corp. v. Friend

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: For purposes of § 1332 and § 1441, "a corporation shall be deemed to be a citizen of every State and foreign state by which it has been incorporated and of the State or foreign state where it has its principal place of business"

  • Legal Question: What state or states is a corporation a citizen of when deciding diversity jurisdiction?

  • Requirements:

    1. Identify every state and foreign state where the corporation has been incorporated, since each one makes it a citizen

    2. Identify the one state or foreign state where the corporation has its principal place of business, which also makes it a citizen

  • Application: Used whenever a corporation is a party and a court must decide whether the parties are "citizens of different States." Under this rule a corporation can be a citizen of more than one state at once, unlike a natural person. In Hertz, the plaintiffs were California citizens. If Hertz's principal place of business was in California, Hertz was also a California citizen and there was no diversity

  • Exceptions: The definition applies "for the purposes of this section and section 1441" only. The rule is about corporations; the reading explains that Ceglia and ConnectU dealt with natural persons, whose citizenship is decided differently. Wording difference to notice: the casebook's statutory excerpt says "every State and foreign state," while the Hertz opinion quotes "any State by which it has been incorporated and of the State where it has its principal place of business." The reading does not explain the difference

  • Distinction: There are two separate bases of corporate citizenship. Incorporation can produce several citizenships ("every State"). Principal place of business produces exactly one, because "place" is singular. Do not merge the two

 

Rule: Nerve center test

  • Source: Case holding, Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part V.A, adopting and expanding Judge Weinfeld's approach in Scot Typewriter Co. v. Underwood Corp. as applied in the Seventh Circuit

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: A corporation's "principal place of business" is "the place where a corporation's officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation's activities," meaning its "nerve center." In practice this "should normally be the place where the corporation maintains its headquarters — provided that the headquarters is the actual center of direction, control, and coordination … and not simply an office where the corporation holds its board meetings"

  • Legal Question: Where is a corporation's "principal place of business" under § 1332(c)(1)?

  • Requirements:

    1. Find the single place within a state where the corporation's high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities

    2. That place is presumptively the corporate headquarters

    3. The headquarters counts only if it is the actual center of direction, control, and coordination, not just a site for board meetings attended by directors and officers who travel there for the occasion

  • Application: Used to decide one half of corporate citizenship, usually when removal or diversity jurisdiction is contested. The test looks at a place within a state, not at the state's total business activity. In Hertz, the unchallenged declaration placed leadership and core executive functions in Park Ridge, New Jersey. That pointed to New Jersey even though California had the largest share of Hertz's rental locations, employees, revenue, and transactions

  • Exceptions: The Court admits "there will be hard cases" and "occasionally counterintuitive results," and accepts them as the price of a clearer rule. The test does not apply to a sham headquarters (see Manipulation limit on the nerve center test below)

  • Distinction: The Hertz test is broader than Scot Typewriter's original version. Scot Typewriter applied the nerve center idea only to corporations with "far-flung and varied activities." Hertz makes it the test for all corporations, including those operating in only a few states. It replaces the business activities test and the combined "total activities" or "center of gravity" approaches

 

Rule: Manipulation limit on the nerve center test

  • Source: Case holding, Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part V.B

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: If "the record reveals attempts at manipulation — for example, that the alleged 'nerve center' is nothing more than a mail drop box, a bare office with a computer, or the location of an annual executive retreat," courts "should instead take as the 'nerve center' the place of actual direction, control, and coordination, in the absence of such manipulation"

  • Legal Question: What does a court do when a corporation's claimed headquarters is not where it is actually directed and controlled?

  • Requirements:

    1. The record shows an attempt at manipulation, meaning the claimed nerve center lacks real direction, control, and coordination

    2. The court then identifies the place of actual direction, control, and coordination and treats that as the nerve center

  • Application: Used by a party opposing diversity jurisdiction to attack a corporation's claimed headquarters as a sham. It answers a gamesmanship concern that runs through the opinion, including the history of corporations manipulating jurisdiction through their choice of state of incorporation

  • Exceptions: The limit applies only when manipulation actually appears in the record. Otherwise the headquarters presumption holds

  • Distinction: This is a different problem from the board-meetings-only office described in Part V.A. That office simply fails to qualify as the headquarters. Manipulation is a deliberate attempt to create a false nerve center. Both lead to the same place: actual direction and control

 

Rule: Burden of persuasion for diversity jurisdiction

  • Source: Case holding and statement of settled principle, Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part V.B ("of course, remains on the party asserting it")

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: The party asserting diversity jurisdiction bears the burden of persuasion. "When challenged on allegations of jurisdictional facts, the parties must support their allegations by competent proof"

  • Legal Question: Who must prove the facts that support diversity jurisdiction, and how?

  • Requirements:

    1. The burden rests on the party invoking diversity jurisdiction, such as a removing defendant

    2. If the jurisdictional facts are challenged, that party must back its allegations with competent proof

  • Application: In Hertz, Hertz was the removing party and the one asserting diversity. It carried its burden with a declaration from an employee relations manager about where its leadership, executive functions, and business activity were located. The District Court treated that declaration as undisputed

  • Exceptions: The reading does not describe any exceptions. The Court remanded so that the respondents would "have a fair opportunity to litigate their case in light of" the new test. That suggests jurisdictional facts can still be contested on remand

  • Distinction: The burden of proving jurisdictional facts is separate from the court's own duty to check its jurisdiction (next rule). The burden decides who loses if the proof falls short. The court's duty applies even when no one raises the issue

 

Rule: Independent judicial obligation to determine subject-matter jurisdiction

  • Source: Case statement, Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part V.A, offered in support of administrative simplicity

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: "Courts have an independent obligation to determine whether subject-matter jurisdiction exists, even when no party challenges it"

  • Legal Question: Must a federal court confirm its own subject-matter jurisdiction even if no party objects?

  • Requirements: A federal court must satisfy itself that subject-matter jurisdiction exists whether or not any party raises the issue

  • Application: In Hertz this obligation is a reason to prefer a simple test. Because courts must check jurisdiction in every case, they "benefit from straightforward rules under which they can readily assure themselves of their power to hear a case"

  • Exceptions: The reading does not discuss any exceptions

  • Distinction: This duty belongs to the court. The burden of persuasion belongs to the party asserting jurisdiction

 

Rule: Article III, § 2 diversity clause

  • Source: U.S. Constitution, Art. III, § 2, as discussed in Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part III

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: The "judicial Power shall extend" to "Controversies … between Citizens of different States." This language "does not automatically confer diversity jurisdiction upon the federal courts." It authorizes Congress to confer that jurisdiction and to set its scope "within constitutional limits"

  • Legal Question: Does Article III by itself give federal courts diversity jurisdiction?

  • Requirements:

    1. There must be a constitutional grant, meaning a controversy between citizens of different states

    2. Congress must also have conferred the jurisdiction by statute, which it first did in the First Judiciary Act of 1789 and does today in § 1332

  • Application: Explains why the case turns on statutory interpretation of § 1332(c)(1) rather than on the Constitution. Congress defines the scope of diversity jurisdiction, so the Court looks to statutory text, history, and administrability

  • Exceptions: Congress's grant must stay "within constitutional limits." The reading does not define those limits further

  • Distinction: The constitutional ceiling and the statutory grant are two separate levels. Article III permits diversity jurisdiction, and § 1332 actually confers it

 

Rule: Historical state of incorporation rule

  • Source: Textbook narrative within Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Part III, describing Louisville, C. & C.R. Co. v. Letson (1844), an unnamed 1809 decision by Chief Justice Marshall, and an unnamed 1928 decision

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: Before 1958, a corporation was "deemed an artificial person of the State by which it had been created," and its citizenship for jurisdictional purposes was set by its state of incorporation. By 1928 this rule was "virtually absolute"

  • Legal Question: Before § 1332(c) was enacted, how was a corporation's citizenship determined for diversity purposes?

  • Requirements: The corporation was a citizen of the state that created it

  • Application: This is background explaining why Congress added principal place of business in 1958. A corporation "closely identified with State A" could get into federal court in State A just by incorporating in State B, "perhaps a State where the corporation did no business at all"

  • Exceptions: Under the earlier 1809 approach, a corporation was "certainly not a citizen." It could still invoke diversity by pleading that all its shareholders were citizens of a different state from the defendants. Letson modified that approach

  • Distinction: The 1958 statute kept ("codified") the place-of-incorporation test and added principal place of business as a second citizenship. It did not replace incorporation. The 1809 shareholder approach looked at the real people behind the corporation. Letson treated the corporation itself as the citizen

 

Rule: Business activities test

  • Source: Ninth Circuit precedent applied by the District Court, as described in Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Parts I and IV. The Supreme Court rejected it in Part V

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: Under the Ninth Circuit approach, a court first measured a corporation's business activity state by state. If activity was "significantly larger" or "substantially predominates" in one state, that state was the principal place of business. If no state stood out, the court used the "nerve center," meaning where "the majority of its executive and administrative functions are performed"

  • Legal Question: Before Hertz, how did the Ninth Circuit locate a corporation's principal place of business?

  • Requirements:

    1. Measure business activity in each state, looking at things like locations, employees, revenue, and transactions

    2. Ask whether activity in one state is significantly larger than in the next closest state, or substantially predominates

    3. If no state qualifies, fall back to the nerve center

  • Application: The District Court used this test to find Hertz a California citizen because California had the "plurality of each of the relevant business activities" and the gap to the next state was "significant." Know this test as the approach Hertz rejected

  • Exceptions: Hertz rejected the test for four main reasons:

    1. It wrongly looks at "the State itself" rather than "a place within a State"

    2. It produces "strange results," because nearly every national retailer would become a California citizen given California's population

    3. It is complex, which breeds litigation and gamesmanship

    4. It does not produce a uniform national rule

  • Distinction: Related approaches courts used were the "total activities" test, which combined nerve center and business activity factors, and the "center of gravity" test described in treatises. The Court described the resulting tests as ones that "tend to overlap" and are described in language that "is imprecise"

 

Rule: Administrative simplicity in jurisdictional statutes

  • Source: Case holding and interpretive principle, Hertz Corp. v. Friend, opening paragraph and Parts V.A–B

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: The Court places "primary weight upon the need for judicial administration of a jurisdictional statute to remain as simple as possible." "Administrative simplicity is a major virtue in a jurisdictional statute," and accepting "occasionally counterintuitive results is the price the legal system must pay to avoid overly complex jurisdictional administration"

  • Legal Question: What interpretive values should guide how courts read jurisdictional statutes?

  • Requirements: The Court gives these reasons for preferring simple jurisdictional rules:

    1. Complex tests spend time and money on which court decides a case rather than on the merits

    2. Complex tests produce appeals and reversals and encourage gamesmanship

    3. Courts must check jurisdiction themselves, so they need straightforward rules

    4. Simple rules are predictable for corporations and for plaintiffs choosing between state and federal court

    5. Simple rules produce a uniform national interpretation

  • Application: This is the main policy justification for the nerve center test. It is a useful argument on an exam whenever a jurisdictional rule is unclear

  • Exceptions: The Court concedes that "there may be no perfect test that satisfies all administrative and purposive criteria"

  • Distinction: The Court weighs this value against purpose-based reasoning about local prejudice and chooses simplicity. It explains that prejudice turns on factors courts "cannot easily measure," such as image, history, and advertising

 

Rule: Local prejudice rationale for diversity jurisdiction

  • Source: Textbook narrative within Hertz Corp. v. Friend, Parts III and IV

  • Document: 892-900

  • Statement: Diversity jurisdiction's "basic rationale" is "opening the federal courts' doors to those who might otherwise suffer from local prejudice against out-of-state parties"

  • Legal Question: Why does diversity jurisdiction exist?

  • Requirements: An out-of-state party faces possible prejudice in a local state court

  • Application: This rationale explains why Congress added principal place of business in 1958, to stop corporations from claiming out-of-state status through incorporation alone. In Hertz the Court declined to use the rationale to fine-tune the test, saying that effort "seems doomed to failure"

  • Exceptions: The Court says measurable factors, such as office or plant location, sales, and employment, "will sometimes bear no more than a distant relation to the likelihood of prejudice"

  • Distinction: This is a rationale for the doctrine, not a test courts apply. Hertz holds that it does not control how "principal place of business" is read

Not covered: The opinion cites 28 U.S.C. §§ 1332(d)(2) and 1441(a) as the basis for Hertz's removal but does not explain them. The assigned reading does not cover removal or the class action diversity provision. Notes question 3 asks about TheFacebook's citizenship using facts from ConnectU, which is not in these pages. The pages give only the corporate test you would apply: the state(s) of incorporation plus the nerve center.

2. Case Narrative

Hertz Corp. v. Friend

Melinda Friend and John Nhieu are two California citizens. They are the plaintiffs, and respondents in the Supreme Court. The Hertz Corporation is the defendant, and petitioner in the Supreme Court. It is a national car rental company. The reading does not say anything more about Friend and Nhieu personally, such as whether they worked for Hertz. It says only that they claimed Hertz violated California's wage and hour laws, and that they wanted to represent a potential class of California citizens who allegedly suffered similar harms.

Hertz is a large nationwide business. It operated facilities in 44 states. Of its 1,606 car rental locations, 273 were in California. About 2,300 of its 11,230 full-time employees worked there. About $811 million of its $4.371 billion in annual revenue came from California, along with about 3.8 million of its roughly 21 million annual rentals. California had about 12% of the nation's population at the time. Hertz's leadership, including the leadership of its domestic subsidiaries, was at its corporate headquarters in Park Ridge, New Jersey. Its "core executive and administrative functions" were carried out there and, "to a lesser extent," in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. Its "major administrative operations" were at those two locations.

The underlying wage and hour conduct is not dated or described further in the reading, and the opinion gives no specific workplace locations beyond California generally. The dispute came down to a disagreement over where Hertz is a citizen. The plaintiffs said Hertz was a California citizen like them because so much of its business was in California. Hertz said its principal place of business was in New Jersey. That disagreement decided whether the case could be heard in federal court.

In September 2007, Friend and Nhieu sued Hertz in a California state court, seeking damages for the alleged wage and hour violations on behalf of themselves and the proposed class.

3. Cases

Case Name: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

  • Document: 892-900

  • Course: Civil Procedure (Class 25, Diversity Jurisdiction)

  • Facts

    • In September 2007, California citizens Melinda Friend and John Nhieu sued Hertz in California state court for alleged violations of California wage and hour laws, on behalf of a proposed class of California citizens

    • Hertz removed the case to federal court, claiming diversity of citizenship under §§ 1332(d)(2) and 1441(a)

    • Plaintiffs argued that Hertz was a California citizen, which would mean no diversity

    • Hertz submitted a declaration from an employee relations manager. It stated that Hertz operated in 44 states, and that California accounted for 273 of 1,606 rental locations, about 2,300 of 11,230 full-time employees, about $811 million of $4.371 billion in annual revenue, and about 3.8 million of roughly 21 million annual rentals. California had about 12% of the national population

    • The declaration stated that Hertz's leadership was at its corporate headquarters in Park Ridge, New Jersey, and that its core executive and administrative functions were carried out there and, to a lesser extent, in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma

    • The District Court accepted Hertz's facts as undisputed. The only dispute was which legal test applied

  • Procedural Posture:

    • California state court: Friend and Nhieu filed a putative class action against Hertz for violations of California wage and hour laws; the opinion does not state any ruling by this court before removal

    • Removal: Hertz (defendant) removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California on the grounds that the plaintiffs and Hertz were citizens of different states, giving the federal court diversity jurisdiction under §§ 1332(d)(2) and 1441(a)

    • U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California: applying Ninth Circuit business activity precedent to Hertz's undisputed declaration, held that Hertz's principal place of business was California, that diversity jurisdiction was therefore lacking, and remanded the case to state court on the plaintiffs' challenge to Hertz's removal

    • Appeal: Hertz (defendant, removing party)-Appellant sought review of the remand order in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit on the grounds that its principal place of business was New Jersey, not California, so diversity jurisdiction existed

    • U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit: affirmed the remand order in a brief memorandum opinion

    • Petition for writ of certiorari: Hertz (defendant below)-Petitioner sought review in the U.S. Supreme Court on the grounds that the Ninth Circuit's business activity test wrongly located its principal place of business in California

    • U.S. Supreme Court: Hertz Corp. is the petitioner; Friend and Nhieu (plaintiffs below) are the respondents; certiorari granted in light of differences among the Circuits in applying the test for corporate citizenship

  • Issue: Whether a corporation's "principal place of business" under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1) is found by measuring its business activities state by state, or is instead the place where its high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities

  • Holding: The U.S. Supreme Court, unanimously and in an opinion by Justice Breyer, held that "principal place of business" means the corporation's "nerve center," the place where its high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities. This will normally be its headquarters, provided the headquarters is the actual center of direction, control, and coordination. Hertz's unchallenged declaration suggested its nerve center was in New Jersey, not California

  • Rule:

    • 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1): A corporation is a citizen of every state and foreign state where it is incorporated and of the state or foreign state where it has its principal place of business

    • Nerve center test: A corporation's principal place of business is the place where its officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities, normally its headquarters if that is the actual center of direction and control and not merely a site for board meetings

    • Manipulation limit on the nerve center test: If the claimed nerve center is a sham, such as a mail drop box, a bare office with a computer, or an annual retreat site, courts look to the place of actual direction, control, and coordination

    • Burden of persuasion for diversity jurisdiction: The party asserting diversity jurisdiction bears the burden of persuasion and must support challenged jurisdictional allegations with competent proof

    • Independent judicial obligation to determine subject-matter jurisdiction: Courts must decide whether subject-matter jurisdiction exists even when no party challenges it

    • Administrative simplicity in jurisdictional statutes: Jurisdictional statutes should be applied as simply as possible, even at the cost of occasionally counterintuitive results

  • Reasoning: The Supreme Court reasoned in several steps:

    • History: Congress added "principal place of business" in 1958, following the 1951 Judicial Conference committee's proposal, to stop the abuse of the "virtually absolute" state of incorporation rule. The phrase then "proved more difficult to apply than its originators likely expected"

    • Circuit confusion: Courts developed nerve center, business activity, total activity, and center of gravity tests that "tend to overlap" and are imprecise. The result was complexity and no national uniformity

    • The purpose-based approach fails: Trying to find the state where a corporation is least likely to face local prejudice "seems doomed to failure," because prejudice depends on factors such as image, history, and advertising that courts cannot easily measure, while measurable factors relate only distantly to prejudice

    • Text: "Place" is singular. "Principal" means the "main, prominent" or "leading" place (citing the Oxford English Dictionary). "State where" shows that the place is within a state, not the state itself. A nerve center fits that reading. The business activity test wrongly measures "the State itself"

    • Strange results: If California-level activity made Hertz a California citizen, "nearly every national retailer" would be a California citizen because of California's population. The Ninth Circuit itself has since recognized this problem

    • Administrative simplicity: Complex tests waste money, produce appeals and reversals, and encourage gamesmanship. Courts must check jurisdiction themselves and benefit from clear rules. Predictability helps corporations and plaintiffs alike

    • A single location: The "corporate brain" metaphor points to one place, while business activities are spread across plants, sales offices, and employees in many locations

    • Imperfection accepted: There will be hard cases, but a clearer rule is worth it. Manipulation is handled by looking to actual direction and control, and the burden stays on the party asserting jurisdiction

    • Application: Hertz's declaration suggested that its nerve center and headquarters were the same and were in New Jersey. Because the test was new, the respondents deserved a fair opportunity to litigate under it

  • Plaintiff's/Appellee's Arguments: Friend and Nhieu argued that Hertz was a California citizen like themselves, so diversity jurisdiction was lacking. They relied on the Ninth Circuit's business activity test and on Hertz's large share of locations, employees, revenue, and transactions in California

  • Defendant/Appellant's Arguments:

    • Hertz argued that the parties were citizens of different states, so federal diversity jurisdiction existed under §§ 1332(a)(1), (c)(1), and (d)(2)

    • Hertz argued that its principal place of business was New Jersey, where its leadership and corporate headquarters were and where its core executive and administrative functions were carried out

    • Hertz relied on its declaration showing operations in 44 states, with California's share of its business roughly tracking California's share of the national population

  • Disposition: The U.S. Supreme Court vacated the Ninth Circuit's judgment and remanded the case for further proceedings consistent with its opinion

  • Importance: Hertz is the controlling test for a corporation's principal place of business. You need it for any diversity question involving a corporate party. Key exam points:

    • A corporation has dual citizenship: every state of incorporation plus one nerve center

    • The nerve center is presumptively the headquarters, but not a board-meeting-only office or a sham such as a mail drop or bare office

    • The case is a leading example of the Court favoring clear, administrable jurisdictional rules over purpose-based balancing

    • Practice working the casebook notes questions: a corporation that is a citizen of only one state is incorporated where its nerve center is; one that is a citizen of two states has its nerve center in a different state from its single state of incorporation; one that is a citizen of three or more states is incorporated in several states. Because a corporation can be a citizen of more states, there are more chances that it shares citizenship with an opposing party

4. Definitions

Diversity jurisdiction

  • Definition: Federal court jurisdiction over controversies between citizens of different states. Article III, § 2 authorizes it, and Congress confers it by statute, first in the 1789 Judiciary Act and now in 28 U.S.C. § 1332

  • Significance: Whether Hertz could keep the case in federal court depended entirely on whether diversity existed between the California plaintiffs and Hertz

  • Distinction: The constitutional grant is not self-executing. Congress decides how much of it federal courts actually receive

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Principal place of business

  • Definition: Under § 1332(c)(1), the one state or foreign state whose "place" is the corporation's main, leading place of business. After Hertz, this is the nerve center

  • Significance: This is the statutory phrase the Court interpreted. It gives a corporation one citizenship in addition to its state(s) of incorporation

  • Distinction: There is exactly one principal place of business because "place" is singular. A corporation can have several states of incorporation

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Nerve center

  • Definition: The place where a corporation's high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities. It is normally the headquarters, if that is the actual center of control

  • Significance: This is the test Hertz adopted. Judge Weinfeld described it in Scot Typewriter as the place "from which it radiates out to its constituent parts"

  • Distinction: It is not a board-meeting-only office, a mail drop box, a bare office with a computer, or the site of an annual executive retreat. Under the Ninth Circuit's old approach the nerve center was only a fallback. Hertz made it the sole test

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Business activities test

  • Definition: An approach that locates the principal place of business by measuring a corporation's business activity, such as plants, sales, employees, revenue, and transactions, state by state, and picking the state where activity is significantly larger or substantially predominates

  • Significance: The District Court and the Ninth Circuit used this test to find Hertz a California citizen. The Supreme Court rejected it

  • Distinction: It measures a state's total activity, while the nerve center test identifies a place within a state

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Total activities test

  • Definition: A test some courts used that combined nerve center and business activity factors to look at a corporation's "total activities"

  • Significance: The Court cites it as an example of the growing complexity it set out to eliminate

  • Distinction: It is a hybrid of the other tests, not a separate theory, and is closely related to the "center of gravity" idea

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Center of gravity

  • Definition: Treatise term for the goal of combined tests that try to find a corporation's overall center of operations

  • Significance: It shows how imprecise principal place of business analysis had become before Hertz

  • Distinction: It is a descriptive concept, not a defined test. Hertz replaced it with the more concrete nerve center

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

State of incorporation

  • Definition: The state that created the corporation, where it "has been incorporated" or filed its incorporation papers

  • Significance: It is one basis of corporate citizenship under § 1332(c)(1). Before 1958 it was the only basis and was "virtually absolute," which allowed jurisdictional manipulation

  • Distinction: A corporation can have more than one, while it has only one principal place of business

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Corporate citizenship

  • Definition: The status § 1332(c)(1) assigns to a corporation, which is a citizen of every state of incorporation and of its principal place of business

  • Significance: Because corporations can be citizens of several states at once, it is easier for a corporation to share citizenship with an opponent, which can defeat diversity

  • Distinction: Natural persons, discussed in Ceglia and ConnectU outside these pages, are citizens of only one state at a time. In 1809 the Court called a corporation "certainly not a citizen" and looked to its shareholders

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Burden of persuasion

  • Definition: The obligation to convince the court that a required fact or condition exists. For diversity jurisdiction it rests on the party asserting jurisdiction

  • Significance: Hertz, the removing party, had to prove diversity. If its jurisdictional facts were challenged, it had to support them with competent proof

  • Distinction: It is different from the court's independent obligation to confirm subject-matter jurisdiction even when no party challenges it

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Subject-matter jurisdiction

  • Definition: A court's power to hear a particular kind of case. Diversity jurisdiction is one category

  • Significance: Courts must check it on their own, which is why the Court valued a simple, administrable test

  • Distinction: The reading does not compare it to other types of jurisdiction

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Remand

  • Definition: Sending a case back to another court. In this reading the term is used in two ways: the District Court sent the removed case back to state court, and the Supreme Court sent the case back to the lower courts for further proceedings

  • Significance: The District Court's remand order was what Hertz appealed. The Supreme Court's own disposition was to vacate and remand

  • Distinction: A remand to state court ends federal jurisdiction over the case. A remand by an appellate court sends the case to a lower court within the same system for further proceedings

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.

 

Local prejudice

  • Definition: Bias that a local state court might have against an out-of-state party. Protecting against it is the "basic rationale" of diversity jurisdiction

  • Significance: It explains the 1958 amendment. Hertz declined to use it to shape the test because it depends on factors courts cannot easily measure

  • Distinction: It is a reason for the doctrine, not part of the legal test courts apply

  • Case: Friend v. Hertz Corp.
