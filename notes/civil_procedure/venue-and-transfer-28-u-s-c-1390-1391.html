1. Rules

  •  § 1390(a): "Venue" means which federal court location is the right place for a case that federal courts can already hear. It is not about whether a federal court has power to hear that type of case at all

  • § 1390(c): The venue rules don't decide which federal court a state case gets removed to, but they do govern moving that case between federal districts afterward

  • § 1391(a): Unless some other law says otherwise, 1391 is the default rule for where any civil case can be filed in federal court

  • § 1391(b)(1): You can sue in any district where a defendant lives, as long as the defendants live in the same state 

  • § 1391(b)(2): You can sue in any district where a substantial part of what happened took place, or where a substantial part of the disputed property is

  • § 1391(b)(3): Only if no district works under (b)(1) or (b)(2), you can sue in any district where at least one defendant is subjects to personal jurisdiction

  • § 1391(c)(1):  A person, who resides in the district where they are domiciled

  • § 1391(c)(2): A business or other organization being sued resides in every district where it's subject to personal jurisdiction 

  • § 1391(c)(3): A defendant who doesn't live in the US can be sued in any district and you ignore that defendant when working out venue for the others

  • § 1391(d): In a state with more than one district, a corporation resides in each district where its contacts would support personal jurisdiction if that district were its own state. If there is no such district, it resides in the district where its contacts are strongest

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1390(a)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1390(a), reproduced at p. 1168. The textbook narrative at p. 1167 supports it: "Within a given court system, and even among those courts with jurisdiction over the case and the parties, a venue statute may require that a case be filed in a certain judicial district"

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: As used in the venue chapter, "venue" refers to "the geographic specification of the proper court or courts for the litigation of a civil action that is within the subject-matter jurisdiction of the district courts in general." It does not refer to any grant or restriction of subject-matter jurisdiction that requires a civil action to be heard only by the district court for a particular district or districts

  • Legal Question: What does "venue" mean under the federal venue chapter, and how is it different from subject-matter jurisdiction?

  • Requirements:

    1. There is a civil action

    2. The action is within the subject-matter jurisdiction "of the district courts in general"

    3. The question is which geographic court or courts are the proper place to litigate it

  • Application: This is the framing rule. Venue matters only once a federal court has power over the case, and the narrative says venue applies "even among those courts with jurisdiction over the case and the parties." On an exam, check subject-matter jurisdiction and personal jurisdiction first, then ask whether the chosen district is a proper venue

  • Exceptions: A statute that gives or limits subject-matter jurisdiction so that only a particular district may hear the case is not a venue provision. It is jurisdictional, so § 1390(a) excludes it from the meaning of "venue." The excerpt omits § 1390(b) (marked "* * *"), and the attached material does not cover it

  • Distinction: Venue is different from subject-matter jurisdiction, which is whether federal district courts in general can hear the case. It is also different from personal jurisdiction, which is power over the parties. The narrative calls venue "akin to personal jurisdiction" because both "account for inconvenience to the parties and the connection between the dispute and the forum court." Still, venue is a separate requirement layered on top of jurisdiction

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1390(c)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1390(c), reproduced at p. 1168

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: The venue chapter "shall not determine the district court to which a civil action pending in a State court may be removed," but it "shall govern the transfer of an action so removed as between districts and divisions of the United States district courts"

  • Legal Question: Does the federal venue chapter control where a state-court case may be removed, and does it govern a removed case after it reaches federal court?

  • Requirements:

    1. A civil action is pending in a state court

    2. The action is removed to a United States district court

    3. Choosing the removal destination is outside the venue chapter

    4. Transfer of the removed action between districts and divisions is governed by the venue chapter

  • Application: When a case comes into federal court by removal rather than by original filing, the general venue rules in § 1391 do not decide which district receives it. Once the case is in federal court, any move between districts or divisions falls under the chapter's transfer provisions

  • Exceptions: This is itself a carve-out from the venue chapter. The attached material does not say what does determine the district to which a state case may be removed

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(a)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(a), reproduced at p. 1168

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: "Except as otherwise provided by law," § 1391 "shall govern the venue of all civil actions brought in district courts of the United States"

  • Legal Question: What is the default venue statute for civil actions brought in federal district court?

  • Requirements:

    1. A civil action

    2. Brought in a United States district court

    3. No other law provides otherwise

  • Application: Section 1391 is the starting point for every federal venue analysis of an originally filed civil action

  • Exceptions: "Except as otherwise provided by law," so another statute can displace § 1391. Part of the text after "law" is omitted in the excerpt ("* * *"), and the attached material does not cover it. Under § 1390(c), the chapter does not decide the district to which a state-court action may be removed

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(1)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(1), reproduced at p. 1168

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: A civil action may be brought in "a judicial district in which any defendant resides, if all defendants are residents of the State in which the district is located"

  • Legal Question: When is venue proper based on where the defendants reside?

  • Requirements:

    1. The district is one in which at least one ("any") defendant resides

    2. All defendants reside in the same State, namely the State where that district is located

    3. Residence is determined under § 1391(c) and, for corporations in multi-district States, § 1391(d)

  • Application: Use this to find residence-based venue. First fix each defendant's residence under subsections (c) and (d). Then check that every defendant resides in the same State. If so, any district in that State where at least one defendant resides is proper. The defendants may live in different districts of that State

  • Exceptions: This basis fails when the defendants reside in different States. Under § 1391(c)(3), a defendant not resident in the United States is disregarded when deciding where the action may be brought against the other defendants, so that defendant does not defeat the "all defendants" requirement

  • Distinction: The text keys only on where defendants reside. The plaintiff's residence is not a basis for venue under (b)(1). (b)(2) keys on events or property instead of residence

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(2)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(2), reproduced at p. 1168

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: A civil action may be brought in "a judicial district in which a substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred, or a substantial part of property that is the subject of the action is situated"

  • Legal Question: When is venue proper based on where the claim arose or where the disputed property is?

  • Requirements: Either of these alternatives:

    1. A substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred in the district, or

    2. A substantial part of the property that is the subject of the action is located in the district

  • Application: This lets the plaintiff sue where the dispute happened, whatever the defendants' residence. Map each event or omission underlying the claim to a district and ask whether what happened there is "substantial." Because the statute says "a substantial part" and not "the" most substantial part, the text allows more than one district to qualify. "Omissions" also counts, so a failure to act can ground venue where it occurred

  • Exceptions: Events or property in a district that are not "substantial" do not support venue there. The events must be ones "giving rise to the claim." The attached material does not define "substantial"

  • Distinction: This basis is transactional and tied to the claim, while (b)(1) is tied to the parties. It mirrors the narrative's point that venue accounts for "the connection between the dispute and the forum court"

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(3)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(3), reproduced at p. 1169

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: "If there is no district in which an action may otherwise be brought as provided in this section," the action may be brought in "any judicial district in which any defendant is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction with respect to such action"

  • Legal Question: Where can an action be brought when no district qualifies under the other venue provisions?

  • Requirements:

    1. No district anywhere qualifies under § 1391(b)(1) or (b)(2) ("no district in which an action may otherwise be brought as provided in this section")

    2. At least one defendant ("any defendant") is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction in the chosen district

    3. That personal jurisdiction exists "with respect to such action"

  • Application: This is a fallback provision. Reach it only after confirming that (b)(1) fails, for example because the defendants reside in different States, and that (b)(2) fails because no district has a substantial part of the events, omissions, or property. Then venue lies wherever any one defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction for this action

  • Exceptions: It is unavailable whenever any district satisfies (b)(1) or (b)(2), even if that district is less convenient for the plaintiff

  • Distinction: (b)(3) uses personal jurisdiction as the venue test, which shows how closely venue and personal jurisdiction are linked. It also applies only "if there is no district" otherwise available, so it is not an independent alternative like (b)(1) and (b)(2)

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(1)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(1), reproduced at p. 1169

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: "For all venue purposes," a natural person, "including an alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence in the United States, shall be deemed to reside in the judicial district in which that person is domiciled"

  • Legal Question: Where does an individual "reside" for venue purposes?

  • Requirements:

    1. The party is a natural person, including a lawful permanent resident alien

    2. Residence equals the judicial district of domicile

  • Application: Use this to determine an individual defendant's residence when applying (b)(1). It applies "for all venue purposes"

  • Exceptions: A natural person who is a defendant "not resident in the United States" falls under § 1391(c)(3), not (c)(1). The attached material does not define "domiciled"

  • Distinction: An individual's residence is fixed in one place, the district of domicile. An entity defendant's residence under (c)(2) can be many districts, wherever it is subject to personal jurisdiction

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(2)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(2), reproduced at p. 1169

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: "An entity with the capacity to sue and be sued in its common name under applicable law, whether or not incorporated, shall be deemed to reside, if a defendant, in any judicial district in which such defendant is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction with respect to the civil action in question"

  • Legal Question: Where does a defendant entity, incorporated or unincorporated, "reside" for venue purposes?

  • Requirements:

    1. The party is an entity with capacity to sue and be sued in its common name under applicable law, whether or not incorporated

    2. The entity is a defendant

    3. It resides in any judicial district where it is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction "with respect to the civil action in question"

  • Application: For an entity defendant, the residence question turns into a personal jurisdiction question for this particular action. That residence then feeds into (b)(1)

  • Exceptions: It applies only "if a defendant," so the text does not set the residence of an entity plaintiff. Part of the subsection is omitted in the excerpt ("* * *"), and the attached material does not cover it. For corporations in States with more than one district, § 1391(d) refines the analysis down to the district level

  • Distinction: Personal jurisdiction is measured "with respect to the civil action in question," so an entity's venue residence can vary from case to case. That is unlike an individual's fixed domicile under (c)(1)

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(3)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(3), reproduced at p. 1169

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: "A defendant not resident in the United States may be sued in any judicial district, and the joinder of such a defendant shall be disregarded in determining where the action may be brought with respect to other defendants"

  • Legal Question: How does venue work for a defendant who does not reside in the United States, and does joining such a defendant affect venue for the others?

  • Requirements:

    1. The defendant is not resident in the United States

    2. Venue as to that defendant is proper in any judicial district

    3. That defendant's joinder is ignored when determining venue as to the other defendants

  • Application: In a multi-defendant case, take the non-U.S.-resident defendant out of the analysis and apply (b)(1) through (b)(3) to the remaining defendants. A foreign-resident defendant therefore cannot defeat the "all defendants are residents of the State" requirement of (b)(1)

  • Exceptions: It covers only defendants "not resident in the United States." A lawful permanent resident is treated as a natural person domiciled in a district under (c)(1)

 

Rule: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(d)

  • Source: Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391(d), reproduced at p. 1169

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: In a State with more than one judicial district, where a corporate defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction when the action is commenced, the corporation "shall be deemed to reside in any district in that State within which its contacts would be sufficient to subject it to personal jurisdiction if that district were a separate State." If there is no such district, the corporation is deemed to reside "in the district within which it has the most significant contacts"

  • Legal Question: In which district or districts of a multi-district State does a corporate defendant reside for venue purposes?

  • Requirements:

    1. The State has more than one judicial district

    2. The defendant is a corporation

    3. The corporation is subject to personal jurisdiction in that State "at the time an action is commenced"

    4. Treat each district as if it were a separate State and ask whether the corporation's contacts with that district alone would support personal jurisdiction. If so, it resides there

    5. If no district qualifies on that basis, it resides in the district where it has "the most significant contacts"

  • Application: Use this when a corporation is subject to personal jurisdiction statewide but you need to know which districts within the State count as its residence. Run a district-by-district personal jurisdiction analysis, then fall back to the "most significant contacts" district if needed

  • Exceptions: It applies only to corporations, only in States with more than one district, and only where the corporation is subject to personal jurisdiction in the State when the action begins. It does not apply in single-district States

  • Distinction: (c)(2) gives the general entity residence rule tied to personal jurisdiction. (d) narrows that rule to the district level for corporations in multi-district States

 

Rule: Federal transfer power

  • Source: Textbook narrative, p. 1167

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: Transfer "refers to the power of a court to send a case to another court within the same court system." In the federal system it is governed by "the federal transfer statute"

  • Legal Question: Can a federal court move a case to another federal court rather than dismissing it?

  • Requirements:

    1. The case is pending in a court

    2. The receiving court is "within the same court system"

  • Application: A court can use transfer to send a case to a better or proper place within the federal system without ending the litigation. Section 1390(c) confirms that removed cases may also be transferred "as between districts and divisions"

  • Exceptions: Transfer is limited to courts "within the same court system," so it cannot send a case to a different system. The text of the federal transfer statute is not reproduced in pages 1167–69, and the attached material does not set out its standards

  • Distinction: Transfer moves the case. Forum non conveniens dismisses it so it can be refiled elsewhere

 

Rule: Forum non conveniens

  • Source: Textbook narrative, p. 1167

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: Under this "ancient common law doctrine," which "the federal courts still use today," "a federal court may dismiss an action because there is another forum that is better suited to hear the case"

  • Legal Question: When may a federal court that has jurisdiction decline to hear a case in favor of another forum?

  • Requirements:

    1. The action is in a federal court

    2. Another forum exists that is "better suited to hear the case"

  • Application: The remedy is dismissal, after which the plaintiff refiles in the other forum. The narrative describes this as "dismissal and refiling due to forum non conveniens." It applies even among courts that have jurisdiction

  • Exceptions: The dismissal is discretionary ("may"). The attached pages do not give the factors or standards for deciding whether another forum is "better suited"

  • Distinction: It is a common law doctrine, while venue and transfer are statutory. Its remedy is dismissal, while transfer moves the case within the same system

 

Rule: Choice of law after a change of forum

  • Source: Textbook narrative, p. 1167. It is posed as an open question that sets up the next chapter's Erie problem

  • Document: 1167-69

  • Statement: When a case moves from one court to another, by transfer or by forum non conveniens dismissal and refiling, a choice-of-law problem arises. Does the court where the case was first filed decide which law governs, or does the court that ends up with the case decide? The reading poses this question but does not answer it

  • Legal Question: Whose choice-of-law approach governs after a case changes forum: the original court's or the receiving court's?

  • Requirements:

    1. The case moves from one court to another by transfer, or by dismissal and refiling under forum non conveniens

    2. The two courts would choose different governing law

  • Application: The issue matters only when the courts disagree. As the narrative notes, "Sometimes, of course, both courts would look at the case and choose the same state's (or country's) law"

  • Exceptions: There is no real conflict if both courts would pick the same law. The attached material does not supply the resolution, which it defers to the next chapter

4. Definitions

Venue

  • Definition: Per the narrative, "the place where a lawsuit may be heard." Per § 1390(a), "the geographic specification of the proper court or courts for the litigation of a civil action that is within the subject-matter jurisdiction of the district courts in general"

  • Significance: This is the central concept of the reading. It is a further limit on where a case may be filed, applying even among courts that have jurisdiction

  • Distinction: It does not include grants or restrictions of subject-matter jurisdiction that confine a case to a particular district. It is "akin to" personal jurisdiction but a separate requirement

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Transfer

  • Definition: "The power of a court to send a case to another court within the same court system"

  • Significance: It is the chapter's second main topic, governed in federal court by the federal transfer statute, whose text is not in these pages. Section 1390(c) applies the venue chapter to transfers of removed cases "as between districts and divisions"

  • Distinction: Transfer moves the case within one court system. Forum non conveniens dismisses it so it can be refiled in a better-suited forum

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Forum non conveniens

  • Definition: An "ancient common law doctrine," roughly translated "the forum is not convenient," under which "a federal court may dismiss an action because there is another forum that is better suited to hear the case"

  • Significance: It is the third tool, alongside venue and transfer, for deciding the proper place for a case. Federal courts still use it today

  • Distinction: It comes from common law rather than statute, and its remedy is dismissal rather than transfer

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Judicial district

  • Definition: The geographic unit of the federal district court system in which venue is laid. Under § 1391(d), a State may contain one district or more than one

  • Significance: Every venue basis in § 1391(b) and every residence rule in § 1391(c)–(d) is stated by district

  • Distinction: A district is different from a State. Venue under (b)(1) requires all defendants to reside in the same State but allows venue in any district of that State where any defendant resides. Section 1390(c) also mentions "divisions" of district courts, which the reading does not define

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Residence

  • Definition: A party's deemed home for venue purposes, set by § 1391(c). For a natural person, it is the district of domicile. For an entity defendant, it is any district where the entity is subject to personal jurisdiction for the action. For a corporation in a multi-district State, § 1391(d) determines it

  • Significance: It drives § 1391(b)(1). Residence must be determined for each defendant before residential venue can be assessed

  • Distinction: "Residence" for venue is a statutory term of art. For entities, it depends on personal jurisdiction rather than physical location

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Domicile

  • Definition: The reading uses it as the test for a natural person's venue residence under § 1391(c)(1) but does not define it; the attached material does not cover its meaning

  • Significance: An individual defendant resides in the judicial district where that person is domiciled

  • Distinction: It applies to natural persons, including lawful permanent residents. Entities instead use the personal jurisdiction test in § 1391(c)(2)

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Alien lawfully admitted for permanent residence

  • Definition: A noncitizen admitted for permanent residence in the United States. Under § 1391(c)(1), such a person is treated like any other natural person and resides in the district of domicile

  • Significance: It places lawful permanent residents under the domicile rule rather than the non-U.S.-resident rule

  • Distinction: Contrast a "defendant not resident in the United States" under § 1391(c)(3), who may be sued in any district and whose joinder is disregarded

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Entity with the capacity to sue and be sued in its common name

  • Definition: Under § 1391(c)(2), an organization, "whether or not incorporated," that applicable law allows to sue and be sued under its own name

  • Significance: When such an entity is a defendant, it resides wherever it is subject to personal jurisdiction for the action in question

  • Distinction: The term covers unincorporated as well as incorporated entities. Section 1391(d) applies only to corporations

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Personal jurisdiction

  • Definition: As used in this reading, a court's power over a defendant "with respect to such action". The pages assume the concept from earlier chapters rather than defining it

  • Significance: It is built into venue in three places: the entity residence test in § 1391(c)(2), the district-by-district corporate test in § 1391(d), and the fallback in § 1391(b)(3). The narrative also calls venue "akin to personal jurisdiction"

  • Distinction: Having personal jurisdiction does not by itself make venue proper. Venue is a separate requirement that applies "even among those courts with jurisdiction over the case and the parties"

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Subject-matter jurisdiction

  • Definition: As used in § 1390(a), the authority of the district courts "in general" to hear a type of civil action

  • Significance: Venue presupposes subject-matter jurisdiction. It decides which proper court hears an action that federal courts can already hear

  • Distinction: A statute that confines a case to a particular district by granting or restricting subject-matter jurisdiction is not a venue rule under § 1390(a)

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Removal

  • Definition: Moving a civil action pending in a State court into a United States district court. The reading refers to it only in § 1390(c)

  • Significance: The venue chapter does not determine the district to which a state case may be removed. It does govern later transfers of the removed case between districts and divisions

  • Distinction: Removal moves a case from a state court system to the federal system. Transfer moves a case within the same court system

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Choice of law

  • Definition: Deciding which jurisdiction's law, whether a state's or a country's, governs the plaintiff's claims

  • Significance: The narrative flags that moving a case by transfer or forum non conveniens raises the question of whether the first court or the receiving court decides governing law. This foreshadows the next chapter

  • Distinction: It matters only when the two courts would choose different law. If both "choose the same state's (or country's) law," there is no conflict

  • Case: None (general terminology)

 

Erie problem

  • Definition: The reading names it only as "our next topic" and links it to choice-of-law issues that arise when a case changes forum. The attached material does not define it further

  • Significance: It connects venue, transfer, and forum non conveniens to the course's next unit on increasingly complex choice-of-law issues

  • Distinction: The attached pages do not distinguish it from ordinary choice of law beyond saying the coming issues are "increasingly complex"

  • Case: None (general terminology)
