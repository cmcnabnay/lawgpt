**Essay technique the professor rewards** (from the model answers):
- **Start with the substantive law.** On summary judgment, first lay out the elements of the claim, then ask what a *reasonable jury* could find on each one.
- **IRAC with subheadings** (one per defendant or prong) and short paragraphs.
- **Analyze every prong even if one is dispositive.** Argue both sides and reach a conclusion. A well-argued "wrong" conclusion can still score full points.
- Use the facts and say why they matter. Flag missing facts and state your assumptions.
- On the collateral order doctrine, judge the **category** of order, not the specific order (Mohawk). Mention the alternatives to collateral appeal (§ 1292(b), mandamus, contempt).

# UNIT I — Introduction & Due Process (Classes 1–4)

## Class 1 (8/25) — What Is Civil Procedure?

### Document: *Anonymous Physician v. Wininger* (Canvas)
**Case: Anonymous Physician v. Wininger** (Ind. Ct. App. 2013)
- Medical malpractice. Indiana's two-year **occurrence-based** statute of limitations. The patient knew by October 2007 that something was wrong (toe angle, pain, wanted a second opinion) but did not file until 2011. The appeals court **reversed the denial of summary judgment**, so the claim is time-barred.
- **Procedure point:** Statute of limitations is an **affirmative defense**. Once the defendant shows the claim was filed outside the period, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show tolling or timeliness. Compare PM2 Q6: SJ for the defendant on limitations when the plaintiff offers no contrary evidence.
- The trigger is awareness of **symptoms** that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate. Actual knowledge of malpractice isn't required.
- It shows a live procedural posture: denial of SJ → **interlocutory appeal**. Connect this to Unit VI, since denials of SJ are normally *not* final.

### Document: Hubbard Introduction (CB 3–6, 45–54)
**Rule: Due Process** (U.S. Const. Amends. V and XIV)
- Process matters independent of outcome. In the "coin flip" hypothetical, the winner still got no due process.
- Civil procedure is the machinery that enforces substantive rights. Keep the distinction between *procedural* and *substantive* law in mind all semester.

## Class 2 (8/26) — Preliminary Relief; Notice

### Document: *Winter v. NRDC* (CB 29–44)
**Rule: Preliminary injunction (four-factor test).** The plaintiff must show **(1)** likely success on the merits, **(2)** **likely** irreparable harm without relief, **(3)** the balance of equities tips in their favor, and **(4)** an injunction is in the public interest.

**Case: Winter v. NRDC** (U.S. 2008)
- The Navy's sonar training allegedly harmed marine mammals (NEPA claim). The Ninth Circuit used a "**possibility**" of irreparable harm standard. The Supreme Court held that is **too lenient**: harm must be **likely**.
- A preliminary injunction is an "**extraordinary remedy**" that is never awarded as of right. All four factors must be shown.
- The balance of equities and the public interest (**national security / military readiness**) were dispositive. The Court vacated without reaching the merits.
- Exam use: courts **defer to the military on national security**. The PM1 essay model answer used Winter to show a Navy motion to seal raised an "important" issue.

### Document: Rule 65 (CB 104–05)
**Rule 65(a): Preliminary injunction.** Requires **notice** to the adverse party. The court may consolidate the hearing with the trial on the merits.

**Rule 65(b): TRO without notice (ex parte)**, only if:
- (A) specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show **immediate and irreparable injury** before the adverse party can be heard, **and**
- (B) the movant's attorney **certifies in writing** the efforts made to give notice and why notice shouldn't be required.

**TRO limits:** It expires in **≤ 14 days** unless extended for good cause. The preliminary injunction hearing is set "at the earliest possible time." The adverse party can move to dissolve on **2 days' notice**. Rule 65(c) requires **security** (a bond).
- **TRO vs. PI:** A TRO is short-term and can be ex parte. A PI requires notice and a hearing. This is the federal-rules echo of Fuentes/Doehr: deprivations without prior hearing are tightly limited.

### Document: U.S. Const. Amend. V; Amend. XIV, § 1 (CB 79)
**Rule: Due Process.** No person shall be deprived of **life, liberty, or property** without due process of law.
- The **Fifth Amendment** binds the **federal** government. The **Fourteenth Amendment** binds the **states**. The PM2 essay was in *state* court, so cite the **Fourteenth** Amendment.
- Requires (1) **state action**, (2) a **deprivation** of life, liberty, or property, (3) **without** due process. Process means **notice** plus an **opportunity to be heard**.

### Document: *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank* (CB 119–28)
**Rule: The Mullane standard.** Notice must be "**reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections**." It must also give enough **time** to appear.

**Case: Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust** (U.S. 1950)
- A New York common trust fund gave notice to beneficiaries **by newspaper publication only**.
- **Known beneficiaries with known addresses:** publication is **insufficient**. Use at least **ordinary mail**.
- **Unknown or hard-to-find beneficiaries:** publication is acceptable when nothing better is feasible.
- "**Process which is a mere gesture is not due process.**" Ask whether this is a means one "**desirous of actually informing**" the absent party would reasonably adopt.
- **Actual notice is NOT required.** The test is **reasonableness**. Compare feasible alternatives and supplements, and weigh cost against the interests at stake.
- Personal service is the gold standard, but it isn't required. Mail is generally acceptable.

## Class 3 (8/27) — Notice

### Document: *Greene v. Lindsey* (CB 130–35)
**Case: Greene v. Lindsey** (U.S. 1982)
- Kentucky eviction notices were **posted on apartment doors** in public housing, where notices were **frequently torn down**. The tenants never saw them.
- **Holding:** Posting alone was **not** reasonably calculated to give notice *under these circumstances*. **Mail** was a cheap, efficient **supplement**.
- Lesson: a method that is generally acceptable (posting) can fail when the sender **knows or should know** it's unreliable in context. Courts ask whether a better, **feasible**, cost-effective alternative existed.

### Document: *Jones v. Flowers* (CB 136–45)
**Rule: Additional reasonable steps.** When the government **learns its notice attempt failed** (certified mail returned "unclaimed") and **before** taking property, it must take **additional reasonable steps** to notify the owner **if practicable**.

**Case: Jones v. Flowers** (U.S. 2006)
- A tax sale of a house. Certified mail came back **unclaimed**. The state then published notice and sold the house.
- **Holding:** Not enough. Once the state knew notice failed, it should have tried, for example, resending by **regular mail** (no signature needed), **posting** on the door, or addressing mail to "**occupant**."
- No duty to search phone books or other records (no "exhaustive search").
- **This was the core of the PM2 essay:** "If the party providing notice reasonably should have known that it failed, it is unreasonable not to try again, if there is another feasible way." Bella's certified mail came back undeliverable. One regular letter to an internet-found address was not enough. The lawyer should have also used the old address, posted at both houses, and tried personal service.
- Signals that notice may have failed: returned mail, and **no response to a letter asking for acknowledgment**. Weigh both sides of that second one, since silence might just mean the person doesn't want to settle.

### Document: Rule 4 (CB 154–55)
**Rule 4(a): Summons contents.** Names the court and parties, is directed to the defendant, gives the plaintiff's attorney's name and address, states the time to appear, warns of **default judgment**, and bears the clerk's **signature** and the court's **seal**.

**Rule 4(c): Service.** The plaintiff is responsible for serving the summons **with the complaint**. Any person **at least 18 and not a party** may serve (a 27-year-old second cousin is fine).

**Rule 4(e): Serving an individual** in the U.S.:
- (1) follow **state law** of the forum state or the state where service is made, **or**
- (2)(A) deliver **personally**; (B) leave at the individual's **dwelling or usual place of abode** with someone of **suitable age and discretion who resides there**; or (C) deliver to an **authorized agent**.
- **PM2 Q3:** Leaving the papers with a **5-year-old** is not "suitable age and discretion," so a **12(b)(5)** motion should be granted. State law (personal delivery only) is just *one* option under 4(e)(1). It does not override 4(e)(2).

**Rule 4(m): Time limit.** **90 days** after filing the complaint, or the court dismisses without prejudice or orders service. The court must extend the time for **good cause**.

**Rule 4(d): Waiver of service.** The defendant has a duty to avoid unnecessary service costs. Without good cause for refusing, the defendant pays service costs. A defendant who waives gets **60 days** to answer.
- **12(b)(4)** is insufficient **process** (the summons itself is defective). **12(b)(5)** is insufficient **service** of process (delivered the wrong way).

## Class 4 (9/1) — Opportunity to Be Heard

### Document: *Fuentes v. Shevin* (CB 82–95)
**Rule:** Due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard **before** the state deprives someone of a significant property interest, at a "meaningful time and in a meaningful manner." The exception is "**extraordinary situations**."

**Case: Fuentes v. Shevin** (U.S. 1972)
- Florida and Pennsylvania **prejudgment replevin** statutes let creditors get a writ (by posting a bond, with no hearing) and have the sheriff seize goods bought on installment (a stove and stereo).
- **Holding:** Unconstitutional. A **temporary** deprivation is still a deprivation. Post-seizure hearings don't cure it. A buyer who hasn't finished paying still has a protected **possessory interest**.
- The hearing must come **before** the seizure, absent extraordinary circumstances (e.g., an urgent public interest, or the state keeping strict control over the process).
- Common mistake: thinking a post-seizure remedy is enough. Under Fuentes it generally isn't.

### Document: *Connecticut v. Doehr* (CB 96–101)
**Rule: Modified Mathews v. Eldridge balancing** for prejudgment remedies between private parties:
1. The **private interest** affected by the prejudgment measure;
2. The **risk of erroneous deprivation** under existing procedures, and the probable value of **additional safeguards**;
3. The **interest of the party seeking the remedy**, plus any **ancillary government interest** in providing the procedure or avoiding added burdens.

**Case: Connecticut v. Doehr** (U.S. 1991)
- DiGiovanni sued Doehr for **assault and battery** and got a **$75,000 attachment on Doehr's home** *ex parte*, based on a conclusory affidavit, with no bond and no exigency.
- **Holding:** Violates due process.
  - (1) Attachment impairs the ability to sell, mortgage, or refinance, clouds title, and hurts credit. That is a significant interest even without physical dispossession.
  - (2) **High risk of error:** a tort claim turns on disputed facts and can't be shown by documents the way a debt can. A one-sided affidavit can't reliably establish probable cause.
  - (3) The plaintiff had **no pre-existing interest** in the house and showed **no exigency** (no sign Doehr was transferring the property).
- Doehr gives you the **framework** (balancing). Fuentes gives the **default rule** (a hearing before deprivation). On an exam, run the three Doehr factors.
- Facts that would change the result: the plaintiff already holds an interest in the property (e.g., a mortgage or lien), there is an exigency (the defendant is about to hide or transfer assets), a **bond**, or a prompt post-deprivation hearing.

# UNIT II — Pleadings & the Pleading Standard (Classes 5–9)

| Era | Date | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
|Common Law Writs|Developed in England from the 1100s–1200s (Henry II's reign, 1154–1189). Carried over to the American colonies and states, where they lasted until the mid-1800s.|The plaintiff had to fit the claim into a specific writ, such as trespass or assumpsit. Choosing the wrong writ meant losing, even with a valid claim.|
|Code Pleading|1848: New York's Field Code|The plaintiff had to plead the "facts constituting a cause of action." Courts fought over the difference between "ultimate facts," which were proper, and "evidence" or "legal conclusions," which were not.|
|FRCP / notice pleading|1934: the Rules Enabling Act authorized the rules. 1938: the FRCP took effect. 1957: Conley v. Gibson.|Rule 8(a)(2) requires only a "short and plain statement of the claim." Conley held a complaint survives unless it appears "beyond doubt" that the plaintiff can prove "no set of facts" supporting the claim.|
|Plausibility pleading|2007: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, an antitrust case. 2009: Ashcroft v. Iqbal.|Twombly retired Conley's "no set of facts" language and required facts making the claim plausible, not merely conceivable. Iqbal applied that standard to all civil cases. It also set a two-step test: (1) disregard legal conclusions, then (2) ask whether the remaining factual allegations plausibly suggest liability.|

## Class 5 (9/2) — Early Pleading Regimes

### Document: Noll, *A Reader's Guide to Pre-Modern Procedure* (CB 162–70)
- **Common law:** You needed the right **writ** (form of action) or you had no case. Pleadings went back and forth to narrow everything to a single issue. Law and equity were separate courts, and **equity had no jury**.
- **Equity:** More flexible. Used documents and affidavits, offered injunctions and specific performance, and had no jury. The **law/equity line resurfaces in the 7th Amendment analysis** (Unit V).

### Document: Note on Code Pleading (CB 173–74)
- The **Field Code** (NY 1848) merged law and equity into one "civil action." The complaint had to state the "**facts constituting the cause of action**."
- **Problem:** Courts fought endlessly over **fact vs. legal conclusion vs. evidence**. Twombly and Iqbal revive a version of this distinction (disregard "conclusions").

### Document: Rules 2, 3, 7, and 8 (CB 175–77)
**Rule 2:** One form of action, the **civil action**.
**Rule 3:** An action is **commenced by filing a complaint** with the court.
**Rule 7(a):** The only **pleadings** allowed are the complaint, the answer (to a complaint, counterclaim, crossclaim, or third-party complaint), and a reply to an answer **only if the court orders one**. **Motions are not pleadings.** This matters for Rule 15(a)(1)(B) and Rule 12(c) ("after the pleadings are closed").

**Rule 8(a): A claim must contain:**
1. a short and plain statement of the grounds for **jurisdiction**;
2. a **short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief**;
3. a **demand for the relief** sought (it may be in the alternative or of different types).

**Rule 8(e):** "Pleadings must be construed so as to do justice."

### Document: *Dioguardi v. Durning* (CB 182–84)
**Case: Dioguardi v. Durning** (2d Cir. 1944, Judge Clark, a drafter of the FRCP)
- A pro se immigrant's inartful complaint against the customs collector over his tonic shipment. The district court dismissed it. The **2d Circuit reversed**.
- However poorly written, the complaint gave enough notice of the claim under Rule 8(a). A complaint isn't dismissed for **lack of detail** or poor form. The defendant's remedy is discovery and summary judgment.
- This is the **high-water mark of liberal "notice pleading."** It contrasts with code pleading's technicality.

### Document: *Conley v. Gibson* (CB 186–90)
**Case: Conley v. Gibson** (U.S. 1957)
- Black railway clerks alleged their union failed to represent them fairly because of race (their jobs were "abolished," then refilled with white workers). The lower courts dismissed. The **Supreme Court reversed**.
- **"No set of facts" standard:** A complaint should not be dismissed "unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove **no set of facts** in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief."
- The Rules require only "**fair notice** of what the claim is and the grounds upon which it rests." Specific facts aren't needed. Discovery and summary judgment do the screening.
- **Retired by Twombly.** On an exam, "any set of facts could be imagined" is the **wrong** modern standard (PM3 Q1, answer B is a trap).

## Class 6 (9/3) — The Twombly and Iqbal Standard

### Document: *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (CB 192–205)
**Rule:** A complaint must plead "**enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face**." Factual allegations must "**raise a right to relief above the speculative level**."

**Case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly** (U.S. 2007)
- A putative class action alleging the "Baby Bells" (ILECs) **conspired** (Sherman Act § 1) to block new competitors and not compete with each other. The complaint alleged only **parallel conduct** plus a conclusory "agreement."
- **Holding:** Dismissal was proper. Parallel conduct is "consistent with" conspiracy, but it is "**just as much in line with**" independent, rational business behavior. The complaint needs facts suggesting an **actual agreement** (context, or "plus factors").
- **"Plausible" is more than "conceivable" or "possible" but less than "probable."** Detailed facts aren't required, but labels, conclusions, and a "formulaic recitation of the elements" won't do.
- **"No set of facts" is retired.**
- **Policy:** The **cost of discovery** (especially in antitrust) and the risk of *in terrorem* settlements justify screening at the pleading stage.

### Document: *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (CB 207–19)
**Rule: The Iqbal two-step** (applies to **all** civil actions, not just antitrust):
1. **Identify and disregard conclusory allegations**: legal conclusions and "threadbare recitals of the elements" aren't presumed true.
2. **Assume the truth of the remaining well-pleaded factual allegations** and ask whether they **plausibly** suggest entitlement to relief. This is "**context-specific**" and draws on "**judicial experience and common sense**."

**Case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal** (U.S. 2009)
- A Pakistani Muslim detainee after 9/11 sued AG Ashcroft and FBI Director Mueller (a Bivens action), alleging they adopted a policy of harsh confinement **because of** race, religion, and national origin.
- **Conclusory, so disregarded:** Ashcroft was the "principal architect," Mueller was "instrumental," and they "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed" to the policy.
- **Remaining facts** (thousands of Arab Muslim men detained) are explained by the "**obvious alternative explanation**": a legitimate policy targeting people with suspected links to the 9/11 attackers, which would produce a **disparate impact**. That isn't purposeful discrimination, so the claim is **not plausible**.
- **No supervisory liability by mere knowledge.** Each official is liable only for their **own** misconduct, and purposeful discrimination means acting "**because of**," not "in spite of," the effect.
- Rule 9(b)'s "intent … may be alleged generally" does **not** permit conclusory allegations of intent.
- Exam use: Run both steps **explicitly**. Strike the conclusions, then weigh the remaining facts against any obvious alternative explanation.

## Class 7 (9/8) — Pleading after Twombly and Iqbal

### Document: *Johnson v. City of Shelby* (CB 221–22)
**Case: Johnson v. City of Shelby** (U.S. 2014, per curiam)
- Police officers alleged they were fired for exposing an alderman's criminal activity. The lower courts dismissed because the complaint **didn't cite 42 U.S.C. § 1983**. The **Supreme Court reversed**.
- The FRCP **don't require pleading the legal theory or statute**. Twombly and Iqbal concern **factual** allegations, not "punctiliously stated" legal theories.
- Exam use: A complaint that alleges facts plausibly showing a violation survives even if it names the wrong statute or none at all. The fix is to amend, not dismiss.

### Document: *In re Text Messaging Antitrust Litigation* (CB 224–27)
**Case: In re Text Messaging Antitrust Litig.** (7th Cir. 2010, Posner)
- Four carriers with about 90% of the market. On an **interlocutory appeal under § 1292(b)** (a link to Unit VI), the court held the complaint **plausibly alleged price-fixing**.
- **"Plus factors"** beyond parallel conduct: a concentrated market; **simultaneous price increases while costs fell**; a switch to an identical pricing structure; meetings through a **trade association** (an opportunity to conspire).
- **Plausibility ≠ probability.** The complaint needn't exclude innocent explanations. **Circumstantial evidence** is enough at the pleading stage.
- **PM3 Q7 was this case.** Near-simultaneous price hikes, CEO statements about "price stability," an internal "we'll follow their lead" email, and CEOs at the same resort make a plausible claim **even though innocent explanations exist** (answer C).

### Document: *Swanson v. Citibank* (CB 228–38)
**Case: Swanson v. Citibank** (7th Cir. 2010, Wood; Posner dissenting in part)
- A Black homeowner was denied a home-equity loan after a low appraisal. She alleged race discrimination (Fair Housing Act).
- **Majority:** Plausibility means the plaintiff "must give enough details about the subject-matter of the case to present a **story that holds together**." It's enough to identify the **type** of discrimination (race), **who** did it (Citibank), and **when** (Feb. 2009). Twombly and Iqbal didn't reinstate fact pleading. The **fraud** claims were properly dismissed.
- **Posner's dissent:** There is an obvious alternative explanation (the 2009 financial crisis, tightened lending), and no comparator allegations (similarly situated white applicants). So it is not plausible.
- Shows that **plausibility is context-dependent and judges disagree**. Simple claims need less. Complex claims (antitrust, high-official misconduct) need more. Argue both sides on the exam.

## Class 8 (9/9) — Pleading in the Alternative; Rule 11; Heightened Pleading

### Document: *Beyond Plausibility* (CB 251)
- Other constraints on what goes in a complaint: **pleading in the alternative** (8(d)), **Rule 11** certification, and **heightened pleading** (9(b), PSLRA). Each is a separate check from Rule 8(a)(2) plausibility.

### Document: Rule 8(d) (CB 252–53)
**Rule 8(d)(1):** Each allegation must be **simple, concise, and direct**. No technical form is required.
**Rule 8(d)(2):** A party may set out **two or more statements of a claim or defense alternatively or hypothetically**. If **any one** of them is sufficient, the pleading is sufficient.
**Rule 8(d)(3):** A party may state as many separate claims or defenses as it has, "**regardless of consistency**."

### Document: *McCormick v. Kopmann* (CB 254–58)
**Case: McCormick v. Kopmann** (Ill. App. 1959)
- McCormick was killed in a collision with Kopmann's truck. His widow sued **Kopmann** (Count I, wrongful death: Kopmann's negligence caused it and the decedent was **free of contributory negligence**) and the **Huls tavern owners** (Count IV, Dram Shop Act: the decedent's **intoxication** caused the crash).
- These counts are factually **inconsistent**. Kopmann argued they were "repugnant." **The court allowed them.**
- The rule: a pleader **genuinely in doubt** about the facts may plead inconsistent alternatives. The jury sorts it out. Verdict: Kopmann liable, Huls not.
- **Limit:** No alternative pleading when the pleader **knows** which version is true. That would be a false statement under Rule 11.
- **PM3 Q2 was this case.** P's survivor, who can't know what happened, may plead "driver ran the red light" **or** "Brakes-R-Us serviced the brakes negligently." The court should **allow alternative and inconsistent theories** (answer B).

### Document: Rule 11 (CB 259–61)
**Rule 11(a):** Every pleading, written motion, and other paper must be **signed** by an attorney (or a pro se party).

**Rule 11(b):** By presenting it, the signer certifies, **after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances**, that:
1. it's **not for an improper purpose** (harass, delay, increase cost);
2. the **legal contentions** are warranted by existing law or a **nonfrivolous argument** to extend, modify, or reverse it;
3. the **factual contentions have evidentiary support** (or, if specifically so identified, will likely have it after discovery);
4. **denials** are warranted on the evidence (or reasonably based on belief or lack of information).

**Rule 11(c):** If 11(b) is violated, the court **may** impose an appropriate sanction on the attorney, firm, or party.
- **Safe harbor, 11(c)(2):** A sanctions motion must be made **separately**. It is **served but not filed**, and can be filed only if the offending paper isn't withdrawn or corrected within **21 days**.
- **11(c)(4):** The sanction is limited to what **suffices to deter** repetition. It can be nonmonetary, a penalty paid into court, or the opponent's fees.

**Rule 11(d):** Rule 11 does **not** apply to discovery (Rules 26–37). **Discovery has its own certification in Rule 26(g).**
- **PM2 Q1 trap:** A legally baseless complaint calls for a **12(b)(6)** motion, not sanctions "under Rule 37."

### Document: *Brown v. Ameriprise Financial Services* (CB 262–70)
**Case: Brown v. Ameriprise Fin. Servs.** (D. Minn. 2011)
- A plaintiff (herself an attorney) filed a putative race-discrimination class action whose allegations were **copied nearly verbatim from an old complaint against Coca-Cola**. At her deposition she couldn't support them.
- Ameriprise **served (didn't file)** a Rule 11 motion, giving her the safe harbor. She refused to withdraw, so it was filed.
- **Violation of 11(b)(3):** no reasonable pre-filing inquiry. "**A plaintiff must adequately plead a claim before obtaining discovery**." You can't "assert first and discover later." "**A shot in the dark is a sanctionable event, even if it somehow hits the mark.**"
- The magistrate recommended striking the class claims and awarding fees. The **district judge went further and dismissed the entire complaint**. The **primary purpose of Rule 11 is deterrence, not compensation**, and a fee award wouldn't deter *in terrorem* class claims.
- **Abandoning the claims doesn't moot sanctions** (Cooter & Gell): the violation is complete when the paper is filed. Courts also have **inherent power** to sanction abuse of the judicial process.

### Document: Introductory Note on Heightened Pleading (CB 271–72)
**Rule 9(b):** For **fraud or mistake**, the circumstances must be stated **with particularity** (who, what, when, where, how). **Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of mind may be alleged generally**, but per Iqbal, not in conclusory terms.

**PSLRA (securities fraud):** A "super-heightened" standard. The plaintiff must plead with particularity facts giving rise to a **strong inference of scienter**.
- Know the **hierarchy**: 8(a)(2) plausibility (default) → 9(b) particularity (fraud or mistake) → PSLRA (securities fraud).

## Class 9 (9/10) — Responding to the Complaint; Amendment

### Document: The Answer (CB 280–81) and Rule 8(b)–(c) (CB 281–82)
**Rule 8(b): Denials.**
- Admit or deny each allegation. A denial must **fairly respond to the substance**. A party can admit in part and deny the rest.
- **8(b)(5):** Lacking knowledge or information sufficient to form a belief **has the effect of a denial**.
- **8(b)(6):** An allegation (other than the amount of damages) is **admitted if not denied** when a response is required.

**Rule 8(c): Affirmative defenses** must be stated in the answer. Examples: **statute of limitations**, **res judicata**, **estoppel**, release, payment, waiver, fraud, contributory negligence.
- An affirmative defense says "even if everything you allege is true, you still lose because of X." **The defendant bears the burden** of pleading and proving it.

### Document: Rule 12 (CB 283–86)
**Rule 12(a): Timing.**
- Answer **21 days** after service of the summons and complaint. **60 days** if service was **waived** (Rule 4(d)).
- **12(a)(4):** Filing a Rule 12 motion **suspends** the answer deadline. If the motion is denied, answer **14 days after notice of the court's action**.
- **PM2 Q8:** Answering 30 days after denial is **untimely**.

**Rule 12(b): The seven defenses.** These may be raised by **motion before pleading** or in the answer:
1. lack of **subject-matter** jurisdiction
2. lack of **personal** jurisdiction
3. improper **venue**
4. insufficient **process** (a defect in the summons itself)
5. insufficient **service** of process (delivered improperly; *see* PM2 Q3)
6. **failure to state a claim** upon which relief can be granted
7. failure to **join a party** under Rule 19

**Rule 12(c): Judgment on the pleadings.** Made **after the pleadings are closed** but early enough not to delay trial. It looks at the complaint **and** the answer.

**Rule 12(d):** If matters **outside the pleadings** are presented and not excluded, a 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion **becomes a Rule 56 summary judgment motion**.

**Rule 12(e): More definite statement.** For a pleading so **vague or ambiguous** the party can't reasonably prepare a response. It must be made **before** the responsive pleading.

**Rule 12(f): Strike.** Removes an **insufficient defense** or **redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous** matter. Made before responding, or within **21 days** after being served if no response is allowed. Motions to strike are **disfavored**.

**Rule 12(g)(2) and 12(h): Waiver.**
- **12(h)(1):** Defenses **(b)(2)–(5)** (personal jurisdiction, venue, process, service) are **WAIVED** if left out of a pre-answer Rule 12 motion, or if not raised in the first responsive pleading or an amendment as of course. Use them or lose them.
- **12(h)(2):** **Failure to state a claim** (and failure to join a Rule 19 party, or to state a legal defense) can be raised **in any pleading, by 12(c) motion, or at trial**.
- **12(h)(3):** **Subject-matter jurisdiction** can be raised **at any time**. The court must dismiss.

**Applying these on the exam (PM1 Q1–2):**
- To argue the complaint fails legally (e.g., the First Amendment doesn't reach a private school) at the earliest opportunity, file a **12(b)(6)** motion.
- If the defendant **already answered** and the 21-day amend-as-of-course window has passed, file a **12(c)** motion, which 12(h)(2) allows.

### Document: *Bowers v. Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems* (CB 288–91)
**Case: Bowers v. MERS** (D. Kan. 2011)
- Mortgagors moved under **12(f)** to strike the defendants' bare-bones **affirmative defenses** as not meeting Twombly and Iqbal.
- **Holding: Twombly and Iqbal plausibility does NOT apply to affirmative defenses.** The motion to strike was denied.
- **Text:** 8(a)(2) requires a "short and plain statement … **showing** that the pleader is entitled to relief." 8(b) and 8(c) only require a party to "**state**" its defenses. There is no "showing" requirement.
- **Fairness:** A defendant has only **21 days** to answer, while a plaintiff can investigate for years before filing (up to the statute of limitations). Motions to strike are **disfavored**, and discovery will flesh out the defenses.

### Document: *Perez v. Gordon & Wong Law Group* (CB 292–95)
**Case: Perez v. Gordon & Wong Law Group** (N.D. Cal. 2012, Koh)
- A debt-collection (FDCPA) plaintiff moved to strike **15 boilerplate affirmative defenses**.
- **Holding: Twombly and Iqbal DO apply to affirmative defenses.** All but one were struck, **with leave to amend**.
- **Reasoning:** The same purpose (fair notice, weeding out boilerplate) applies. Bare legal conclusions ("failure to mitigate," "unclean hands") with no facts don't give fair notice. Some listed "defenses" (e.g., failure to state a claim) **aren't affirmative defenses at all**; they just negate an element.
- **Bowers vs. Perez is a district-court split.** On an exam, give **both** positions and the textual argument ("showing" vs. "state").

### Document: Rule 15 (CB 297–98)
**Rule 15(a)(1): Amend once as a matter of course** (no permission needed):
- (A) within **21 days after serving** the pleading; **or**
- (B) if a responsive pleading is required, within **21 days after service of the responsive pleading**, or **21 days after service of a 12(b), (e), or (f) motion**, whichever is earlier.

**Rule 15(a)(2): Otherwise** you need the opposing party's **written consent** or the **court's leave**. "The court should **freely give leave when justice so requires**."

**Rule 15(a)(3):** Respond to an amended pleading within the time remaining or **14 days**, whichever is later.

**Foman v. Davis reasons to deny leave:** **undue delay**, **bad faith or dilatory motive**, **repeated failure to cure**, **undue prejudice** to the opponent, and **futility**.
- **Prejudice** means harm to the opponent's ability to litigate the merits (lost witnesses or evidence, a need for new discovery). The **mere passage of time** or **planning around the old pleading** isn't enough.

**Exam calls:**
- **PM2 Q10:** Answer served 10 days ago → **amend as of course** under 15(a)(1)(A), answer D.
- **PM1 Q2:** Answer served 25 days ago → the window is closed, so get leave or consent, or use 12(c).
- **PM3 Q5:** Amendment 18 months in, no new discovery needed, and D's only claimed "prejudice" is strategy planning → **grant leave** (answer B).

**Also know:** 15(b) (amendments during or after trial to conform to the evidence), 15(c) (**relation back** to the original filing date for statute-of-limitations purposes), and 15(d) (supplemental pleadings about events **after** the original pleading).

### Document: *Global Tech. & Trading v. Tech Mahindra* (CB 299–302)
**Case: Global Tech. & Trading v. Tech Mahindra** (7th Cir. 2015, Easterbrook)
- A broker sued for a roughly $600K commission on an oral deal. **Four years in**, the defendant raised for the first time (at **summary judgment**) that the **Illinois Business Brokers Act** barred the claim. That is an **affirmative defense omitted from the answer**.
- **Holding:** Not fatal. Rule 8(c) says affirmative defenses belong in the answer, but **a late defense can be allowed absent prejudice**. Effectively it's a **Rule 15 amendment**.
- **"Prejudice" means harm to the plaintiff's ability to meet the defense on the merits** (e.g., a witness died or documents were lost). **Extra litigation expense or a lost tactical advantage isn't prejudice.** Here the defense was purely legal, so there was no prejudice.
- Easterbrook also notes the distinction between "**waiver**" (intentional relinquishment) and "**forfeiture**" (failure to raise in time).

### Document: *Beeck v. Aquaslide 'N' Dive Corp.* (CB 302–06)
**Case: Beeck v. Aquaslide 'N' Dive Corp.** (8th Cir. 1977)
- Beeck was badly injured on a water slide. Aquaslide **admitted manufacturing it** in its answer (relying on **three insurers'** investigations). **After the statute of limitations ran**, Aquaslide's president inspected the slide, found it was a **knock-off**, and moved to amend to **deny** manufacture.
- The trial court **granted leave** and held a **separate trial** on manufacture. The jury found Aquaslide didn't make it. **Affirmed:** no abuse of discretion.
- **Foman factors:** There was **no bad faith** (reasonable reliance on the insurers, prompt action once the truth was found). **Prejudice** to the plaintiffs (time-barred against the real maker) is serious but not decisive. They may have other remedies (e.g., against the seller or owner). It would be unjust to make Aquaslide pay for a slide it didn't make.
- Leave is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**. Leave to amend is **liberally granted**. Deny it only for bad faith, undue delay, undue prejudice, or futility.

# UNIT III — Discovery (Classes 10–12)

## Class 10 (9/15) — The Rules Framework for Discovery

### Document: Rules 26, 30, and 33–37 (CB 319–46)
**Rule 26(a): Required disclosures** (no request needed).
- **26(a)(1) initial disclosures:**
  - (i) names of people likely to have discoverable info that the party **may use to support** its claims or defenses (**not** info used **solely for impeachment**);
  - (ii) copies or descriptions of documents and ESI the party may use;
  - (iii) a **damages computation**;
  - (iv) **insurance agreements**.
- Due within **14 days after the 26(f) conference**.
- **26(a)(2):** expert disclosures. **26(a)(3):** pretrial disclosures.

**Rule 26(b)(1): Scope of discovery** (the core test). Any matter that is:
1. **nonprivileged**,
2. **relevant to any party's claim or defense**, and
3. **proportional to the needs of the case**.

The proportionality factors are the importance of the issues, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative **access** to information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery, and whether the **burden or expense outweighs the likely benefit**.
- Information **need not be admissible** to be discoverable.

**Rule 26(b)(2)(C):** The court **must** limit discovery that is unreasonably **cumulative or duplicative**, obtainable from a **more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive source**, sought after ample opportunity to get it, or outside 26(b)(1).

**Rule 26(b)(5): Privilege claims.**
- **(A) Privilege log:** The withholding party must **expressly claim** the privilege and **describe** what's withheld well enough for others to assess the claim **without revealing** the privileged content.
- **(B) Clawback procedure:** The producing party **notifies** the receiving party. The receiver must promptly **return, sequester, or destroy** the material, **may not use or disclose it** until the claim is resolved, and may present it to the court **under seal** for a ruling.

**Rule 26(c): Protective orders.**
- Requires a motion, a **good-faith meet-and-confer certification**, and **good cause**.
- Protects against **annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense**.
- Options include forbidding the discovery, limiting its scope, setting terms, sealing a deposition, and **(c)(1)(G): requiring that a trade secret or confidential commercial information not be revealed, or be revealed only in a specified way**.
- **PM2 Q5:** The soda formula is relevant and not privileged (not legal advice, not prepared for litigation), so the right move is a **protective order under 26(c)(1)(G)** (answer C).

**Other Rule 26 provisions:**
- **26(d)(1):** No discovery before the **26(f) conference** (with limited exceptions).
- **26(e):** **Duty to supplement** disclosures and responses that turn out to be incomplete or incorrect.
- **26(f):** The parties must confer early to plan discovery (preservation of ESI, privilege issues, a phased plan) and submit a written report.
- **26(g): Certification for discovery** (the counterpart to Rule 11). Signing certifies, after **reasonable inquiry**, that disclosures are complete and correct, and that requests, responses, and objections are warranted, **not for an improper purpose**, and **not unreasonable or unduly burdensome**. Under **26(g)(3)** the court **must** sanction a violation that lacks substantial justification.

**The discovery devices:**

| Device | Key limits |
|---|---|
| **Rule 30 depositions** | **10 per side** without leave; **1 day of 7 hours** each. **30(b)(6):** the organization designates who testifies for it. **30(c)(2):** objections must be concise and nonargumentative. An instruction **not to answer** is allowed only to preserve a privilege, enforce a court limit, or present a 30(d)(3) motion. |
| **Rule 33 interrogatories** | **Parties only**, not their lawyers (Hickman). **25** including subparts. Answer in **30 days**. **33(b)(4):** objections must be **specific** or they're **waived**. **33(d):** you may answer by producing business records. |
| **Rule 34 production** | Documents, ESI, tangible things, entry onto land. Respond in **30 days**. Objections must be **specific** and **state whether responsive material is being withheld**. Nonparties are reached by **Rule 45** subpoena. |
| **Rule 35 exams** | Physical or mental exam **only by court order**, on **good cause**, when the condition is **in controversy**. |
| **Rule 36 admissions** | **Deemed admitted** if not answered in **30 days**. Admissions are **conclusively established** unless the court allows withdrawal (36(b)). |

**Rule 37: Enforcement.**
- **37(a): Motion to compel** (must certify a good-faith attempt to confer). An evasive or incomplete answer counts as a failure to answer. **37(a)(5):** the **loser pays expenses** unless substantially justified.
- **37(b)(2): Violating a court order.** Sanctions include facts **deemed established**, **precluding** evidence, **striking pleadings**, **dismissal**, **default judgment**, and **contempt**.
- **37(c)(1):** Failing to disclose or supplement means the party **can't use** that info or witness unless the failure was **substantially justified or harmless**.
- **37(d):** Failing to attend your own deposition or to answer interrogatories at all permits sanctions **without a prior order**.
- **37(e): Lost ESI.**
  - Applies when ESI that should have been preserved is lost through a failure to take reasonable steps and **can't be restored**.
  - On **prejudice**, the court may order measures **no greater than necessary to cure** it.
  - **Only on intent to deprive** may the court impose an **adverse inference**, **dismissal**, or **default**.

### Document: *Mancia v. Mayflower Textile Services* (CB 348–60)
**Case: Mancia v. Mayflower Textile Servs.** (D. Md. 2008, Magistrate Judge Grimm)
- FLSA overtime plaintiffs moved to compel. The defendants used **boilerplate objections** ("overly broad, unduly burdensome, not reasonably calculated…") with no specifics.
- **Boilerplate objections violate Rules 33(b)(4) and 34(b)(2)** (objections must be **specific**), and **unparticularized objections are waived**.
- **Rule 26(g)** imposes an **objective** duty to "**stop and think**" before serving any request, response, or objection. It covers *requests* (overbroad demands) as much as *responses*.
- The court ordered a **cooperative meet-and-confer**: estimate the realistic **damages and fees** range (the amount in controversy), then size discovery **proportionally** and consider **phased discovery** (cheapest, most promising sources first).
- **Theme:** Cooperation in discovery is **consistent with** zealous advocacy, not opposed to it.

## Class 11 (9/16) — Privilege & Work Product

**Two distinct protections. Don't mix them up.**

| | **Attorney-client privilege** | **Work product (Hickman; Rule 26(b)(3))** |
|---|---|---|
| Protects | **Confidential communications** between lawyer and client for **legal advice** | **Documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation** by or for a party or its **representative** |
| Strength | **Absolute** (unless waived or crime-fraud) | **Qualified.** *Fact* work product yields to **substantial need + undue hardship**. *Opinion* work product is near-absolute. |
| Facts? | **Facts are NOT privileged**, only the communication | **Underlying facts are NOT protected.** You can depose the witness. |
| Waiver | Any disclosure to a third party (outside the privilege) | Disclosure to an **adversary or a "conduit" to an adversary** |

### Document: *SodexoMAGIC, LLC v. Drexel University* (CB 385–89)
**Rule: Attorney-client privilege elements:**
1. a communication by a client (or prospective client);
2. to a lawyer (or a lawyer's subordinate) **acting as a lawyer**;
3. made **in confidence**;
4. **for the purpose of obtaining legal advice**; and
5. not waived.

**Case: SodexoMAGIC v. Drexel University** (E.D. Pa. 2018)
- A contract dispute. The court reviewed corporate emails **in camera** for privilege.
- **In-house counsel's** communications are privileged **only when the purpose is legal advice, not business advice** (the "**primary purpose**" test for mixed messages). The party claiming privilege bears the **burden**.
- **Merely copying a lawyer** on an email doesn't make it privileged. A lawyer or paralegal acting as a "**scrivener**" (just typing in requested changes) isn't giving legal advice.
- An internal business email that **relies on** prior legal advice isn't privileged unless it **repeats counsel's advice essentially verbatim**. Then only that part is privileged and gets **redacted**. The rest is produced.
- **Sending a privileged email outside the company** (e.g., to "College X") **destroys the privilege** as to that communication.

### Document: *Upjohn Co. v. United States* (CB 390–96)
**Case: Upjohn Co. v. United States** (U.S. 1981)
- Upjohn's general counsel investigated foreign bribes. **Questionnaires** went to managers over the Chairman's signature, stamped **highly confidential**, with answers returned to the GC. The IRS summoned the questionnaires and interview notes.
- **Holding: The "control group" test is rejected.** The privilege covers communications from **employees at any level** made **at the direction of corporate superiors**, **to counsel acting as counsel**, to **secure legal advice** for the corporation, about matters **within the scope of the employee's duties**, where the employees knew the purpose and the communications were **kept confidential**.
- **Rationale:** Lower-level employees have the info lawyers need. The privilege protects giving information **to** the lawyer, not just advice **from** the lawyer. It encourages corporate **compliance**.
- **Facts aren't privileged.** The IRS could still interview the employees itself. Convenience to the government doesn't override the privilege.
- **Work product part:** Attorney **notes and memos** of interviews reveal the attorney's **mental processes** (opinion work product). They get **special protection** that isn't overcome by a simple showing of substantial need.

### Document: *Hickman v. Taylor* (CB 398–409)
**Case: Hickman v. Taylor** (U.S. 1947), the **origin of the work-product doctrine**
- A tug sank and 5 crew died. The tug owners' lawyer **Fortenbaugh** privately interviewed the survivors and took **signed statements**, and made **memoranda** of other interviews. The plaintiff (by interrogatory) demanded copies of the statements and accounts of the oral statements. Fortenbaugh refused and was **held in contempt**.
- **Holding: Not discoverable** on a "**naked, general demand**" with **no showing of necessity**.
  - The **attorney-client privilege does NOT apply**: these are statements from third-party witnesses, not client communications. But the attorney's **work product** is protected by a separate policy.
  - A lawyer must be able to prepare "**with a certain degree of privacy**." Otherwise "much of what is now put down in writing would remain unwritten," and the profession would be demoralized.
  - The **burden is on the party seeking discovery** to show **necessity or justification** (e.g., the witness is unavailable or can be reached only with difficulty).
  - Here the survivors' **public testimony** was available and they **could be interviewed directly**, so there was no need.
  - **Oral statements and the lawyer's memos of them** get even more protection. Forcing disclosure would make the attorney a **witness** and risk inaccuracy.
- **Interrogatories go to parties, not lawyers.**
- Justice **Jackson's concurrence:** "Discovery was hardly intended to enable a learned profession to perform its functions … **on wits borrowed from the adversary**."
- **Now codified in Rule 26(b)(3).**

## Class 12 (9/22) — Privilege & Work Product (cont.)

### Document: Rule 26(b) (CB 411–12)
**Rule 26(b)(3)(A): Fact work product.**
- Covers documents and tangible things **prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial** by or for a party or its **representative**. "Representative" includes the **attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer, or agent**.
- Discoverable **only if** (i) otherwise within 26(b)(1) **and** (ii) the requester shows **substantial need** and **cannot, without undue hardship, obtain the substantial equivalent** by other means.

**Rule 26(b)(3)(B): Opinion work product.** Even when ordering discovery, the court **must protect** the **mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories** of an attorney or other representative. This is near-absolute.

**Rule 26(b)(3)(C):** A party or witness can always get a copy of **their own previous statement**.
- **"In anticipation of litigation":** Most courts use a "**because of**" test. It's work product if prepared **because of** the prospect of litigation, even if it also serves business purposes, but **not** if it would have been prepared in substantially the same form anyway (e.g., routine reports).
- **Waiver (Sedona):** Disclosing work product waives protection only if the recipient is an **adversary or a "conduit" to one**. Sharing with an interested non-adversary does not waive it.

**Exam calls:**
- **PM1 Q3:** The insurer's report on whether Waterslide Co. made the slide is **work product**, since an insurer is a "representative." The plaintiff examined the slide themselves, so there's **no substantial need**. **But the facts aren't protected**: the plaintiff can **depose the insurer** about who made the slide (answer B).
- **PM3 Q4:** In-house counsel commissioned a root-cause report two days after an explosion. It is **work product if prepared because of anticipated litigation** (answer A). A separate SEC filing obligation doesn't negate this, and in-house counsel counts.

### Document: Federal Rule of Evidence 502 (CB 431–32) 
- **Traditional rule:** **Any** disclosure, even inadvertent, waived the privilege. That made privilege review hugely expensive.

**FRE 502(a): Intentional disclosure.** Waiver extends to **undisclosed** material on the **same subject matter** only if the waiver was **intentional**, the disclosed and undisclosed material **concern the same subject**, and they **ought in fairness be considered together**. This prevents selective "sword and shield" use.

**FRE 502(b): Inadvertent disclosure is NOT a waiver** if:
1. the disclosure was **inadvertent**;
2. the holder **took reasonable steps to prevent** disclosure; **and**
3. the holder **promptly took reasonable steps to rectify** the error, including following **FRCP 26(b)(5)(B)**.

**FRE 502(c):** Covers disclosures made in a state proceeding.

**FRE 502(d): Court order.** A federal court can order that disclosure connected with the litigation is **not a waiver**. The order **binds all other federal and state proceedings**, even if the production wasn't careful. This is the best protection.

**FRE 502(e): Party agreement (clawback).** Binds **only the parties** unless it's incorporated into a court order.

**Exam calls:**
- **PM1 Q4:** The lawyer **dropped the strategy memo on a stack** and handed the whole pile over in a rush. That is **not "reasonable steps to prevent"**, so it's **waived** and there's no clawback (answer B).
- **PM3 Q3:** The firm had **reasonable screening steps** and **promptly notified** the other side, so there's **no waiver** (answer B).

### Document: *In re Abilify Products Liability Litigation* (CB 419–29)
**Case: In re Abilify (Aripiprazole) Prods. Liab. Litig.** (N.D. Fla. 2017, MDL)
- Plaintiffs attacked the drug makers' **privilege logs** and categories of withheld documents. The court reviewed them all **in camera**.
- The **logs were adequate** under 26(b)(5)(A) and the court's ESI order. Most categorical challenges failed, though a few documents were ordered produced.
- **Work product extends to materials prepared for OTHER litigation** (prior product cases, regulatory investigations), not just the current case.
- Not naming a specific attorney ("Legal Dept.") isn't fatal if the context shows legal involvement. **Attachments** need not be separately logged if that would reveal privileged info.
- **Facts sent to counsel for legal advice:** the *communication* is privileged, but the underlying facts are still discoverable elsewhere.
- **"Primary purpose" test** for mixed business/legal communications. **Merely cc'ing a lawyer doesn't make it privileged**, but wide distribution **doesn't automatically defeat** privilege either.
- **Third-party consultants** (e.g., PR or marketing firms) acting as the **functional equivalent** of employees, or assisting counsel, **don't waive** the privilege.

### Document: Note on Privilege Logs, Redaction, and the Crime-Fraud Exception (CB 433–36)
**Case: Chevron Corp. v. Weinberg Group** (D.D.C. 2012). It arose from the Ecuador (Lago Agrio) judgment scandal: a consultant allegedly "cleansed" a tainted expert report.
- **Generic log entries** ("attorney-client privilege") **don't satisfy 26(b)(5)(A)**.
- Chevron showed **substantial need** for **fact work product**, so Weinberg had to produce it **with opinion work product redacted**. **Redaction** is the tool for mixed documents.

**Case: Chevron Corp. v. Donziger** (S.D.N.Y. 2013). Chevron sought documents from the plaintiffs' law firm (Patton Boggs).

**Rule: Crime-fraud exception.** Neither privilege nor work product protects communications **in furtherance of a contemplated or ongoing crime or fraud**, **even if the lawyer didn't know**. The focus is the **client's** purpose.
- **Standard:** **probable cause**, meaning a prudent person would have a reasonable basis to suspect the crime or fraud **and** that the communications **furthered** it.
- **PM3 Q8** (client asks the lawyer to **backdate a patent application**):
  - The client's first question ("is this lawful?") is arguably ordinary legal advice.
  - The client's later **instruction to draft a backdated version** is a communication seeking the lawyer's services **in furtherance of fraud**, so it is not privileged **even though the lawyer refused**. The intended answer is **C**.

# UNIT IV — Summary Judgment & Adjudication Without Trial (Classes 13–14)

**Three checkpoints, three standards:**

| Stage | Question | What the court looks at |
|---|---|---|
| **12(b)(6)** | Are the **allegations** plausible? | The complaint only. Allegations are taken as true. |
| **Rule 56 SJ** | Is there a **genuine dispute of material fact**, i.e., could a **reasonable jury** find for the nonmovant? | **Evidence** (depositions, affidavits, documents) viewed **most favorably to the nonmovant** |
| **Rule 50 JMOL** | Same "reasonable jury" test | **Trial evidence** |

## Class 13 (9/23) — Summary Judgment

### Document: Summary Judgment Note and Rule 56 (CB 563–64)
**Rule 56(a):** The court **shall grant** summary judgment if the movant shows (1) there is **no genuine dispute as to any material fact** and (2) the movant is **entitled to judgment as a matter of law**. It can be for the whole case, a claim, a defense, or **part** of a claim.

**Rule 56(b):** Unless the court orders otherwise, a party may move **any time until 30 days after the close of all discovery**.

**Rule 56(c)(1):** A party supports a factual position by **citing record materials** (depositions, documents, ESI, affidavits or declarations, admissions, interrogatory answers), **or** by showing the materials cited don't establish a dispute, or that the adverse party **cannot produce admissible evidence** to support the fact.

**Rule 56(c)(4):** Affidavits must be made on **personal knowledge**, set out facts that would be **admissible**, and show the affiant is **competent** to testify.

**Rule 56(d):** If the nonmovant shows by affidavit that it **can't yet present essential facts**, the court may defer or deny the motion or allow more discovery.

**Rule 56(e):** If a party fails to properly address another's assertion of fact, the court may treat the fact as **undisputed**.

**Rule 56(f):** The court may grant SJ **sua sponte** or on grounds not raised, **after notice** and a reasonable time to respond.

**Rule 56(g):** The court may also enter an order stating that material facts are not genuinely in dispute (**partial SJ**).

**Rule: Anderson v. Liberty Lobby** (U.S. 1986) (discussed in the note)
- **Material** means the fact **might affect the outcome under the governing substantive law**. The substantive law defines materiality. Example: carelessness is material in negligence but **immaterial** in strict liability.
- **Genuine** means the evidence is such that a **reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmovant**. A "scintilla" isn't enough.
- **The SJ standard mirrors the directed-verdict (Rule 50) standard.** It also incorporates the **substantive evidentiary burden** (e.g., "clear and convincing" in libel).
- **Credibility determinations, weighing evidence, and drawing inferences are jury functions.** The nonmovant's evidence "**is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor**."
- Brennan's dissent warned this could make SJ "a full-blown paper trial on the merits."

**Rule: Matsushita v. Zenith** (U.S. 1986)
- The substantive law can **limit permissible inferences**. In an antitrust conspiracy case, evidence **equally consistent with independent, lawful conduct** doesn't create a genuine issue. The plaintiff must offer evidence that "**tends to exclude the possibility**" of independent action. An **economically implausible** theory (predatory pricing conspiracy) needs **more persuasive** evidence.
- **Compare Twombly** (pleading) with **Matsushita** (SJ): the same "parallel conduct isn't enough" logic at different stages.

**Mueller (Taylor Swift case)** (discussed in the note): SJ was **denied** on the interference-with-employment claims because they turned on dueling, **credibility-dependent** accounts of the groping incident, which is for the jury. SJ was **granted** on the slander claims, which could be resolved **as a matter of law**. The same event can produce different SJ outcomes for different claims.

### Document: Introductory Note on the Celotex Trilogy (CB 578–81)
- **Trilogy (1986):** **Celotex** (the movant's burden), **Anderson** (the "genuine dispute" and "reasonable jury" standard, plus the substantive evidentiary standard), and **Matsushita** (implausible inferences). Together they made SJ a **robust** pretrial screening tool instead of a disfavored one.

### Document: Introductory Note to *Celotex v. Catrett* (CB 582–83)
**Trial burdens:**
- **Burden of production:** If **nobody** puts on evidence, the party with this burden (usually the **plaintiff**) **loses**. It's satisfied by evidence that, if believed, would entitle the party to relief. Once met, the **other side must produce contrary evidence** or lose.
- **Burden of persuasion:** If the evidence is in **equipoise** (50/50), the party with this burden (the **plaintiff**, by **preponderance**) **loses**.

**SJ burdens:**
- The **movant always bears the burden of persuasion** on the motion.
- **Plaintiff as movant** (bears the trial burden): must present evidence supporting **every element** such that **no reasonable jury** could find for the defendant. A motion with no evidence is a "non-starter."
- **Defendant as movant (option 1):** present evidence that **affirmatively disproves** an element. The plaintiff must then come back with controverting evidence.
- **Defendant as movant (option 2):** **just point out the absence of evidence**, with no evidence of its own. That is **the Celotex question**.

### Document: *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett* (CB 584–90)
**Case: Celotex Corp. v. Catrett** (U.S. 1986, Rehnquist)
- A widow sued asbestos makers for her husband's death. Celotex moved for SJ arguing the plaintiff **had no evidence** her husband was exposed to **Celotex's** products: she had **identified no witnesses** in **interrogatory answers**. Celotex attached **no affidavits** of its own. The D.C. Circuit said the motion was "fatally defective." **The Supreme Court reversed.**
- **Holding:** Rule 56 mandates SJ, **after adequate time for discovery**, against a party who **fails to make a showing sufficient to establish an essential element** of its case **on which it bears the burden of proof at trial**. A "**complete failure of proof**" on an essential element "renders all other facts immaterial."
- The movant who **doesn't bear the trial burden** need **not** negate the opponent's claim with affidavits. It may discharge its burden by "**pointing out** … **an absence of evidence** to support the nonmoving party's case," using the record (depositions, interrogatory answers, admissions).
- The movant must still "**inform the court of the basis**" for the motion and identify the relevant parts of the record. A **conclusory** "they have no evidence" isn't enough (**White's concurrence**; **Brennan's dissent**).
- The burden then **shifts**: the nonmovant must **go beyond the pleadings** and designate **specific facts** (with evidence, which **need not be in admissible form yet** but must be reducible to admissible evidence) showing a genuine issue for trial.
- SJ is "**an integral part of the Federal Rules**," not a "disfavored procedural shortcut." Under **notice pleading**, SJ (not 12(b)(6)) is the main tool for weeding out factually unsupported claims.
- On remand, the D.C. Circuit found the plaintiff's evidence (a letter, a deposition transcript) enough to survive.

**Exam framework for any SJ question (see the PM3 essay model notes):**
1. **State the Rule 56(a) standard** and the **Anderson** gloss.
2. **Lay out the substantive law, element by element.** The professor says *start here*.
3. **Who bears the burden at trial on each element?** If the movant doesn't, Celotex lets it point to the absence of evidence.
4. **For each element:** what evidence exists? View it most favorably to the **nonmovant** and draw all **reasonable** inferences in its favor. **Could a reasonable jury** find for the nonmovant?
5. **Separate genuine disputes** (credibility contests, which go to the jury) from **speculation or opinion without basis** (which doesn't create a genuine issue).
6. **Cross-motions:** analyze **each motion separately**, flipping whose view of the evidence controls (PM1 Q5: deny both).

**Exam applications:**
- **PM2 Q6:** There is a genuine dispute on **who was driving** (P's testimony vs. D's). But **limitations is an affirmative defense** on which **D bears the burden**. D produced **evidence** (Witness A: January 15, 2023, more than 2 years before February 5, 2025), and **P offered nothing** in response. So there is **no genuine dispute** on limitations, and D gets SJ (answer D).
- **PM3 essay (toxic exposure):**
  - **Elements (a) illness and (c) damages:** easily shown (a doctor's diagnosis; $99,600 in receipts).
  - **The battleground is (b) causation from workplace exposure.**
  - For Pamela: the doctor's note says "probably workplace," and toxic chemicals were onsite (both sides agree).
  - Against her: at deposition the doctor **disavowed** the note ("I don't know," it reflected bias against corporations). Pamela's own belief is **speculation** ("I bet that's what happened"), not personal knowledge. The CEO testified there were no exposure incidents.
  - **Ask:** can a reasonable jury infer actual exposure, or only guess? Argue both ways, then conclude.

## Class 14 (9/24) — Summary Judgment; Other Pre-Trial Dispositions

### Document: *Scott v. Harris* (CB 613–21)
**Case: Scott v. Harris** (U.S. 2007, Scalia)
- Harris fled a speeding stop at **85+ mph**. Deputy Scott **rammed his car from behind**. Harris crashed and became **quadriplegic**, then sued for Fourth Amendment excessive force. The lower courts **denied SJ** based on Harris's account (careful driving, roads cleared). This came up on **interlocutory appeal** (qualified immunity).
- **Holding:** SJ for Scott. "**When opposing parties tell two different stories, one of which is blatantly contradicted by the record, so that no reasonable jury could believe it, a court should not adopt that version of the facts** for purposes of ruling on a motion for summary judgment."
- The **undisputed, authentic dashcam video** showed a "Hollywood-style car chase of the most frightening sort." The Court viewed the facts "**in the light depicted by the videotape**."
- On the merits: ending a chase that endangers innocent bystanders is **reasonable** even if it risks the fleeing driver's life. That driver is culpable, and the bystanders are innocent.
- **Stevens's dissent:** Eight appellate judges below watched the same tape and saw something different, which is itself evidence that reasonable jurors could disagree. Judges are substituting their own **factfinding** for the jury's.
- **Exam:** Scott is a **narrow exception** to the "view facts most favorably to the nonmovant" rule. It needs objective evidence (like a video) of **unquestioned authenticity** that **blatantly** contradicts the account. Ordinary conflicting testimony is **not** enough. The jury decides that (Tolan).

### Document: *Tolan v. Cotton* 
**Case: Tolan v. Cotton** (U.S. 2014, per curiam)
- Police wrongly believed Tolan's car was stolen (a typo in the plate number) and confronted him on **his parents' porch**. Sgt. Cotton allegedly **slammed Tolan's mother** into the garage door. Tolan shouted "get your f---ing hands off my mom," and Cotton **shot him**. The district court granted SJ, and the **5th Circuit affirmed on qualified immunity**.
- **Holding: Vacated and remanded.** The 5th Circuit **credited the officer's version** on four disputed facts:
  1. **Lighting:** dim vs. floodlit
  2. **The mother's demeanor:** agitated vs. calm
  3. **Tolan's words:** a threat vs. a son's plea
  4. **Tolan's position:** charging vs. on his knees
- **Rule:** Even in **qualified immunity** cases, courts must **view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant** (Anderson). Witnesses on both sides have their "own perceptions, recollections, and even potential biases," so **credibility disputes are for the jury**.
- **Scott vs. Tolan:** Scott had an **objective video** that blatantly contradicted one story. Tolan was **testimony vs. testimony**, a classic **genuine dispute**, so SJ is improper.

### Document: Introductory Note on Default Judgment; Rules 41, 54(c), 55, and 60 (CB 309–13)
**Rule 55: Default.**
- **55(a) Entry of default:** The **clerk** enters default when a party **has failed to plead or otherwise defend**, as shown by affidavit or otherwise.
- **55(b)(1) Clerk's judgment:** Only for a **sum certain**, against a defendant who **hasn't appeared** and isn't a minor or incompetent.
- **55(b)(2) Court's judgment:** Required in all other cases. If the defaulting party **has appeared**, it gets **7 days' written notice** before the hearing.
- **55(c):** The court may set aside an **entry of default for good cause**, and a **default judgment under Rule 60(b)**.
- **55(d):** Default judgment against the **United States** only if the claimant establishes the claim **by evidence** satisfactory to the court.

**Rule 54(c):** A **default judgment must not differ in kind from, or exceed in amount, what is demanded in the pleadings**. Other final judgments should grant the relief the party is entitled to **even if not demanded**.

**Rule 60(b): Relief from a final judgment.** The grounds are:
1. **mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect**;
2. **newly discovered evidence** that couldn't have been found in time for a Rule 59(b) motion;
3. **fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct** by the opposing party;
4. the judgment is **void** (e.g., no jurisdiction, or **no constitutionally adequate notice**, which links to Mullane);
5. it has been satisfied or released, or prospective application is **no longer equitable**;
6. **any other reason** that justifies relief (extraordinary circumstances).

**Rule 60(c):** The motion must be made within a **reasonable time**, and for **(1)–(3), no more than 1 year** after the judgment.

**Rule 41(a): Voluntary dismissal.**
- **41(a)(1)(A)(i):** The plaintiff may dismiss **without a court order** by notice **before the opposing party serves an answer or an SJ motion**.
- **41(a)(1)(A)(ii):** Or by a stipulation signed by all parties.
- These are **without prejudice** unless stated otherwise.
- **The two-dismissal rule:** If the plaintiff previously dismissed an action **based on the same claim**, a notice dismissal **operates as an adjudication on the merits**.
- **41(a)(2):** Otherwise, dismissal is **by court order** on terms the court considers proper.

**Rule 41(b): Involuntary dismissal.** For failure to **prosecute** or to comply with the rules or a court order. **Unless the order says otherwise, it operates as an adjudication on the merits** (which matters for claim preclusion), **except** dismissals for **lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, or failure to join a Rule 19 party**.

**Rule 41(d):** If a plaintiff who previously dismissed refiles the same claim, the court may order the plaintiff to pay the **costs** of the prior action.

# UNIT V — Trial (Classes 15–16 Part 1)

## Class 15 (9/29) — Trial by Jury

### Document: U.S. Const. Amend. VII; Rules 38 and 39 (CB 474–77) — ⚠️ the Amendment text isn't in exam packet
**Rule: Seventh Amendment.** "In Suits at **common law**, where the value in controversy shall exceed **twenty dollars**, the right of trial by jury shall be **preserved**…"
- "**Common law**" means **law, not equity** (as of 1791 in England). Equity had **no jury**.
- "**Preserved**" means it keeps the 1791 right rather than creating new ones. That's why we use **historical analogy** for modern claims.
- The Amendment applies only in **federal** court. It is **not incorporated** against the states.

**Rule 38(a):** The jury right under the 7th Amendment or a federal statute "is preserved to the parties **inviolate**."

**Rule 38(b): Demand.** Serve a **written demand** no later than **14 days after the last pleading** directed to the issue is served. It may be included in a pleading.

**Rule 38(c):** A demand may **specify issues**. Otherwise it covers **all** jury-triable issues.

**Rule 38(d): Waiver.** **Failing to properly serve and file a demand waives the jury right.** A demand can be withdrawn only with **all parties' consent**.

**Rule 39(a):** When a jury is demanded, the case is tried to a jury **unless** the parties stipulate to a bench trial, or the court finds there's **no federal right** to a jury on some issues.

**Rule 39(b):** Issues with no proper demand are tried by the court, but the court **may, on motion, order a jury** anyway.

**Rule 39(c):** In a non-jury case the court may use an **advisory jury**, or, with the parties' consent, a jury whose verdict is binding.

### Document: *Curtis v. Loether* (CB 480–84)
**Case: Curtis v. Loether** (U.S. 1974)
- A Black woman sued white landlords under the **Fair Housing Act (Title VIII, § 812)** for refusing to rent to her, seeking **actual and punitive damages**. The **defendants** demanded a jury. The plaintiff resisted, fearing local juror prejudice.
- **Holding: Jury right exists.**
  - (1) The 7th Amendment applies to **statutory** causes of action and causes that **didn't exist in 1791**.
  - (2) A § 812 damages action "**sounds basically in tort**": the statute creates a legal duty and compensates for breach.
  - (3) **Damages** are "the traditional form of relief offered in the courts of law."
- **NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin** (no jury in **administrative** proceedings) doesn't apply when Congress lets parties sue in **ordinary federal court**.
- **Title VII backpay is distinguished.** Congress **labeled it "equitable"** and made it **discretionary and restitutionary**, so there is no jury.
- **Policy** concerns (delay, juror bias against civil-rights plaintiffs) can't override the 7th Amendment's command.
- Either party can demand the jury. **The jury right belongs to both sides.**

### Document: *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local 391 v. Terry* (CB 484–91)
**Rule: The two-step test** (from Tull v. United States):
1. **Historical analog:** Compare the statutory action to **18th-century actions in English courts before the merger of law and equity**.
2. **Remedy:** Is the remedy sought **legal or equitable** in nature? **This step is more important.**

**Case: Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry** (U.S. 1990)
- Laid-off truck drivers sued their **union** for breaching its **duty of fair representation** (DFR) in handling their grievances, seeking **backpay** (compensatory damages).
- **Step 1 (history):** The suit requires proving both (a) the **employer's breach of the CBA**, which is **contract-like and legal**, and (b) the **union's DFR breach**, which is **trust/fiduciary-like and equitable**. That leaves the analogy **in equipoise**.
  - The **attorney malpractice** analogy was rejected because employees can't fire the union.
  - The **arbitration vacatur** analogy was rejected because no arbitrator ever decided the DFR claim.
  - Per **Ross v. Bernhard**, look at the **nature of each issue**, not the whole suit.
- **Step 2 (remedy):** **Money damages are the classic legal remedy.** The two exceptions don't apply:
  - **Restitution or disgorgement:** The backpay isn't money **wrongfully held by the union**. It's wages the employer would have paid.
  - **Incidental to or intertwined with injunctive relief:** No injunction was sought.
- **Title VII backpay is distinguished** (labeled equitable by Congress, and restitutionary when sought from the employer).
- **Holding: Jury right exists.**
- **Brennan (concurring):** Drop the historical step and look only at the remedy. **Kennedy (dissent):** The trust analogy should control.

**Exam framework (PM2 Q9, PM3 Q6):**
1. Is the claim **new or statutory**? That's fine (Curtis). Congress can't strip the jury right from a legal claim tried in federal court.
2. **Find the closest 1791 analog.** Tort and contract are legal. Trust, fiduciary, and injunction claims are equitable.
3. **Remedy (decisive):** Money damages are legal **unless** they're restitutionary (disgorging the defendant's ill-gotten gains) or incidental to an injunction.
4. Conclude.

**Exam calls:**
- **PM2 Q9:** A new Medicare intentional-tort statute with **money damages** has a **tort analog** and a **legal remedy**, so there's a **jury right** (answer A).
- **PM3 Q6:** A disgorgement-of-profits claim calls for this **two-step analysis** (answer C). Disgorgement leans equitable, but you must run the test.

### Document: *Colgrove v. Battin* (CB 511–18)
**Case: Colgrove v. Battin** (U.S. 1973)
- A Montana federal **local rule** set **6-person** civil juries. **Holding:** A six-person civil jury **satisfies the 7th Amendment**.
- The 7th Amendment's reference to "common law" defines the **kind of case** that gets a jury. It does **not** freeze every **incident** of the 1791 jury, such as **12 members**.
- **Functional test:** Studies showed **no meaningful difference** in reliability or deliberation between 6 and 12 jurors. Size isn't "substantive."
- **Dissents:** History and tradition say 12. Smaller juries are less representative.
- **PM2 Q2:** A 6-person jury is constitutional. Answer A ("Constitution requires 12") is **wrong**.

### Document: Rule 48 (CB 521–22)
**Rule 48(a):** A jury must have **at least 6 and no more than 12** members. **Each juror must participate** in the verdict unless excused under **Rule 47(c)**.

**Rule 48(b):** **Unless the parties stipulate otherwise**, the verdict must be **unanimous** and returned by a jury of **at least 6**.

**Rule 48(c):** **Polling.** Jurors must be polled individually on a party's request or the court's own motion. If the poll shows a lack of unanimity or assent, the court may direct further deliberation or order a new trial.

- **PM2 Q2:** 9 empaneled, 3 excused, 6 deliberate, verdict 5–1. Six is enough, but with **no stipulation, the verdict must be unanimous**, so the **defendant wins** that argument (answer B).
- The answer key says "**unlike criminal juries, civil juries do not need to be unanimous**" is **wrong** under the FRCP.

### Document: Rule 47 and 28 U.S.C. § 1870 (CB 536–37) — ⚠️ § 1870 isn't in exam packet
**Rule 47(a): Voir dire.** The court may examine prospective jurors itself or let the parties do it. If the court does it, it must allow the parties to supplement with further questions.

**Rule 47(b):** **Peremptory challenges** are governed by **28 U.S.C. § 1870**.

**Rule 47(c):** During trial or deliberation, the court may **excuse a juror for good cause**.

**28 U.S.C. § 1870:**
- Each party gets **3 peremptory challenges**. Several defendants or plaintiffs **may be treated as a single party**, or the court may allow additional challenges.
- **Challenges for cause** are **unlimited** and decided by the **court**.
- **Peremptory challenges** traditionally need no reason. **Limit:** they can't be used to discriminate by **race** (Batson, extended to **civil** cases in Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete) or **sex** (J.E.B.).
- **For-cause challenges** require a showing of bias (e.g., the juror knows a party or has prejudged the case).

## Class 16, Part 1 (9/30) — Taking the Case from the Jury

### Document: Rule 50 (CB 627–28)
**Rule 50(a): Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL; formerly "directed verdict").**
- **Timing:** **After a party has been fully heard** on an issue and **before the case is submitted to the jury**.
- **Standard:** A **reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis** to find for the party on that issue. This is the **same standard as SJ** (Anderson): view the evidence most favorably to the nonmovant, with **no credibility determinations**.
- **Form:** The motion must specify the judgment sought and the law and facts that entitle the movant to it.

**Rule 50(b): Renewed JMOL (formerly "JNOV").**
- **Requires a prior 50(a) motion.** It is a *renewal*, so if you didn't move under 50(a) before submission, you **can't** move under 50(b).
- **Timing:** File **within 28 days after entry of judgment**. It may be joined with an alternative **Rule 59 new-trial** request.
- **The court may:** (1) allow judgment on the verdict, (2) order a new trial, or (3) direct entry of JMOL.

**Rule 50(c):** If the court **grants** renewed JMOL, it must also **conditionally rule** on the new-trial motion (stating its grounds), so an appellate court knows what happens if it reverses.

**Rule 50(d):** The party against whom JMOL was entered may move for a new trial **within 28 days**.

**Rule 50(e):** If the court **denies** JMOL, the winner (the **appellee**) may argue on appeal for a **new trial** as a fallback if the appellate court reverses. The appellate court may then order a new trial, remand, or **direct entry of judgment** (Neely).

- **Seventh Amendment:** A **pre-verdict** JMOL doesn't violate the jury right (**Galloway**).

### Document: Rule 59 (CB 630)
**Rule 59(a)(1)(A): New trial after a jury trial**, "for any reason for which a new trial has heretofore been granted in an action at law in federal court." Examples:
- verdict against the **clear (great) weight of the evidence**;
- **excessive or inadequate damages** (→ **remittitur**, with a new trial if the plaintiff refuses the reduction);
- prejudicial errors in **instructions or evidence**;
- misconduct by jurors or attorneys;
- newly discovered evidence.

**Rule 59(a)(1)(B):** After a **bench** trial, for any reason a rehearing has traditionally been granted in equity.

**Rule 59(b):** Motion due **within 28 days after entry of judgment**. **59(d):** The court may order a new trial **on its own** within 28 days.

**Rule 59(e):** Motion to **alter or amend a judgment**, due **within 28 days**.

**Rule 50 vs. Rule 59:**
- **Rule 50 JMOL** **ends** the case for the movant. Its standard is "**no reasonable jury**," and the judge may **not** weigh credibility.
- **Rule 59 new trial** is **less drastic**: a new jury decides. So the judge **may weigh the evidence** and grant a new trial if the verdict is against the **great weight of the evidence** or a **miscarriage of justice**, **even if** some evidence supports it.

### Document: Rule 61 (CB 634)
**Rule 61: Harmless error.** No error in admitting or excluding evidence, or any other error, justifies a new trial or disturbing a judgment **unless it affects a party's substantial rights** (unless justice requires otherwise). Courts **must disregard** harmless errors **at every stage**.
- Exam use: Pair Rule 61 with Rule 59 and with appeals. Ask whether the error **likely affected the outcome**.

### Document: *Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.* (CB 637–42)
**Case: Neely v. Martin K. Eby Constr. Co.** (U.S. 1967)
- A worker fell to his death from a missile-silo scaffold. The **jury awarded $25,000** to his daughter. The trial court **denied** the defendant's JNOV motion (Eby had moved for a directed verdict at the close of evidence, preserving the issue). The **10th Circuit reversed** for insufficient evidence and **directed dismissal**.
- **Holding: The appellate court may itself direct entry of judgment for the defendant.**
  - (1) **No Seventh Amendment problem.** An appellate JNOV intrudes on the jury no more than a trial court's JNOV does.
  - (2) **Rule 50(e)** is permissive. It preserves the appellee's chance to argue for a **new trial**, but doesn't require a remand.
- **Practical guidance:**
  - If reversal rests on a **dispositive legal issue**, direct judgment.
  - If it rests on **insufficient evidence**, consider whether the gap is **curable** (e.g., evidence was wrongly excluded). The appellee should raise new-trial grounds in the **trial court, the appellate brief, or a petition for rehearing**.
- Here Neely raised **no** new-trial grounds below and told the 10th Circuit the trial was "fairly tried." Her excluded-evidence argument came **too late** (first raised in the Supreme Court). **Affirmed.**
- **Exam:** **Preserve** your issues. Movants must make a **50(a) motion before submission** to later renew under 50(b). Verdict-winners must **raise new-trial grounds** as a fallback.

# UNIT VI — Finality & Appeal (Classes 16 Part 2–17)

**Roadmap: Can the court of appeals hear this appeal NOW?** Work through the routes in order:
1. **§ 1291 final decision?** Does it end the litigation on the merits, leaving nothing but execution? If so, it is appealable.
2. **Rule 54(b)?** Multiple claims or parties, one or more **fully** resolved, and the district court **expressly finds "no just reason for delay."**
3. **§ 1292(a)(1)?** An order **granting, refusing, modifying, or dissolving an injunction** is appealable as of right.
4. **§ 1292(b)?** The district judge **certifies** the question **and** the court of appeals agrees to hear it.
5. **Collateral order doctrine (Cohen)?** The order is conclusive, important and separate from the merits, and effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Judge it **by category**.
6. **Mandamus?** Only for clear abuse of discretion or usurpation of power.
7. **Contempt?** Defy the order, be held in contempt, then appeal the contempt (Microsoft).

## Class 16, Part 2 (9/30) — Finality

### Document: 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (CB 669)
**Rule § 1291:** Courts of appeals have jurisdiction over appeals from **all final decisions** of the district courts.

**Rule: Catlin v. United States.** A final decision "**ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment**."
- Nuances: It must end the litigation **on the merits**. **Collateral** matters like attorney's fees can remain pending. "Nothing left" means nothing **but execution**.

**Policies behind the final judgment rule:**
- avoid **piecemeal** appeals;
- **efficiency**, since many errors become moot or harmless by the end;
- **deference** to trial judges;
- prevent **harassment by repeated appeals**;
- one appeal per case, after everything is decided.

**Not final** (just steps toward a judgment):
- **denials** of 12(b) motions;
- **denials** of SJ (see Wininger and Scott for exceptions);
- **partial SJ on liability alone**;
- **discovery orders**;
- an order to **refile** a complaint.

### Document: Rule 54(b) (CB 676)
**Rule 54(b): Partial final judgment.** In a case with **multiple claims or multiple parties**, the court **may direct entry of a final judgment** as to **one or more, but fewer than all**, claims or parties **only if** it **expressly determines that there is no just reason for delay**.
- Otherwise, an order resolving fewer than all claims or parties **doesn't end the action** and **may be revised** any time before final judgment.
- **It requires that a claim be completely resolved.** It does **not** apply to a **single claim** with multiple remedies (Wetzel).

### Document: 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)–(b) (CB 677)
**Rule § 1292(a)(1):** Interlocutory orders "**granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions**, or refusing to dissolve or modify injunctions" are **appealable as of right**. This links to Winter (a preliminary injunction) and Rule 65.

**Rule § 1292(b): Certified interlocutory appeal.** It takes **two keys**:
- **(1) The district judge certifies in writing** that the order involves:
  - (a) a **controlling question of law**,
  - (b) with **substantial ground for difference of opinion**,
  - (c) where an immediate appeal **may materially advance the ultimate termination** of the litigation.
- **(2) The court of appeals, in its discretion, permits** the appeal. The application must be made **within 10 days** of the order.
- Filing does **not stay** proceedings unless ordered.
- **In re Text Messaging** came up this way.

### Document: *Liberty Mutual Insurance Co. v. Wetzel* (CB 678–82)
**Case: Liberty Mutual Ins. Co. v. Wetzel** (U.S. 1976)
- Female employees sued under **Title VII** over pregnancy-related policies, seeking an **injunction, backpay, damages, and fees**. The district court granted **partial SJ on liability only**, withheld the injunction, and **certified it under Rule 54(b)**. The 3d Circuit heard the appeal. **The Supreme Court raised jurisdiction on its own** and held there was **no appellate jurisdiction**.
- **Not § 1291:** Partial SJ on liability, with **remedies unresolved**, is interlocutory.
- **Not Rule 54(b):** It was a **single claim** (one legal theory, one set of facts) with **multiple requested remedies** under Rule 8(a)(3). Rule 54(b) **doesn't apply to single-claim actions**, so the district court's certification was **ineffective**.
- **Not § 1292(a)(1):** No injunction was actually granted or denied. Even a *de facto* denial would have let only the **plaintiffs** appeal, not Liberty Mutual.
- **Not § 1292(b):** The district court **didn't certify** under 1292(b), and the 10-day petition to the court of appeals never happened. A 54(b) certification **can't substitute** for 1292(b).
- **Exam:** **Appellate jurisdiction can't be created by the parties' consent or by a district court's say-so.** Courts check it **on their own** (sua sponte).

## Class 17 (10/1) — Collateral Order Doctrine

### Document: *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.* (CB 685–87)
**Rule: The collateral order doctrine.** A "small class" of orders that **don't end the case** still count as "**final decisions**" under § 1291 if they:
1. **conclusively determine** the disputed question;
2. resolve an **important issue completely separate from the merits** ("too important to be denied review and too independent of the cause itself"); and
3. are **effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment**.

**Case: Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.** (U.S. 1949)
- A small shareholder brought a **derivative suit** alleging $100M+ of insider looting. A **new New Jersey statute** required small-stake derivative plaintiffs to **post security** (a bond for the defendants' expenses, here **$125,000**). The district court **refused to apply** it. The defendants appealed immediately.
- **Holding: Appealable.**
  - The order was **conclusive**, not tentative.
  - **Whether security is required** is **separate** from whether insiders looted the company.
  - It **can't wait**: by the end of the case, the protection the security provides (against the expenses of litigating) would be **lost**.
- **Caveat:** Not **every** security order is appealable. A **discretionary** ruling on the **amount** (which can be revisited) is not conclusive. Here the **right** to security was a serious, unsettled legal question.
- On the merits, the Court held the NJ statute applies in federal diversity suits (an Erie issue).

### Document: *Mohawk Industries v. Carpenter* (CB 688–98)
**Case: Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter** (U.S. 2009, Sotomayor)
- An employee claimed he was fired for reporting that Mohawk employed undocumented workers. The district court found Mohawk **waived attorney-client privilege** and ordered it to disclose communications about the employee's meeting with counsel. Mohawk sought a collateral-order appeal.
- **Holding: Disclosure orders adverse to the attorney-client privilege are NOT immediately appealable collateral orders.**
- **Prongs 1 (conclusive) and 2 (separate from the merits) were satisfied.** It **failed prong 3** (effectively unreviewable).
- **Apply Cohen CATEGORICALLY, to the whole class of orders**, not to the individual case. The question is "whether deferring review until final judgment so imperils the interest as to justify the **cost of allowing immediate appeal of the entire class** of relevant orders." Importance "**in the abstract**" isn't enough.
- **Why post-judgment review is adequate:**
  - The appellate court can **vacate and order a new trial** without the privileged material (as with any evidentiary error).
  - Clients and lawyers won't really be chilled ex ante by the remote chance of an erroneous ruling.
  - Most privilege rulings are routine.
- **Other safety valves exist** for particularly injurious or novel rulings:
  - (1) **§ 1292(b)** certification;
  - (2) **mandamus**;
  - (3) **defy the order** and accept **Rule 37(b) sanctions** or **contempt**, then appeal;
  - (4) **protective orders** to limit spillover.
- The collateral order doctrine should stay "**narrow**." It must "**never be allowed to swallow the general rule**." Expansion should come through **rulemaking** (28 U.S.C. §§ 2072(c), 1292(e)), not case law.
- **Thomas (concurring):** Stop expanding the doctrine judicially. Leave it all to rulemaking.

### Document: *Microsoft I and Microsoft II* (CB 701–04)
**Case: United States v. Microsoft Corp. (Microsoft I)** (S.D.N.Y. 2014, Preska)
- A **Stored Communications Act warrant** sought a user's emails stored in **Dublin, Ireland**. Microsoft's motion to vacate was **denied**, and it filed a notice of appeal.
- **Holding:** An order denying a motion to quash or vacate a subpoena or warrant is **not final**. The established route to review is: **defy, be held in contempt, and appeal the contempt order** (which **is** final).

**Case: United States v. Microsoft Corp. (Microsoft II)** (S.D.N.Y. 2014)
- The parties **stipulated** that Microsoft wouldn't comply, and the court entered a **contempt order with no sanctions**, purely to create a final order for appeal.
- This shows **contempt is a genuine, available route** to immediate review. That **supports Mohawk's conclusion** that discovery orders are reviewable through other means, which weighs **against** collateral-order status.
- Its "empty formality" quality is the discussion point. Contempt has real costs for **individuals** (risk, reputation) and fewer for a big company or the **government**.

**Exam application (PM1 essay; PM2 Q7):**
- **The model IRAC structure:**
  - **Issue:** Is the order appealable now?
  - **Rule:** § 1291 plus the Cohen 3 prongs. Assess the **category** of order (Mohawk).
  - **Apply each prong:**
    - **(1) Conclusive?** Has the court finally decided, with no sign of reconsidering?
    - **(2) Important and separate?** Can the appeals court decide it **without touching the merits**? Is the interest weighty (e.g., national security, privacy, anonymity)?
    - **(3) Effectively unreviewable?** Is this a "**can't un-ring the bell**" situation? Once confidential info is public or a name is in the record, a later appeal can't fix it.
  - **Counter-argument from Mohawk:** Keep the doctrine narrow. Discovery orders generally aren't collateral orders. Alternatives exist (§ 1292(b), mandamus, contempt per Microsoft).
  - **Conclude.** Either answer can score well if well argued.
- **PM1 essay (motion to seal Navy sonar documents denied):** The model answer says **appealable**. Release can't be undone, the order is separate from the merits, and national security is important (Winter deference). The alternative answer leans on Mohawk plus the contempt route.
- **PM2 Q7 (motion to proceed anonymously denied):** **Collateral order** (answer E). It is conclusive, separate from the intentional-tort merits, and **unreviewable later** because once the name is public, "you can't un-ring that bell."

# UNIT VII — The Effects of Judgment: Preclusion & Joinder (Classes 18–20)

**Two doctrines under the umbrella of "res judicata":**

| | **Claim preclusion** ("res judicata," "bar," "merger") | **Issue preclusion** ("collateral estoppel") |
|---|---|---|
| Bars | The **whole claim**, including everything that **was or could have been** raised | Only **specific issues actually litigated and necessarily decided** |
| Second suit | The **same claim** | Can be a **different claim** |
| Who's bound | Parties and **privies** | Traditionally parties and privies. Mutuality is now abandoned (Bernhard, Parklane) for **who may invoke** it, but **due process still limits who can be bound** (Hansberry, Taylor). |
| Raised as | An **affirmative defense** (8(c)). The **defendant bears the burden** (Taylor). | Same |

## Class 18 (10/6) — Preclusion

### Document: Res Judicata (CB 735–38)
**Rule: Claim preclusion** (Petro-Hunt elements; matches your lecture notes):
1. The **parties are identical or in privity**;
2. the prior judgment was rendered by a **court of competent jurisdiction**;
3. the prior action ended in a **final judgment on the merits**; and
4. the **same claim or cause of action** is involved in both suits.

It bars a losing plaintiff's **second shot** and a losing defendant's **new defenses after the fact**. It covers what **could have been raised**.

**"Final judgment on the merits":**
- **Counts:** A winner and loser were declared and the dispute ended. This **includes SJ on statute of limitations** (no facts tried, but still "on the merits") and **Rule 41(b)** involuntary dismissals.
- **Doesn't count:** dismissal **without prejudice**, or dismissal for lack of **jurisdiction, venue, or Rule 19 joinder** (41(b) exceptions).

**"Same claim":**
- The **modern transactional test**: the same **"nucleus of operative facts"** or "single group of operative facts," **regardless of legal theory or remedy**. Look at relation in **time, space, origin, and motivation**, whether the claims form a **convenient trial unit**, and the parties' **expectations**.
- **Older** tests: the "same evidence" test and the "same primary right" test.

**Privity:** The nonparty holds an interest in the subject matter that a party held. Example: an **assignee** of a contract claim.

**Rule: Issue preclusion** (Georgia-Pacific elements):
1. The **precise issue** was raised and **actually litigated** in the prior proceeding;
2. its determination was **necessary to the outcome**;
3. the prior proceeding resulted in a **final judgment on the merits**; and
4. the party **against whom** preclusion is sought had a **full and fair opportunity to litigate**.

- Here "final judgment on the merits" refers to **the issue**. A finally decided **jurisdictional or procedural** ruling can be issue-preclusive.

**Remedy for a wrong judgment:** **Appeal it.** Don't bring a new suit (lecture notes).

### Document: *Rose v. Board of Election Commissioners* (CB 738–42)
**Case: Rose v. Board of Election Comm'rs for City of Chicago** (7th Cir. 2016). **The baseline claim-preclusion case.**
- Rose tried to run for Chicago alderman, but his nominating petitions were challenged and he was kept off the ballot. He sued in **Illinois state court** and lost. He did **not appeal**. He then sued in **federal court** (adding a **§ 1983** constitutional claim) against the same defendants.
- **Holding: Barred by claim preclusion.**
  - **28 U.S.C. § 1738:** Federal courts give a **state-court judgment the same preclusive effect it would get in that state's courts**, so Illinois preclusion law applies.
  - **Same parties, a final judgment on the merits** (under Illinois Rule 273, an involuntary dismissal counts unless it was for jurisdiction, venue, or joinder), and the **same cause of action** under the "**single group of operative facts**" test, **regardless of the new legal theory**.
  - The **§ 1983 claim could have been raised** in state court, since **state courts are presumptively competent** over federal claims. So it's barred.
  - **Full and fair opportunity:** The earlier proceeding met due process minimums. **Choosing not to appeal doesn't make the opportunity illusory.**
  - "Fairness" arguments don't override settled preclusion doctrine.

### Document: *Rush v. City of Maple Heights* (CB 744–51)
**Case: Rush v. City of Maple Heights** (Ohio 1958)
- Rush fell from a motorcycle because of a **pothole**. **Suit 1** (municipal court) sought **property damage** to the motorcycle; she **won** ($100). **Suit 2** sought **personal injuries**. She used **issue preclusion** on the city's negligence from Suit 1 and won $12,000.
- **Holding: Suit 2 is BARRED.** "Where a person suffers both personal injuries and property damage as a result of the same wrongful act, **only a single cause of action** arises." The different injuries are just **separate items of damage**.
- **Rule against claim splitting** (the majority rule): One tort supports one action, so **all damages must be sought in one suit**.
- **Overruled Vasu v. Kohlers**' paragraph 4, which allowed separate suits. That was **dicta**: Vasu actually turned on lack of **privity** between an insured and his insurer.
- It rejected **Brunsden v. Humphrey**'s "same evidence" test, adopting **Lord Coleridge's dissent**: one act, one moment, one harm. It is "a subtlety not warranted by law" that **torn clothes and torn limbs** make two suits.
- **Policy:** Prevent **vexatious litigation**. Otherwise a plaintiff could bring a **small** claim first (cheaper for the defendant to lose than to defend), **win**, and then use issue preclusion for a **big** second claim (from your lecture notes).
- **Dissent:** **Stability.** People relied on Vasu, so apply the new rule prospectively only.
- **Ironic twist:** Rush argued **issue** preclusion (offensively). The city won on **claim** preclusion. You must assert the **whole claim** the first time.

### Document: *Cromwell v. County of Sac* (CB 754–57)
**Case: Cromwell v. County of Sac** (U.S. 1876). **The foundational statement of the two branches.**
- A county issued **courthouse bonds** that were tainted by fraud (the courthouse was never built). In **Suit 1** (Smith, as a front for Cromwell), suing on **25 coupons**, the county won because the holder **didn't prove he paid value** before maturity. In **Suit 2**, Cromwell sued on **different bonds and coupons** from the same issue and offered to prove he **did** pay value.
- **Holding:**
  - **(1) Same claim or demand:** The judgment is an "**absolute bar**," covering everything **offered and everything that might have been offered** (claim preclusion).
  - **(2) Different claim or demand** (as here, **different bonds and coupons**): The judgment operates only as an **estoppel** on matters "**actually litigated and determined**," **not** on what "might have been" litigated (issue preclusion).
- **Applied:**
  - Cromwell **is estopped** from denying the bonds are **void against the county** except in the hands of a **bona fide holder for value** (that was decided).
  - He is **not** estopped from proving he **paid value for these different bonds**. Failing to prove value for one coupon is "not even presumptive" evidence about another.
- **Why limit issue preclusion?** Parties may skip issues for **non-merits reasons**: a **small amount at stake**, **difficulty getting evidence**, **expense**, or their situation at the time.
- **Default judgments** don't support **issue** preclusion on a different claim, because nothing was actually litigated.
- **Privity:** Cromwell was bound by Smith's suit as the **real party in interest** (it was prosecuted for his "sole use and benefit").
- **Exam:** First ask **same claim or different claim?** That tells you which doctrine applies. Then identify **exactly what was decided**.

## Class 19 (10/7) — Preclusion; Joinder

**Mutuality** (the old rule): Only someone who would have been **bound** by the first judgment could **use** it. Modern law abandons it.
- **Defensive nonmutual:** A **defendant** uses a prior judgment against a **plaintiff** who lost the issue to someone else (Bernhard; Blonder-Tongue).
- **Offensive nonmutual:** A **new plaintiff** uses a prior judgment against a **defendant** who lost the issue to someone else (Parklane, at the court's discretion).
- **Never** bind someone who **wasn't a party or privy** (due process: Hansberry, Taylor).

### Document: *Bernhard v. Bank of America* (CB 759–63)
**Case: Bernhard v. Bank of America** (Cal. 1942, Traynor, J.)
- Elderly Mrs. Sather's money was moved to an account controlled by **Cook**, who withdrew it. In the **probate accounting**, Bernhard (a beneficiary) objected, but the probate court found Sather had **made a gift** to Cook. Later Bernhard, now **administratrix** of the estate, sued **Bank of America** for paying out the money. The bank pleaded the probate finding.
- **Holding: The bank (a stranger to the probate case) may use issue preclusion against Bernhard.** **Mutuality is abolished.**
- **Three questions:**
  1. Was the **issue identical**? Yes: ownership of the money.
  2. Was there a **final judgment on the merits**? Yes: the probate order.
  3. Was the **party AGAINST whom the plea is asserted** a party or in privity to the prior case? Yes. Bernhard was effectively a party; she litigated the **same right** in a **different capacity** (beneficiary vs. administratrix), representing the same interests.
- **Due process** limits **who can be BOUND**, **not who can ASSERT** preclusion. "No satisfactory rationalization" supports mutuality.
- This is **defensive nonmutual** issue preclusion.

### Document: *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore* (CB 768–73)
**Case: Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore** (U.S. 1979)
- The **SEC** sued Parklane (an equitable action with **no jury**) and won a finding that its **proxy statement was materially false and misleading**. Shareholder **Shore** then brought a **class action for damages** and moved for partial SJ, using that finding **offensively**.
- **Holding:** Federal courts **may allow offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel**. Trial courts have **broad discretion**.
- **General rule:** **Don't allow it** where (1) **the plaintiff could easily have joined** the earlier action, or (2) it would be **unfair to the defendant**.
- **Unfairness factors:**
  - **(a)** Small or nominal stakes in suit 1, so **little incentive to litigate vigorously**, especially if future suits weren't **foreseeable**;
  - **(b)** The judgment relied on is **inconsistent with earlier judgments** favoring the defendant (the "50th plaintiff" problem);
  - **(c)** The second action offers **procedural opportunities unavailable in the first** that **could readily cause a different result**.
- **Why offensive use is different:** Defensive use encourages joining all defendants up front. Offensive use encourages potential plaintiffs to "**wait and see**" ("everything to gain, nothing to lose"), which can **increase** litigation.
- **Applied:**
  - Shore **couldn't easily have joined** the SEC suit.
  - The stakes were serious and follow-on suits **foreseeable**, so Parklane had every incentive to fight.
  - There were **no inconsistent judgments**.
  - The only new procedural feature was a **jury**, which isn't likely to cause a different result.
  - So **estoppel applies**.
- **Seventh Amendment:** Precluding relitigation of facts found in a prior **equitable nonjury** proceeding **doesn't violate** the jury right. Common-law preclusion doctrine has evolved since 1791. Connects to Unit V.
- **Rehnquist (dissent):** It denies a jury and coerces defendants.

**Exam framework for nonmutual issue preclusion:**
1. Run the **four issue-preclusion elements**.
2. **Who is invoking it**, against whom? Is it **offensive or defensive**?
3. **Was the party being bound a party** to suit 1? If not, stop. **Due process forbids it** (Taylor's six exceptions aside).
4. **If offensive, apply the Parklane factors**: could the plaintiff easily have joined, the stakes and foreseeability, inconsistent judgments, new procedural opportunities.

### Document: Introductory Notes on Joinder; Rules 13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, and 24 (CB 712–21)
**Joinder decides who's a party, which decides who's bound.**

**Rule 13(a): Compulsory counterclaim.** A pleading **must** state any counterclaim that arises out of the **same transaction or occurrence** as the opposing party's claim and doesn't require adding a party the court can't get jurisdiction over. **If omitted, it's lost**, which is preclusion by rule.

**Rule 13(b): Permissive counterclaim.** **Any other** claim against an opposing party.

**Rule 13(g): Crossclaim** against a **co-party**, arising from the same transaction or occurrence. It is **permissive**.

**Rule 13(h):** Additional parties may be added to a counterclaim or crossclaim under Rules 19 and 20.

**Rule 14: Impleader (third-party practice).**
- **14(a)(1):** A defending party may bring in a nonparty "**who is or may be liable to it for all or part of the claim against it**." The liability must be **derivative**: **indemnity, contribution, subrogation**. You can't implead someone as an "it was him, not me" alternative defendant. **Leave is needed** if it's filed **more than 14 days after serving the original answer**.
- **14(a)(2)–(3):** The third-party defendant may assert defenses and claims. The plaintiff may assert claims against the third-party defendant arising from the same transaction.
- **14(b):** A plaintiff facing a counterclaim may also implead.

**Rule 18(a): Joinder of claims.** A party may join **as many claims as it has** against an opposing party, **related or not**. But **preclusion** (Rush) effectively **forces** joining all transactionally related claims.

**Rule 19: Required parties.**
- **19(a)(1):** A person is required, and **must be joined if feasible** (subject to service and no destruction of subject-matter jurisdiction), if:
  - **(A)** without them the court **can't accord complete relief** among the existing parties; **or**
  - **(B)** they **claim an interest** in the subject, and disposing of the case without them may **(i) impair or impede their ability to protect that interest**, or **(ii)** leave an existing party at **substantial risk of double, multiple, or inconsistent obligations**.
- **19(b):** If joinder **isn't feasible**, decide whether to proceed or dismiss "**in equity and good conscience**," considering:
  1. the **prejudice** to the absent person or existing parties;
  2. whether prejudice can be **lessened** by shaping relief;
  3. whether a judgment without them would be **adequate**;
  4. whether the plaintiff would have an **adequate remedy** if the case were dismissed.
- Failure to join a Rule 19 party is a **12(b)(7)** defense (preserved by **12(h)(2)**).

**Rule 20: Permissive joinder of parties.** Plaintiffs may join together, and defendants may be joined, if:
1. a right to relief is asserted jointly, severally, or in the alternative arising out of the **same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences**; **and**
2. there is **any question of law or fact common** to all.

Under **20(b)** the court may order **separate trials** to prevent prejudice.

**Rule 21:** **Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissing the action.** The court may **add or drop parties** or **sever** claims at any time, on just terms, even on its own.

**Rule 24: Intervention.**
- **24(a) Of right:** On a **timely** motion, if the person has an unconditional statutory right, **or** claims an **interest** relating to the property or transaction, the disposition **may impair or impede** their ability to protect it, **and existing parties don't adequately represent** that interest.
- **24(b) Permissive:** On a timely motion, if the person has a conditional statutory right or a **claim or defense sharing a common question of law or fact**. The court considers **undue delay or prejudice**.
- **24(c):** The motion must be accompanied by a **pleading**.

### Document: *Breaking Glass Pictures v. Does 1–188* (CB 729–32)
**Case: Breaking Glass Pictures, LLC v. Does 1–188** (E.D. Mo. 2013)
- A copyright suit against **188 anonymous BitTorrent users** in **one "swarm"** (over about 9 weeks) who downloaded *6 Degrees of Hell*. The court **sua sponte** considered **misjoinder**.
- **Holding (1):** Joinder is improper under **Rule 20(a)(2)**. Being in the same swarm isn't the **same transaction, occurrence, or series**. BitTorrent doesn't require any Doe to have **contributed to any other Doe's** download, and the activity spanned **9 weeks**. The court adopted the *Hard Drive Productions* view, noting a **circuit split** among district courts.
- **Holding (2), alternative:** **Even if Rule 20(a) were satisfied**, the court may **sever in its discretion** under **Rule 21** to avoid **prejudice and unfairness** and in the interest of justice. With 188 defendants, each could attend every deposition and must serve every other defendant, so discovery and a variety of defenses would be **unwieldy**.
- **Disposition:** All but Doe 1 were **severed and dismissed without prejudice**. The plaintiff can refile individually.
- **Policy subtext:** "Copyright troll" suits use mass joinder to cheaply subpoena ISPs and extract settlements.
- **Exam:** Permissive joinder is a **balance**: **efficiency and consistency** vs. **fairness and manageability**. Rule 20 is construed **liberally**, but courts keep **discretion** under Rules 20(b) and 21.

## Class 20 (10/8) — Preclusion & Representation

### Document: Preclusion and Representation (CB 775)
- **Default rule:** **Joinder defines who's bound.** Binding a **nonparticipant** is the "taboo," the **outer limit** of preclusion.
- **The two frontier questions:**
  - **Taylor v. Sturgell:** When does a judgment bind people who weren't joined, **outside** a class action?
  - **Hansberry v. Lee:** When does a **class action** judgment **fail to bind** people within the defined class?

### Document: *Taylor v. Sturgell* (CB 776–87)
**Rule: General rule (Hansberry).** "One is not bound by a judgment in personam in a litigation in which he is not designated as a party or to which he has not been made a party by service of process." This reflects the "deep-rooted historic tradition that **everyone should have his own day in court**."
- **Federal common law** governs the preclusive effect of **federal** judgments (Semtek).

**Case: Taylor v. Sturgell** (U.S. 2008, Ginsburg, unanimous)
- **Herrick** filed a **FOIA** request for the FAA's technical documents on a vintage **F-45 airplane** and **lost** (trade-secret exemption). His **friend Taylor** (of the same Antique Aircraft Association, represented by the **same lawyer**) then requested the **same documents** and sued in D.C. The D.C. Circuit barred Taylor's suit under "**virtual representation**" (a multi-factor "close enough" test).
- **Holding: "Virtual representation" is REJECTED.**

**The SIX exceptions to the rule against nonparty preclusion (memorize these):**
1. **Agreement:** The nonparty **agreed to be bound** by the determination.
2. **Pre-existing substantive legal relationship:** e.g., preceding and succeeding **owners of property**, **bailee and bailor**, **assignor and assignee**. This is traditional "privity."
3. **Adequate representation** in certain limited circumstances: properly conducted **class actions**, and suits by **trustees, guardians, and other fiduciaries**.
   - **Minimum requirements (Hansberry; Richards):** (a) **interests aligned**, **and** (b) either the party **understood itself to be acting in a representative capacity** or the original court **took care to protect** the nonparty's interests. **Sometimes** (c) **notice** is also required.
4. **Control:** The nonparty **assumed control** over the earlier litigation and had its day in court.
5. **Proxy:** A nonparty relitigates **through a proxy**, i.e., a **designated representative or agent** of the party bound.
6. **Special statutory scheme:** e.g., **bankruptcy** or **probate**, if consistent with due process.

**Why "virtual representation" fails:**
- (1) It conflicts with the **fundamental** rule and the "constrained approach" of **discrete exceptions**.
- (2) It would create "**de facto class actions at will**," bypassing **Rule 23's** protections (notice, adequacy, certification).
- (3) It is unworkable: there is no firm guidance, and it invites costly discovery. "**Crisp rules with sharp corners**" beat "opaque standards."

**No special rule for "public law" (FOIA) cases.**
- FOIA relief goes to the requester.
- **Stare decisis** handles repetitive suits on the same legal question.
- Any limits on repeat suits should come from **Congress**.

**Burden:** Claim preclusion is an **affirmative defense** (8(c)). **The defendant must prove** that Taylor is Herrick's **agent or proxy**. The burden doesn't shift to the plaintiff. Defendants can use discovery to fill information gaps.

**Disposition:** **Remanded** to decide whether Taylor is acting as Herrick's **agent (exception 5)**. A mere close relationship isn't enough.

**Exam:** When someone not in suit 1 is said to be bound, **walk through all six categories**. "Same interests," "friends," or "same lawyer" **alone** fail.

### Document: *Hansberry v. Lee* (CB 789–94)
**Case: Hansberry v. Lee** (U.S. 1940)
- A Chicago **racially restrictive covenant** would take effect only if owners of **95% of the frontage** signed. In the earlier case, **Burke v. Kleiman** (a "class" suit by one owner "for herself and others similarly situated"), the parties **falsely stipulated** that 95% had signed, and the covenant was enforced.
- Later the **Hansberrys** (a Black family; Carl Hansberry was Lorraine Hansberry's father) bought a home there. Neighbors sued to enforce the covenant and argued **Burke was res judicata** on the 95% issue. The Illinois courts agreed, even though they found the stipulation was false.
- **Holding: Due process forbids binding the Hansberrys** (and their seller) by the Burke judgment.
- **General rule:** Nonparties aren't bound. Binding them violates **due process**, and such a judgment isn't entitled to **full faith and credit**.
- **Class-suit exception:** Absent members **can** be bound by a class judgment, but **only if they're adequately represented** by parties with the **same interests**, under a procedure that **fairly ensures protection** of absent parties' interests.
- **Here, conflicting interests:** The covenant gave signers **both rights and obligations**. Those trying to **enforce** it (the Burke plaintiffs) **can't represent** those who want to **resist** it (the Hansberrys' seller). Owners "**free alternatively either to assert rights or to challenge them**" **aren't a single class**.
- **The representation must be assumed in the first suit.** Burke didn't designate a defendant class or seek relief beyond the named parties, and its decree didn't purport to bind others. A later court can't retroactively treat parties as representatives.
- There was a risk of **fraudulent or collusive** sacrifice of absent parties' rights (the false stipulation).
- **Legacy:** This is the **constitutional foundation of adequate representation** in class actions (Rule 23(a)(4)), cited by Taylor as exception #3's minimum requirements. It **previews Rule 23** (the next unit, after the midterm).

## Must-know tests

**Due Process and notice**
- **Mullane:** notice must be **reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances**, to apprise parties, with time to respond. It need not be actual notice.
- **Jones:** If you **know** notice failed, take **additional reasonable steps** if practicable. **Greene:** posting alone fails where notices get torn down.
- **Doehr:** weigh (1) the private interest, (2) the **risk of erroneous deprivation** and the value of safeguards, (3) the interest of the party seeking the remedy plus the government's interest.

**Preliminary relief**
- **Winter PI:** likely success, **likely** irreparable harm, balance of equities, public interest.

**Pleading**
- **Twombly/Iqbal:** (1) disregard conclusions, (2) ask whether the remaining facts **plausibly** show liability, in light of obvious alternative explanations.
- **Rule 8(d):** alternative and **inconsistent** pleading is OK if genuinely uncertain (McCormick).
- **Rule 11(b):** no improper purpose, warranted law, **evidentiary support after reasonable inquiry**, warranted denials.
- **12(h):** (b)(2)–(5) waived if omitted from the first motion or answer. (b)(6) can be raised through trial. SMJ can be raised anytime.
- **Foman (amendment):** undue delay, bad faith, repeated failure to cure, **undue prejudice**, futility.

**Discovery**
- **Rule 26(b)(1):** nonprivileged + relevant + **proportional**. Admissibility isn't required.
- **Work product (Hickman, 26(b)(3)):** prepared in anticipation of litigation by a party or its representative. Fact work product yields to **substantial need + undue hardship**. Opinion work product is protected. **Facts aren't protected.**
- **Upjohn:** employee communications to counsel at superiors' direction, for legal advice, confidential. The control-group test is rejected.
- **Crime-fraud:** probable cause that the communication **furthered** a crime or fraud. The client's purpose controls.

**Summary judgment**
- **Rule 56 / Anderson:** **no genuine dispute of material fact** (could a reasonable jury find for the nonmovant?) + entitled to judgment as a matter of law. View evidence most favorably to the nonmovant. No credibility calls.
- **Celotex:** a movant without the trial burden can just **point to the absence of evidence**. The nonmovant must then show specific facts.
- **Scott vs. Tolan:** a video that **blatantly contradicts** one side ends the dispute. Testimony against testimony goes to the jury.

**Trial**
- **7th Amendment (Terry):** (1) an 18th-century analog, (2) **legal vs. equitable remedy** (more important). Damages are legal unless restitutionary or incidental to an injunction.

**Appeal**
- **Collateral order (Cohen/Mohawk):** **conclusive**; **important and separate from the merits**; **effectively unreviewable** after final judgment. Judged **categorically**. Alternatives: § 1292(b), mandamus, contempt (Microsoft).

**Preclusion and joinder**
- **Claim preclusion:** same parties/privies, competent court, **final judgment on the merits**, **same claim** (transactional nucleus of facts). It bars what was or **could have been** raised.
- **Issue preclusion:** same issue **actually litigated**, **necessarily decided**, final judgment, **full and fair opportunity** for the party bound.
- **Parklane (offensive nonmutual):** discretion. Refuse if the plaintiff **could easily have joined** or it's **unfair** (low stakes or unforeseeable future suits, inconsistent judgments, new procedural opportunities).
- **Taylor's six nonparty exceptions:** agreement, substantive legal relationship, adequate representation, control, proxy, statutory scheme.
- **Rule 19(a):** complete relief / impair the absentee's interest / risk of inconsistent obligations. **19(b):** the equity-and-good-conscience factors.
- **Rule 20:** same transaction, occurrence, or series **+** a common question.
