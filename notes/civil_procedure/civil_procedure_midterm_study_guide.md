# UNIT I — Introduction & Due Process (Classes 1–4)

## Class 1 (8/25) — What Is Civil Procedure?

### Reading: *Anonymous Physician v. Wininger*
**Rule:** Statute of limitations is an **affirmative defense**. Once the defendant shows the claim was filed outside the period, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show tolling or timeliness.

**Case: Anonymous Physician v. Wininger** (Ind. Ct. App. 2013)
- **Facts:**
  - Medical malpractice claim under Indiana's two-year **occurrence-based** statute of limitations.
  - The patient knew by October 2007 that something was wrong (toe angle, pain, wanted a second opinion) but did not file until 2011.
  - The trial court denied the physician's SJ motion, which came up on **interlocutory appeal**.
- **Issue:** Was the malpractice claim time-barred when the patient knew of the symptoms years before filing?
- **Holding:** Yes. The court **reversed the denial of summary judgment**, so the claim is barred.
  - The trigger is awareness of **symptoms** that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate. Actual knowledge of malpractice isn't required.
  - Shows a live procedural posture: denial of SJ → interlocutory appeal. Connects to Unit VI, since denials of SJ are normally *not* final.
- **Takeaway:** An occurrence-based statute of limitations starts running when the plaintiff knows of symptoms that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate, not when the plaintiff learns of the malpractice.

### Reading: Hubbard Introduction (CB 3–6, 45–54)
**Rule: Due Process** (U.S. Const. Amends. V and XIV)
- Process matters independent of outcome. In the "coin flip" hypothetical, the winner still got no due process.
- Civil procedure is the machinery that enforces substantive rights. The distinction between *procedural* and *substantive* law runs through the whole course.

## Class 2 (8/26) — Preliminary Relief; Notice

### Reading: *Winter v. NRDC* (CB 29–44)
**Rule: Preliminary injunction (four-factor test).** The plaintiff must show **(1)** likely success on the merits, **(2)** **likely** irreparable harm without relief, **(3)** the balance of equities tips in their favor, and **(4)** an injunction is in the public interest.

**Case: Winter v. NRDC** (U.S. 2008)
- **Facts:** The Navy's sonar training exercises allegedly harmed marine mammals (NEPA claim). The Ninth Circuit upheld a preliminary injunction using a "**possibility**" of irreparable harm standard.
- **Issue:** Is a "possibility" of irreparable harm enough for a preliminary injunction, and did the equities support one here?
- **Holding:** No. Harm must be **likely**; "possibility" is **too lenient**. The injunction was vacated.
  - A preliminary injunction is an "**extraordinary remedy**" never awarded as of right. All four factors must be shown.
  - The balance of equities and the public interest (**national security / military readiness**) were dispositive. The Court vacated without reaching the merits.
  - Courts **defer to the military on national security**. Useful when arguing an issue is "important" (e.g., the collateral order doctrine).
- **Takeaway:** A preliminary injunction requires a likelihood (not a mere possibility) of irreparable harm, along with likely success on the merits, a favorable balance of equities, and consistency with the public interest.

### Reading: Rule 65 (CB 104–05)
**Rule 65(a): Preliminary injunction.** Requires **notice** to the adverse party. The court may consolidate the hearing with the trial on the merits.

**Rule 65(b): TRO without notice (ex parte)**, only if:
- (A) specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show **immediate and irreparable injury** before the adverse party can be heard, **and**
- (B) the movant's attorney **certifies in writing** the efforts made to give notice and why notice shouldn't be required.

**TRO limits:** It expires in **≤ 14 days** unless extended for good cause. The preliminary injunction hearing is set "at the earliest possible time." The adverse party can move to dissolve on **2 days' notice**. Rule 65(c) requires **security** (a bond).
- **TRO vs. PI:** A TRO is short-term and can be ex parte. A PI requires notice and a hearing. This is the federal-rules echo of Fuentes/Doehr: deprivations without a prior hearing are tightly limited.

### Reading: U.S. Const. Amend. V; Amend. XIV, § 1 (CB 79)
**Rule: Due Process.** No person shall be deprived of **life, liberty, or property** without due process of law.
- The **Fifth Amendment** binds the **federal** government. The **Fourteenth Amendment** binds the **states**. A state-court case means citing the **Fourteenth**.
- Requires (1) **state action**, (2) a **deprivation** of life, liberty, or property, (3) **without** due process. Process means **notice** plus an **opportunity to be heard**.

### Reading: *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank* (CB 119–28)
**Rule: The Mullane standard.** Notice must be "**reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections**." It must also give enough **time** to appear.

**Case: Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust** (U.S. 1950)
- **Facts:** A New York common trust fund gave notice of a judicial accounting to its beneficiaries **by newspaper publication only**.
- **Issue:** Does notice by publication alone satisfy due process for beneficiaries of a common trust fund?
- **Holding:** Not for known beneficiaries with known addresses; publication is **insufficient** for them and at least **ordinary mail** is required. For unknown or hard-to-find beneficiaries, publication is acceptable when nothing better is feasible.
  - "**Process which is a mere gesture is not due process.**" The question is whether this is a means one "**desirous of actually informing**" the absent party would reasonably adopt.
  - **Actual notice is NOT required.** The test is **reasonableness**: compare feasible alternatives and supplements, and weigh cost against the interests at stake.
  - Personal service is the gold standard, but it isn't required. Mail is generally acceptable.
- **Takeaway:** Notice must be reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to inform interested parties, so publication is not enough for anyone whose name and address are known.

## Class 3 (8/27) — Notice

### Reading: *Greene v. Lindsey* (CB 130–35)
**Rule:** A generally acceptable method of notice fails when the sender **knows or should know** it is unreliable in context and a better, feasible alternative exists.

**Case: Greene v. Lindsey** (U.S. 1982)
- **Facts:** Kentucky eviction notices were **posted on apartment doors** in public housing, where notices were **frequently torn down**. The tenants never saw them and default judgments were entered.
- **Issue:** Is posting notice on the door, alone, constitutionally adequate when posted notices are known to be removed?
- **Holding:** No. Posting alone was **not** reasonably calculated to give notice *under these circumstances*. **Mail** was a cheap, efficient **supplement**.
  - Courts ask whether a better, **feasible**, cost-effective alternative existed.
- **Takeaway:** Posting alone is constitutionally inadequate when the government knows it often fails and a cheap supplement like mail is available.

### Reading: *Jones v. Flowers* (CB 136–45)
**Rule: Additional reasonable steps.** When the government **learns its notice attempt failed** (certified mail returned "unclaimed") and **before** taking property, it must take **additional reasonable steps** to notify the owner **if practicable**.

**Case: Jones v. Flowers** (U.S. 2006)
- **Facts:** The state sent notice of a tax sale of Jones's house by certified mail. It came back **unclaimed**. The state then published notice and sold the house.
- **Issue:** When mailed notice is returned unclaimed, must the state do more before taking the property?
- **Holding:** Yes. Once the state knew notice failed, it had to take additional reasonable steps, e.g., resending by **regular mail** (no signature needed), **posting** on the door, or addressing mail to "**occupant**."
  - No duty to search phone books or other records (no "exhaustive search").
  - Core idea: "If the party providing notice reasonably should have known that it failed, it is unreasonable not to try again, if there is another feasible way."
  - Signals that notice may have failed: returned mail, and **no response to a letter asking for acknowledgment** (both sides arguable, since silence might just mean the person doesn't want to settle).
- **Takeaway:** When the government learns that its notice attempt failed, due process requires additional reasonable steps if any are practicable.

### Reading: Rule 4 (CB 154–55)
**Rule 4(a): Summons contents.** Names the court and parties, is directed to the defendant, gives the plaintiff's attorney's name and address, states the time to appear, warns of **default judgment**, and bears the clerk's **signature** and the court's **seal**.

**Rule 4(c): Service.** The plaintiff is responsible for serving the summons **with the complaint**. Any person **at least 18 and not a party** may serve (a 27-year-old second cousin is fine).

**Rule 4(e): Serving an individual** in the U.S.:
- (1) follow **state law** of the forum state or the state where service is made, **or**
- (2)(A) deliver **personally**; (B) leave at the individual's **dwelling or usual place of abode** with someone of **suitable age and discretion who resides there**; or (C) deliver to an **authorized agent**.
- Leaving papers with a **5-year-old** is not "suitable age and discretion," so a **12(b)(5)** motion should be granted. State law (e.g., personal delivery only) is just *one* option under 4(e)(1). It does not override 4(e)(2).

**Rule 4(m): Time limit.** **90 days** after filing the complaint, or the court dismisses without prejudice or orders service. The court must extend the time for **good cause**.

**Rule 4(d): Waiver of service.** The defendant has a duty to avoid unnecessary service costs. Without good cause for refusing, the defendant pays service costs. A defendant who waives gets **60 days** to answer.
- **12(b)(4)** is insufficient **process** (the summons itself is defective). **12(b)(5)** is insufficient **service** of process (delivered the wrong way).

## Class 4 (9/1) — Opportunity to Be Heard

### Reading: *Fuentes v. Shevin* (CB 82–95)
**Rule:** Due process requires notice and an opportunity to be heard **before** the state deprives someone of a significant property interest, at a "meaningful time and in a meaningful manner." The exception is "**extraordinary situations**."

**Case: Fuentes v. Shevin** (U.S. 1972)
- **Facts:** Florida and Pennsylvania **prejudgment replevin** statutes let creditors get a writ (by posting a bond, with no hearing) and have the sheriff seize goods bought on installment (a stove and stereo).
- **Issue:** Do prejudgment replevin statutes that allow seizure without a prior hearing violate due process?
- **Holding:** Yes, unconstitutional.
  - A **temporary** deprivation is still a deprivation. Post-seizure hearings don't cure it.
  - A buyer who hasn't finished paying still has a protected **possessory interest**.
  - The hearing must come **before** the seizure, absent extraordinary circumstances (e.g., an urgent public interest, with the state keeping strict control over the process).
  - Common mistake: treating a post-seizure remedy as enough. Under Fuentes it generally isn't.
- **Takeaway:** Prejudgment seizure of property without notice and a prior hearing, on a creditor's bare say-so, violates due process.

### Reading: *Connecticut v. Doehr* (CB 96–101)
**Rule: Modified Mathews v. Eldridge balancing** for prejudgment remedies between private parties:
1. The **private interest** affected by the prejudgment measure;
2. The **risk of erroneous deprivation** under existing procedures, and the probable value of **additional safeguards**;
3. The **interest of the party seeking the remedy**, plus any **ancillary government interest** in providing the procedure or avoiding added burdens.

**Case: Connecticut v. Doehr** (U.S. 1991)
- **Facts:** DiGiovanni sued Doehr for **assault and battery** and got a **$75,000 attachment on Doehr's home** *ex parte*, based on a conclusory affidavit, with no bond and no exigency.
- **Issue:** Does a statute allowing prejudgment attachment of real estate without notice, hearing, bond, or exigency violate due process?
- **Holding:** Yes.
  - (1) Attachment impairs the ability to sell, mortgage, or refinance, clouds title, and hurts credit. That is a significant interest even without physical dispossession.
  - (2) **High risk of error:** a tort claim turns on disputed facts and can't be shown by documents the way a debt can. A one-sided affidavit can't reliably establish probable cause.
  - (3) The plaintiff had **no pre-existing interest** in the house and showed **no exigency** (no sign Doehr was transferring the property).
  - Doehr is the **framework** (balancing). Fuentes is the **default rule** (a hearing before deprivation).
  - Facts that would change the result: the plaintiff already holds an interest in the property (a mortgage or lien), an exigency (the defendant is about to hide or transfer assets), a **bond**, or a prompt post-deprivation hearing.
- **Takeaway:** Prejudgment attachment is tested under the three-part Mathews balancing (private interest, risk of erroneous deprivation, and the government or plaintiff's interest), and attachment without notice, a hearing, a bond, or exigent circumstances fails.

## Class 5 (9/2) — Early Pleading Regimes

### Reading: Noll, *A Reader's Guide to Pre-Modern Procedure* (CB 162–70)
- **Common law:** A plaintiff needed the right **writ** (form of action) or had no case. Pleadings went back and forth to narrow everything to a single issue. Law and equity were separate courts, and **equity had no jury**.
- **Equity:** More flexible. Used documents and affidavits, offered injunctions and specific performance, and had no jury. The **law/equity line resurfaces in the 7th Amendment analysis** (Unit V).

### Reading: Note on Code Pleading (CB 173–74)
- The **Field Code** (NY 1848) merged law and equity into one "civil action." The complaint had to state the "**facts constituting the cause of action**."
- **Problem:** Courts fought endlessly over **fact vs. legal conclusion vs. evidence**. Twombly and Iqbal revive a version of this distinction (disregard "conclusions").

### Reading: Rules 2, 3, 7, and 8 (CB 175–77)
**Rule 2:** One form of action, the **civil action**.
**Rule 3:** An action is **commenced by filing a complaint** with the court.
**Rule 7(a):** The only **pleadings** allowed are the complaint, the answer (to a complaint, counterclaim, crossclaim, or third-party complaint), and a reply to an answer **only if the court orders one**. **Motions are not pleadings.** This matters for Rule 15(a)(1)(B) and Rule 12(c) ("after the pleadings are closed").

**Rule 8(a): A claim must contain:**
1. a short and plain statement of the grounds for **jurisdiction**;
2. a **short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief**;
3. a **demand for the relief** sought (it may be in the alternative or of different types).

**Rule 8(e):** "Pleadings must be construed so as to do justice."

### Reading: *Dioguardi v. Durning* (CB 182–84)
**Rule:** A complaint isn't dismissed for **lack of detail** or poor form if it gives notice of the claim under Rule 8(a).

**Case: Dioguardi v. Durning** (2d Cir. 1944)
- **Facts:** A pro se immigrant filed an inartful complaint against the customs collector over the handling and sale of his tonic shipment. The district court dismissed it.
- **Issue:** Does a poorly drafted complaint that still suggests a claim satisfy Rule 8(a)?
- **Holding:** Yes, the **2d Circuit reversed**. However poorly written, the complaint gave enough notice of the claim. The defendant's remedy is discovery and summary judgment.
  - The **high-water mark of liberal "notice pleading,"** in contrast to code pleading's technicality.
- **Takeaway:** Under Rule 8 notice pleading, an inartful complaint survives if it gives notice of the claim, and weak claims are left to discovery and summary judgment.

### Reading: *Conley v. Gibson* (CB 186–90)
**Rule: "No set of facts" standard.** A complaint should not be dismissed "unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove **no set of facts** in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief." **Retired by Twombly.**

**Case: Conley v. Gibson** (U.S. 1957)
- **Facts:** Black railway clerks alleged their union failed to represent them fairly because of race: their jobs were "abolished," then refilled with white workers. The lower courts dismissed.
- **Issue:** Did the complaint state a claim when it alleged discrimination in general terms without specific facts?
- **Holding:** Yes, the **Supreme Court reversed**.
  - The Rules require only "**fair notice** of what the claim is and the grounds upon which it rests." Specific facts aren't needed. Discovery and summary judgment do the screening.
  - "Any set of facts could be imagined" is the **wrong** modern standard.
- **Takeaway:** Conley's "no set of facts" standard (later retired by Twombly) read Rule 8 to require only fair notice of the claim and its grounds.

## Class 6 (9/3) — The Twombly and Iqbal Standard

### Reading: *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* (CB 192–205)
**Rule:** A complaint must plead "**enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face**." Factual allegations must "**raise a right to relief above the speculative level**."

**Case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly** (U.S. 2007)
- **Facts:** A putative class action alleged the "Baby Bells" (ILECs) **conspired** (Sherman Act § 1) to block new competitors and not compete with each other. The complaint alleged only **parallel conduct** plus a conclusory "agreement."
- **Issue:** Does an allegation of parallel conduct plus a bare assertion of conspiracy state a § 1 claim?
- **Holding:** No, dismissal was proper.
  - Parallel conduct is "consistent with" conspiracy, but it is "**just as much in line with**" independent, rational business behavior. The complaint needs facts suggesting an **actual agreement** (context, or "plus factors").
  - **"Plausible" is more than "conceivable" or "possible" but less than "probable."** Detailed facts aren't required, but labels, conclusions, and a "formulaic recitation of the elements" won't do.
  - **"No set of facts" is retired.**
  - **Policy:** The **cost of discovery** (especially in antitrust) and the risk of *in terrorem* settlements justify screening at the pleading stage.
- **Takeaway:** A complaint must plead enough facts to make the claim plausible, not merely conceivable, and parallel conduct without more does not plausibly suggest an agreement.

### Reading: *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (CB 207–19)
**Rule: The Iqbal two-step** (applies to **all** civil actions, not just antitrust):
1. **Identify and disregard conclusory allegations**: legal conclusions and "threadbare recitals of the elements" aren't presumed true.
2. **Assume the truth of the remaining well-pleaded factual allegations** and ask whether they **plausibly** suggest entitlement to relief. This is "**context-specific**" and draws on "**judicial experience and common sense**."

**Case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal** (U.S. 2009)
- **Facts:** A Pakistani Muslim detainee held after 9/11 sued AG Ashcroft and FBI Director Mueller (a Bivens action), alleging they adopted a policy of harsh confinement **because of** race, religion, and national origin.
- **Issue:** Did the complaint plead enough facts to plausibly show that Ashcroft and Mueller purposefully discriminated?
- **Holding:** No.
  - **Conclusory, so disregarded:** Ashcroft was the "principal architect," Mueller was "instrumental," and they "knew of, condoned, and willfully and maliciously agreed" to the policy.
  - **Remaining facts** (thousands of Arab Muslim men detained) are explained by the "**obvious alternative explanation**": a legitimate policy targeting people with suspected links to the 9/11 attackers, which would produce a **disparate impact**. That isn't purposeful discrimination, so the claim is **not plausible**.
  - **No supervisory liability by mere knowledge.** Each official is liable only for their **own** misconduct, and purposeful discrimination means acting "**because of**," not "in spite of," the effect.
  - Rule 9(b)'s "intent … may be alleged generally" does **not** permit conclusory allegations of intent.
  - Takeaway: run both steps **explicitly**. Strike the conclusions, then weigh the remaining facts against any obvious alternative explanation.
- **Takeaway:** Plausibility applies to all civil actions: courts disregard legal conclusions, then ask whether the remaining well-pleaded facts plausibly show liability, drawing on judicial experience and common sense.

## Class 7 (9/8) — Pleading after Twombly and Iqbal

### Reading: *Johnson v. City of Shelby* (CB 221–22)
**Rule:** The FRCP **don't require pleading the legal theory or statute**. Twombly and Iqbal concern **factual** allegations, not "punctiliously stated" legal theories.

**Case: Johnson v. City of Shelby** (U.S. 2014)
- **Facts:** Police officers alleged they were fired for exposing an alderman's criminal activity. The lower courts dismissed because the complaint **didn't cite 42 U.S.C. § 1983**.
- **Issue:** Must a complaint cite § 1983 to state a claim for a constitutional violation?
- **Holding:** No, the **Supreme Court reversed**. A complaint that alleges facts plausibly showing a violation survives even if it names the wrong statute or none at all. The fix is to amend, not dismiss.
- **Takeaway:** Rule 8 requires plausible facts, not legal theories, so a complaint is not dismissed for failing to cite the right statute.

### Reading: *In re Text Messaging Antitrust Litigation* (CB 224–27)
**Rule:** **Plausibility ≠ probability.** A complaint needn't exclude innocent explanations; **circumstantial evidence** ("plus factors") is enough at the pleading stage.

**Case: In re Text Messaging Antitrust Litig.** (7th Cir. 2010)
- **Facts:** Four carriers with about 90% of the market allegedly fixed text-messaging prices. The case came up on an **interlocutory appeal under § 1292(b)** (a link to Unit VI).
- **Issue:** Did the complaint plausibly allege a price-fixing conspiracy?
- **Holding:** Yes.
  - **"Plus factors"** beyond parallel conduct: a concentrated market; **simultaneous price increases while costs fell**; a switch to an identical pricing structure; meetings through a **trade association** (an opportunity to conspire).
  - Example: near-simultaneous price hikes, CEO statements about "price stability," an internal "we'll follow their lead" email, and CEOs at the same resort make a plausible claim **even though innocent explanations exist**.
- **Takeaway:** Parallel conduct plus "plus factors" (such as industry structure and conduct against self-interest) can make an antitrust conspiracy plausible under Twombly.

### Reading: *Swanson v. Citibank* (CB 228–38)
**Rule:** Plausibility means the plaintiff "must give enough details about the subject-matter of the case to present a **story that holds together**." It is **context-dependent**: simple claims need less, complex claims (antitrust, high-official misconduct) need more.

**Case: Swanson v. Citibank** (7th Cir. 2010)
- **Facts:** A Black homeowner was denied a home-equity loan after a low appraisal. She alleged race discrimination under the Fair Housing Act, plus fraud claims.
- **Issue:** Did the complaint plausibly allege race discrimination?
- **Holding:** Yes on discrimination. Identifying the **type** of discrimination (race), **who** did it (Citibank), and **when** (Feb. 2009) is enough. Twombly and Iqbal didn't reinstate fact pleading. The **fraud** claims were properly dismissed.
  - **Posner's dissent:** There is an obvious alternative explanation (the 2009 financial crisis, tightened lending), and no comparator allegations (similarly situated white applicants), so it is not plausible.
  - Shows that judges disagree on plausibility, so both sides are arguable.
- **Takeaway:** Twombly and Iqbal did not bring back fact pleading, so a simple claim like discrimination needs only the type, the actor, and the timing, while fraud still requires Rule 9(b) particularity.

## Class 8 (9/9) — Pleading in the Alternative; Rule 11; Heightened Pleading

### Reading: *Beyond Plausibility* (CB 251)
- Other constraints on what goes in a complaint: **pleading in the alternative** (8(d)), **Rule 11** certification, and **heightened pleading** (9(b), PSLRA). Each is a separate check from Rule 8(a)(2) plausibility.

### Reading: Rule 8(d) (CB 252–53)
**Rule 8(d)(1):** Each allegation must be **simple, concise, and direct**. No technical form is required.
**Rule 8(d)(2):** A party may set out **two or more statements of a claim or defense alternatively or hypothetically**. If **any one** of them is sufficient, the pleading is sufficient.
**Rule 8(d)(3):** A party may state as many separate claims or defenses as it has, "**regardless of consistency**."

### Reading: *McCormick v. Kopmann* (CB 254–58)
**Rule:** A pleader **genuinely in doubt** about the facts may plead inconsistent alternatives, and the jury sorts it out. No alternative pleading when the pleader **knows** which version is true (that would be a false statement under Rule 11).

**Case: McCormick v. Kopmann** (Ill. App. 1959)
- **Facts:**
  - McCormick was killed in a collision with Kopmann's truck.
  - His widow sued **Kopmann** (Count I, wrongful death: Kopmann's negligence caused it and the decedent was **free of contributory negligence**) and the **Huls tavern owners** (Count IV, Dram Shop Act: the decedent's **intoxication** caused the crash).
  - Kopmann argued the counts were "repugnant."
- **Issue:** May a plaintiff plead factually inconsistent counts against different defendants?
- **Holding:** Yes, **the court allowed them**. Verdict: Kopmann liable, Huls not.
  - Example: a survivor who can't know what happened may plead "driver ran the red light" **or** "Brakes-R-Us serviced the brakes negligently."
- **Takeaway:** Rule 8(d) allows inconsistent alternative pleading so long as the plaintiff has a good-faith basis for each alternative.

### Reading: Rule 11 (CB 259–61)
**Rule 11(a):** Every pleading, written motion, and other paper must be **signed** by an attorney (or a pro se party).

**Rule 11(b):** By presenting it, the signer certifies, **after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances**, that:
1. it's **not for an improper purpose** (harass, delay, increase cost);
2. the **legal contentions** are warranted by existing law or a **nonfrivolous argument** to extend, modify, or reverse it;
3. the **factual contentions have evidentiary support** (or, if specifically so identified, will likely have it after discovery);
4. **denials** are warranted on the evidence (or reasonably based on belief or lack of information).

**Rule 11(c):** If 11(b) is violated, the court **may** impose an appropriate sanction on the attorney, firm, or party.
- **Safe harbor, 11(c)(2):** A sanctions motion must be made **separately**. It is **served but not filed**, and can be filed only if the offending paper isn't withdrawn or corrected within **21 days**.
- **11(c)(4):** The sanction is limited to what **suffices to deter** repetition. It can be nonmonetary, a penalty paid into court, or the opponent's fees.

**Rule 11(d):** Rule 11 does **not** apply to discovery (Rules 26–37). **Discovery has its own certification in Rule 26(g).**
- A legally baseless complaint calls for a **12(b)(6)** motion, not sanctions "under Rule 37."

### Reading: *Brown v. Ameriprise Financial Services* (CB 262–70)
**Rule:** Rule 11(b)(3) requires a reasonable pre-filing inquiry. "**A plaintiff must adequately plead a claim before obtaining discovery**"; a party can't "assert first and discover later."

**Case: Brown v. Ameriprise Fin. Servs.** (D. Minn. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - A plaintiff (herself an attorney) filed a putative race-discrimination class action whose allegations were **copied nearly verbatim from an old complaint against Coca-Cola**. At her deposition she couldn't support them.
  - Ameriprise **served (didn't file)** a Rule 11 motion, giving her the safe harbor. She refused to withdraw, so it was filed.
- **Issue:** Did filing allegations without a reasonable factual inquiry violate Rule 11, and what sanction fits?
- **Holding:** Yes, a **violation of 11(b)(3)**. The **district judge dismissed the entire complaint** (going further than the magistrate's recommendation to strike the class claims and award fees).
  - "**A shot in the dark is a sanctionable event, even if it somehow hits the mark.**"
  - The **primary purpose of Rule 11 is deterrence, not compensation**, and a fee award wouldn't deter *in terrorem* class claims.
  - **Abandoning the claims doesn't moot sanctions** (Cooter & Gell): the violation is complete when the paper is filed. Courts also have **inherent power** to sanction abuse of the judicial process.
- **Takeaway:** Rule 11(b)(3) requires a reasonable pre-filing inquiry into the factual contentions, and violating it can bring sanctions that reach the whole complaint.

### Reading: Introductory Note on Heightened Pleading (CB 271–72)
**Rule 9(b):** For **fraud or mistake**, the circumstances must be stated **with particularity** (who, what, when, where, how). **Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of mind may be alleged generally**, but per Iqbal, not in conclusory terms.

**PSLRA (securities fraud):** A "super-heightened" standard. The plaintiff must plead with particularity facts giving rise to a **strong inference of scienter**.
- **Hierarchy**: 8(a)(2) plausibility (default) → 9(b) particularity (fraud or mistake) → PSLRA (securities fraud).

## Class 9 (9/10) — Responding to the Complaint; Amendment

### Reading: The Answer (CB 280–81) and Rule 8(b)–(c) (CB 281–82)
**Rule 8(b): Denials.**
- Admit or deny each allegation. A denial must **fairly respond to the substance**. A party can admit in part and deny the rest.
- **8(b)(5):** Lacking knowledge or information sufficient to form a belief **has the effect of a denial**.
- **8(b)(6):** An allegation (other than the amount of damages) is **admitted if not denied** when a response is required.

**Rule 8(c): Affirmative defenses** must be stated in the answer. Examples: **statute of limitations**, **res judicata**, **estoppel**, release, payment, waiver, fraud, contributory negligence.
- An affirmative defense says "even if everything alleged is true, the plaintiff still loses because of X." **The defendant bears the burden** of pleading and proving it.

### Reading: Rule 12 (CB 283–86)
**Rule 12(a): Timing.**
- Answer **21 days** after service of the summons and complaint. **60 days** if service was **waived** (Rule 4(d)).
- **12(a)(4):** Filing a Rule 12 motion **suspends** the answer deadline. If the motion is denied, answer **14 days after notice of the court's action** (answering 30 days after denial is **untimely**).

**Rule 12(b): The seven defenses.** These may be raised by **motion before pleading** or in the answer:
1. lack of **subject-matter** jurisdiction
2. lack of **personal** jurisdiction
3. improper **venue**
4. insufficient **process** (a defect in the summons itself)
5. insufficient **service** of process (delivered improperly)
6. **failure to state a claim** upon which relief can be granted
7. failure to **join a party** under Rule 19

**Rule 12(c): Judgment on the pleadings.** Made **after the pleadings are closed** but early enough not to delay trial. It looks at the complaint **and** the answer.

**Rule 12(d):** If matters **outside the pleadings** are presented and not excluded, a 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion **becomes a Rule 56 summary judgment motion**.

**Rule 12(e): More definite statement.** For a pleading so **vague or ambiguous** the party can't reasonably prepare a response. It must be made **before** the responsive pleading.

**Rule 12(f): Strike.** Removes an **insufficient defense** or **redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous** matter. Made before responding, or within **21 days** after being served if no response is allowed. Motions to strike are **disfavored**.

**Rule 12(g)(2) and 12(h): Waiver.**
- **12(h)(1):** Defenses **(b)(2)–(5)** (personal jurisdiction, venue, process, service) are **WAIVED** if left out of a pre-answer Rule 12 motion, or if not raised in the first responsive pleading or an amendment as of course.
- **12(h)(2):** **Failure to state a claim** (and failure to join a Rule 19 party, or to state a legal defense) can be raised **in any pleading, by 12(c) motion, or at trial**.
- **12(h)(3):** **Subject-matter jurisdiction** can be raised **at any time**. The court must dismiss.

**Which motion when:**
- To argue the complaint fails legally (e.g., the First Amendment doesn't reach a private school) at the earliest opportunity: a **12(b)(6)** motion.
- If the defendant **already answered** and the 21-day amend-as-of-course window has passed: a **12(c)** motion, which 12(h)(2) allows.

### Reading: *Bowers v. Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems* (CB 288–91)
**Rule:** Twombly and Iqbal plausibility does **NOT** apply to affirmative defenses (one side of a district-court split).

**Case: Bowers v. MERS** (D. Kan. 2011)
- **Facts:** Mortgagors moved under **12(f)** to strike the defendants' bare-bones **affirmative defenses** as not meeting Twombly and Iqbal.
- **Issue:** Does the Twombly/Iqbal plausibility standard apply to affirmative defenses?
- **Holding:** No. The motion to strike was denied.
  - **Text:** 8(a)(2) requires a "short and plain statement … **showing** that the pleader is entitled to relief." 8(b) and 8(c) only require a party to "**state**" its defenses. There is no "showing" requirement.
  - **Fairness:** A defendant has only **21 days** to answer, while a plaintiff can investigate for years before filing (up to the statute of limitations). Motions to strike are **disfavored**, and discovery will flesh out the defenses.
- **Takeaway:** Some courts refuse to apply Twombly and Iqbal plausibility to affirmative defenses, so a short-form defense that gives fair notice survives a motion to strike.

### Reading: *Perez v. Gordon & Wong Law Group* (CB 292–95)
**Rule:** Twombly and Iqbal **DO** apply to affirmative defenses (the other side of the split).

**Case: Perez v. Gordon & Wong Law Group** (N.D. Cal. 2012)
- **Facts:** A debt-collection (FDCPA) plaintiff moved to strike **15 boilerplate affirmative defenses**.
- **Issue:** Must affirmative defenses meet the Twombly/Iqbal plausibility standard?
- **Holding:** Yes. All but one were struck, **with leave to amend**.
  - The same purpose (fair notice, weeding out boilerplate) applies. Bare legal conclusions ("failure to mitigate," "unclean hands") with no facts don't give fair notice.
  - Some listed "defenses" (e.g., failure to state a claim) **aren't affirmative defenses at all**; they just negate an element.
  - **Bowers vs. Perez is a district-court split.** Both positions plus the textual argument ("showing" vs. "state") belong in an answer.
- **Takeaway:** Other courts apply plausibility to affirmative defenses, striking bare-bones defenses under Rule 12(f) with leave to amend.

### Reading: Rule 15 (CB 297–98)
**Rule 15(a)(1): Amend once as a matter of course** (no permission needed):
- (A) within **21 days after serving** the pleading; **or**
- (B) if a responsive pleading is required, within **21 days after service of the responsive pleading**, or **21 days after service of a 12(b), (e), or (f) motion**, whichever is earlier.

**Rule 15(a)(2): Otherwise** the amending party needs the opposing party's **written consent** or the **court's leave**. "The court should **freely give leave when justice so requires**."

**Rule 15(a)(3):** Respond to an amended pleading within the time remaining or **14 days**, whichever is later.

**Foman v. Davis reasons to deny leave:** **undue delay**, **bad faith or dilatory motive**, **repeated failure to cure**, **undue prejudice** to the opponent, and **futility**.
- **Prejudice** means harm to the opponent's ability to litigate the merits (lost witnesses or evidence, a need for new discovery). The **mere passage of time** or **planning around the old pleading** isn't enough.

**Applications:**
- Answer served 10 days ago → **amend as of course** under 15(a)(1)(A).
- Answer served 25 days ago → the window is closed, so get leave or consent, or use 12(c).
- Amendment 18 months in, no new discovery needed, and D's only claimed "prejudice" is strategy planning → **grant leave**.

**Also:** 15(b) (amendments during or after trial to conform to the evidence), 15(c) (**relation back** to the original filing date for statute-of-limitations purposes), and 15(d) (supplemental pleadings about events **after** the original pleading).

### Reading: *Global Tech. & Trading v. Tech Mahindra* (CB 299–302)
**Rule:** A late affirmative defense can be allowed absent **prejudice**, effectively as a **Rule 15 amendment**. Prejudice means harm to the plaintiff's ability to meet the defense on the merits.

**Case: Global Tech. & Trading v. Tech Mahindra** (7th Cir. 2015)
- **Facts:** A broker sued for a roughly $600K commission on an oral deal. **Four years in**, the defendant raised for the first time (at **summary judgment**) that the **Illinois Business Brokers Act** barred the claim. That is an **affirmative defense omitted from the answer**.
- **Issue:** Is an affirmative defense forfeited when it was left out of the answer and raised for the first time at summary judgment?
- **Holding:** Not fatal. Rule 8(c) says affirmative defenses belong in the answer, but **a late defense can be allowed absent prejudice**.
  - Prejudice examples: a witness died or documents were lost. **Extra litigation expense or a lost tactical advantage isn't prejudice.** Here the defense was purely legal, so there was no prejudice.
  - Easterbrook also distinguishes "**waiver**" (intentional relinquishment) from "**forfeiture**" (failure to raise in time).
- **Takeaway:** An affirmative defense omitted from the answer is not automatically forfeited and can be raised later if the plaintiff suffers no unfair prejudice.

### Reading: *Beeck v. Aquaslide 'N' Dive Corp.* (CB 302–06)
**Rule:** Leave to amend is **liberally granted** and reviewed for **abuse of discretion**. Deny it only for bad faith, undue delay, undue prejudice, or futility (Foman).

**Case: Beeck v. Aquaslide 'N' Dive Corp.** (8th Cir. 1977)
- **Facts:**
  - Beeck was badly injured on a water slide. Aquaslide **admitted manufacturing it** in its answer (relying on **three insurers'** investigations).
  - **After the statute of limitations ran**, Aquaslide's president inspected the slide, found it was a **knock-off**, and moved to amend to **deny** manufacture.
  - The trial court **granted leave** and held a **separate trial** on manufacture. The jury found Aquaslide didn't make it.
- **Issue:** Did the trial court abuse its discretion by allowing the amendment after the limitations period had run against the real manufacturer?
- **Holding:** No abuse of discretion, **affirmed**.
  - There was **no bad faith** (reasonable reliance on the insurers, prompt action once the truth was found).
  - **Prejudice** to the plaintiffs (time-barred against the real maker) is serious but not decisive. They may have other remedies (e.g., against the seller or owner). It would be unjust to make Aquaslide pay for a slide it didn't make.
- **Takeaway:** Leave to amend under Rule 15(a) is freely given, and absent prejudice, bad faith, or undue delay a defendant may amend even to deny a fact it previously admitted.

# UNIT III — Discovery (Classes 10–12)

## Class 10 (9/15) — The Rules Framework for Discovery

### Reading: Rules 26, 30, and 33–37 (CB 319–46)
**Rule 26(a): Required disclosures** (no request needed).
- **26(a)(1) initial disclosures:**
  - (i) names of people likely to have discoverable info that the party **may use to support** its claims or defenses (**not** info used **solely for impeachment**);
  - (ii) copies or descriptions of documents and ESI the party may use;
  - (iii) a **damages computation**;
  - (iv) **insurance agreements**.
- Due within **14 days after the 26(f) conference**.
- **26(a)(2):** expert disclosures. **26(a)(3):** pretrial disclosures.

**Rule 26(b)(1): Scope of discovery** (the core test). Any matter that is:
1. **nonprivileged**,
2. **relevant to any party's claim or defense**, and
3. **proportional to the needs of the case**.

The proportionality factors are the importance of the issues, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative **access** to information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery, and whether the **burden or expense outweighs the likely benefit**.
- Information **need not be admissible** to be discoverable.

**Rule 26(b)(2)(C):** The court **must** limit discovery that is unreasonably **cumulative or duplicative**, obtainable from a **more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive source**, sought after ample opportunity to get it, or outside 26(b)(1).

**Rule 26(b)(5): Privilege claims.**
- **(A) Privilege log:** The withholding party must **expressly claim** the privilege and **describe** what's withheld well enough for others to assess the claim **without revealing** the privileged content.
- **(B) Clawback procedure:** The producing party **notifies** the receiving party. The receiver must promptly **return, sequester, or destroy** the material, **may not use or disclose it** until the claim is resolved, and may present it to the court **under seal** for a ruling.

**Rule 26(c): Protective orders.**
- Requires a motion, a **good-faith meet-and-confer certification**, and **good cause**.
- Protects against **annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense**.
- Options include forbidding the discovery, limiting its scope, setting terms, sealing a deposition, and **(c)(1)(G): requiring that a trade secret or confidential commercial information not be revealed, or be revealed only in a specified way**.
- Example: a soda formula is relevant and not privileged (not legal advice, not prepared for litigation), so the right move is a **protective order under 26(c)(1)(G)**.

**Other Rule 26 provisions:**
- **26(d)(1):** No discovery before the **26(f) conference** (with limited exceptions).
- **26(e):** **Duty to supplement** disclosures and responses that turn out to be incomplete or incorrect.
- **26(f):** The parties must confer early to plan discovery (preservation of ESI, privilege issues, a phased plan) and submit a written report.
- **26(g): Certification for discovery** (the counterpart to Rule 11). Signing certifies, after **reasonable inquiry**, that disclosures are complete and correct, and that requests, responses, and objections are warranted, **not for an improper purpose**, and **not unreasonable or unduly burdensome**. Under **26(g)(3)** the court **must** sanction a violation that lacks substantial justification.

**The discovery devices:**

| Device | Key limits |
|---|---|
| **Rule 30 depositions** | **10 per side** without leave; **1 day of 7 hours** each. **30(b)(6):** the organization designates who testifies for it. **30(c)(2):** objections must be concise and nonargumentative. An instruction **not to answer** is allowed only to preserve a privilege, enforce a court limit, or present a 30(d)(3) motion. |
| **Rule 33 interrogatories** | **Parties only**, not their lawyers (Hickman). **25** including subparts. Answer in **30 days**. **33(b)(4):** objections must be **specific** or they're **waived**. **33(d):** a party may answer by producing business records. |
| **Rule 34 production** | Documents, ESI, tangible things, entry onto land. Respond in **30 days**. Objections must be **specific** and **state whether responsive material is being withheld**. Nonparties are reached by **Rule 45** subpoena. |
| **Rule 35 exams** | Physical or mental exam **only by court order**, on **good cause**, when the condition is **in controversy**. |
| **Rule 36 admissions** | **Deemed admitted** if not answered in **30 days**. Admissions are **conclusively established** unless the court allows withdrawal (36(b)). |

**Rule 37: Enforcement.**
- **37(a): Motion to compel** (must certify a good-faith attempt to confer). An evasive or incomplete answer counts as a failure to answer. **37(a)(5):** the **loser pays expenses** unless substantially justified.
- **37(b)(2): Violating a court order.** Sanctions include facts **deemed established**, **precluding** evidence, **striking pleadings**, **dismissal**, **default judgment**, and **contempt**.
- **37(c)(1):** Failing to disclose or supplement means the party **can't use** that info or witness unless the failure was **substantially justified or harmless**.
- **37(d):** Failing to attend one's own deposition or to answer interrogatories at all permits sanctions **without a prior order**.
- **37(e): Lost ESI.**
  - Applies when ESI that should have been preserved is lost through a failure to take reasonable steps and **can't be restored**.
  - On **prejudice**, the court may order measures **no greater than necessary to cure** it.
  - **Only on intent to deprive** may the court impose an **adverse inference**, **dismissal**, or **default**.

### Reading: *Mancia v. Mayflower Textile Services* (CB 348–60)
**Rule:** **Boilerplate objections violate Rules 33(b)(4) and 34(b)(2)** (objections must be **specific**), and **unparticularized objections are waived**. **Rule 26(g)** imposes an **objective** duty to "**stop and think**" before serving any request, response, or objection.

**Case: Mancia v. Mayflower Textile Servs.** (D. Md. 2008)
- **Facts:** FLSA overtime plaintiffs moved to compel. The defendants used **boilerplate objections** ("overly broad, unduly burdensome, not reasonably calculated…") with no specifics.
- **Issue:** Do generic, unparticularized objections satisfy the discovery rules, and what does Rule 26(g) require of both sides?
- **Holding:** No. The objections were improper and waived, and Rule 26(g) covers *requests* (overbroad demands) as much as *responses*.
  - The court ordered a **cooperative meet-and-confer**: estimate the realistic **damages and fees** range (the amount in controversy), then size discovery **proportionally** and consider **phased discovery** (cheapest, most promising sources first).
  - **Theme:** Cooperation in discovery is **consistent with** zealous advocacy, not opposed to it.
- **Takeaway:** Boilerplate discovery objections are improper and waived, and Rule 26(g) requires counsel to certify that both requests and responses are reasonable and proportional.

## Class 11 (9/16) — Privilege & Work Product

**Two distinct protections:**

| | **Attorney-client privilege** | **Work product (Hickman; Rule 26(b)(3))** |
|---|---|---|
| Protects | **Confidential communications** between lawyer and client for **legal advice** | **Documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation** by or for a party or its **representative** |
| Strength | **Absolute** (unless waived or crime-fraud) | **Qualified.** *Fact* work product yields to **substantial need + undue hardship**. *Opinion* work product is near-absolute. |
| Facts? | **Facts are NOT privileged**, only the communication | **Underlying facts are NOT protected.** The witness can still be deposed. |
| Waiver | Any disclosure to a third party (outside the privilege) | Disclosure to an **adversary or a "conduit" to an adversary** |

### Reading: *SodexoMAGIC, LLC v. Drexel University* (CB 385–89)
**Rule: Attorney-client privilege elements:**
1. a communication by a client (or prospective client);
2. to a lawyer (or a lawyer's subordinate) **acting as a lawyer**;
3. made **in confidence**;
4. **for the purpose of obtaining legal advice**; and
5. not waived.

**Case: SodexoMAGIC v. Drexel University** (E.D. Pa. 2018)
- **Facts:** In a contract dispute, the court reviewed contested corporate emails **in camera** for privilege, including emails involving in-house counsel, emails merely copying lawyers, and one sent outside the company.
- **Issue:** Which of the internal emails involving lawyers were protected by the attorney-client privilege?
- **Holding:** Only those whose primary purpose was legal advice; the rest had to be produced, with privileged portions redacted.
  - **In-house counsel's** communications are privileged **only when the purpose is legal advice, not business advice** (the "**primary purpose**" test for mixed messages). The party claiming privilege bears the **burden**.
  - **Merely copying a lawyer** on an email doesn't make it privileged. A lawyer or paralegal acting as a "**scrivener**" (just typing in requested changes) isn't giving legal advice.
  - An internal business email that **relies on** prior legal advice isn't privileged unless it **repeats counsel's advice essentially verbatim**. Then only that part is privileged and gets **redacted**.
  - **Sending a privileged email outside the company** (e.g., to "College X") **destroys the privilege** as to that communication.
- **Takeaway:** Attorney-client privilege covers only communications whose primary purpose is legal advice, and mixed business-legal documents get redacted rather than withheld.

### Reading: *Upjohn Co. v. United States* (CB 390–96)
**Rule:** The privilege covers communications from **employees at any level** made **at the direction of corporate superiors**, **to counsel acting as counsel**, to **secure legal advice** for the corporation, about matters **within the scope of the employee's duties**, where the employees knew the purpose and the communications were **kept confidential**. The "control group" test is rejected.

**Case: Upjohn Co. v. United States** (U.S. 1981)
- **Facts:** Upjohn's general counsel investigated foreign bribes. **Questionnaires** went to managers over the Chairman's signature, stamped **highly confidential**, with answers returned to the GC. The IRS summoned the questionnaires and interview notes.
- **Issue:** Does the attorney-client privilege protect communications between corporate counsel and employees outside the "control group," and are the interview notes protected work product?
- **Holding:** Yes to both. **The "control group" test is rejected.**
  - **Rationale:** Lower-level employees have the info lawyers need. The privilege protects giving information **to** the lawyer, not just advice **from** the lawyer. It encourages corporate **compliance**.
  - **Facts aren't privileged.** The IRS could still interview the employees itself. Convenience to the government doesn't override the privilege.
  - **Work product:** Attorney **notes and memos** of interviews reveal the attorney's **mental processes** (opinion work product). They get **special protection** that isn't overcome by a simple showing of substantial need.
- **Takeaway:** Corporate attorney-client privilege covers communications with employees at any level made to give counsel information needed for legal advice, rejecting the control-group test.

### Reading: *Hickman v. Taylor* (CB 398–409)
**Rule:** Materials an attorney prepares in anticipation of litigation (witness statements, memoranda, mental impressions) are **work product**, not discoverable on a "**naked, general demand**" without a showing of **necessity or justification**. **Now codified in Rule 26(b)(3).**

**Case: Hickman v. Taylor** (U.S. 1947), the **origin of the work-product doctrine**
- **Facts:**
  - A tug sank and 5 crew died. The tug owners' lawyer **Fortenbaugh** privately interviewed the survivors and took **signed statements**, and made **memoranda** of other interviews.
  - The plaintiff (by interrogatory) demanded copies of the statements and accounts of the oral statements. Fortenbaugh refused and was **held in contempt**.
- **Issue:** May a party obtain, without any showing of need, the written statements and interview memoranda prepared by opposing counsel in anticipation of litigation?
- **Holding:** No, **not discoverable** without a showing of necessity.
  - The **attorney-client privilege does NOT apply**: these are statements from third-party witnesses, not client communications. But the attorney's **work product** is protected by a separate policy.
  - A lawyer must be able to prepare "**with a certain degree of privacy**." Otherwise "much of what is now put down in writing would remain unwritten," and the profession would be demoralized.
  - The **burden is on the party seeking discovery** to show **necessity or justification** (e.g., the witness is unavailable or can be reached only with difficulty). Here the survivors' **public testimony** was available and they **could be interviewed directly**, so there was no need.
  - **Oral statements and the lawyer's memos of them** get even more protection. Forcing disclosure would make the attorney a **witness** and risk inaccuracy.
  - **Interrogatories go to parties, not lawyers.**
  - Justice **Jackson's concurrence:** "Discovery was hardly intended to enable a learned profession to perform its functions … **on wits borrowed from the adversary**."
- **Takeaway:** Materials prepared in anticipation of litigation are protected work product, discoverable only on a showing of substantial need, and an attorney's mental impressions get near-absolute protection.

## Class 12 (9/22) — Privilege & Work Product (cont.)

### Reading: Rule 26(b) (CB 411–12)
**Rule 26(b)(3)(A): Fact work product.**
- Covers documents and tangible things **prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial** by or for a party or its **representative**. "Representative" includes the **attorney, consultant, surety, indemnitor, insurer, or agent**.
- Discoverable **only if** (i) otherwise within 26(b)(1) **and** (ii) the requester shows **substantial need** and **cannot, without undue hardship, obtain the substantial equivalent** by other means.

**Rule 26(b)(3)(B): Opinion work product.** Even when ordering discovery, the court **must protect** the **mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories** of an attorney or other representative. This is near-absolute.

**Rule 26(b)(3)(C):** A party or witness can always get a copy of **their own previous statement**.
- **"In anticipation of litigation":** Most courts use a "**because of**" test. It's work product if prepared **because of** the prospect of litigation, even if it also serves business purposes, but **not** if it would have been prepared in substantially the same form anyway (e.g., routine reports).
- **Waiver (Sedona):** Disclosing work product waives protection only if the recipient is an **adversary or a "conduit" to one**. Sharing with an interested non-adversary does not waive it.

**Applications:**
- An insurer's report on whether a slide company made the slide is **work product**, since an insurer is a "representative." If the plaintiff examined the slide themselves, there's **no substantial need**. **But the facts aren't protected**: the plaintiff can **depose the insurer** about who made the slide.
- In-house counsel commissions a root-cause report two days after an explosion. It is **work product if prepared because of anticipated litigation**. A separate SEC filing obligation doesn't negate this, and in-house counsel counts.

### Reading: Federal Rule of Evidence 502 (CB 431–32)
- **Traditional rule:** **Any** disclosure, even inadvertent, waived the privilege. That made privilege review hugely expensive.

**FRE 502(a): Intentional disclosure.** Waiver extends to **undisclosed** material on the **same subject matter** only if the waiver was **intentional**, the disclosed and undisclosed material **concern the same subject**, and they **ought in fairness be considered together**. This prevents selective "sword and shield" use.

**FRE 502(b): Inadvertent disclosure is NOT a waiver** if:
1. the disclosure was **inadvertent**;
2. the holder **took reasonable steps to prevent** disclosure; **and**
3. the holder **promptly took reasonable steps to rectify** the error, including following **FRCP 26(b)(5)(B)**.

**FRE 502(c):** Covers disclosures made in a state proceeding.

**FRE 502(d): Court order.** A federal court can order that disclosure connected with the litigation is **not a waiver**. The order **binds all other federal and state proceedings**, even if the production wasn't careful. This is the best protection.

**FRE 502(e): Party agreement (clawback).** Binds **only the parties** unless it's incorporated into a court order.

**Applications:**
- A lawyer **drops a strategy memo on a stack** and hands the whole pile over in a rush. That is **not "reasonable steps to prevent,"** so it's **waived** and there's no clawback.
- A firm with **reasonable screening steps** that **promptly notifies** the other side has **no waiver**.

### Reading: *In re Abilify Products Liability Litigation* (CB 419–29)
**Rule:** A privilege log satisfies 26(b)(5)(A) if it describes withheld material well enough to assess the claim. **Work product extends to materials prepared for OTHER litigation**, and the **"primary purpose"** test governs mixed business/legal communications.

**Case: In re Abilify (Aripiprazole) Prods. Liab. Litig.** (N.D. Fla. 2017)
- **Facts:** Plaintiffs attacked the drug makers' **privilege logs** and whole categories of withheld documents. The court reviewed them all **in camera**.
- **Issue:** Were the defendants' privilege logs adequate, and were the challenged categories of documents privileged or work product?
- **Holding:** The **logs were adequate** under 26(b)(5)(A) and the court's ESI order. Most categorical challenges failed, though a few documents were ordered produced.
  - Work product covers materials prepared for prior product cases and regulatory investigations, not just the current case.
  - Not naming a specific attorney ("Legal Dept.") isn't fatal if the context shows legal involvement. **Attachments** need not be separately logged if that would reveal privileged info.
  - **Facts sent to counsel for legal advice:** the *communication* is privileged, but the underlying facts are still discoverable elsewhere.
  - **Merely cc'ing a lawyer doesn't make it privileged**, but wide distribution **doesn't automatically defeat** privilege either.
  - **Third-party consultants** (e.g., PR or marketing firms) acting as the **functional equivalent** of employees, or assisting counsel, **don't waive** the privilege.
- **Takeaway:** A privilege log satisfies Rule 26(b)(5)(A) if it describes each withheld document well enough for the other side to assess the privilege claim.

### Reading: Note on Privilege Logs, Redaction, and the Crime-Fraud Exception (CB 433–36)
**Rule: Crime-fraud exception.** Neither privilege nor work product protects communications **in furtherance of a contemplated or ongoing crime or fraud**, **even if the lawyer didn't know**. The focus is the **client's** purpose.
- **Standard:** **probable cause**, meaning a prudent person would have a reasonable basis to suspect the crime or fraud **and** that the communications **furthered** it.

**Case: Chevron Corp. v. Weinberg Group** (D.D.C. 2012)
- **Facts:** Arising from the Ecuador (Lago Agrio) judgment scandal, Chevron sought documents from a consultant that allegedly "cleansed" a tainted expert report. The consultant's log used generic privilege entries.
- **Issue:** Did the generic log satisfy 26(b)(5)(A), and could Chevron reach the consultant's work product?
- **Holding:** **Generic log entries** ("attorney-client privilege") **don't satisfy 26(b)(5)(A)**. Chevron showed **substantial need** for **fact work product**, so Weinberg had to produce it **with opinion work product redacted**.
  - **Redaction** is the tool for mixed documents.
- **Takeaway:** Generic privilege-log entries are inadequate, and fact work product yields to substantial need while opinion work product is redacted.

**Case: Chevron Corp. v. Donziger** (S.D.N.Y. 2013)
- **Facts:** Chevron sought documents from the Ecuadorian plaintiffs' law firm (Patton Boggs) related to the alleged fraud.
- **Issue:** Does the crime-fraud exception reach the firm's privileged communications and work product?
- **Holding:** Communications in furtherance of the fraud lose protection under the crime-fraud exception.
- **Takeaway:** Under the crime-fraud exception, communications made in furtherance of a crime or fraud lose attorney-client and work-product protection.

**Application** (client asks the lawyer to **backdate a patent application**):
- The client's first question ("is this lawful?") is arguably ordinary legal advice.
- The client's later **instruction to draft a backdated version** is a communication seeking the lawyer's services **in furtherance of fraud**, so it is not privileged **even though the lawyer refused**.

# UNIT IV — Summary Judgment & Adjudication Without Trial (Classes 13–14)

**Three checkpoints, three standards:**

| Stage | Question | What the court looks at |
|---|---|---|
| **12(b)(6)** | Are the **allegations** plausible? | The complaint only. Allegations are taken as true. |
| **Rule 56 SJ** | Is there a **genuine dispute of material fact**, i.e., could a **reasonable jury** find for the nonmovant? | **Evidence** (depositions, affidavits, documents) viewed **most favorably to the nonmovant** |
| **Rule 50 JMOL** | Same "reasonable jury" test | **Trial evidence** |

## Class 13 (9/23) — Summary Judgment

### Reading: Summary Judgment Note and Rule 56 (CB 563–64)
**Rule 56(a):** The court **shall grant** summary judgment if the movant shows (1) there is **no genuine dispute as to any material fact** and (2) the movant is **entitled to judgment as a matter of law**. It can be for the whole case, a claim, a defense, or **part** of a claim.

**Rule 56(b):** Unless the court orders otherwise, a party may move **any time until 30 days after the close of all discovery**.

**Rule 56(c)(1):** A party supports a factual position by **citing record materials** (depositions, documents, ESI, affidavits or declarations, admissions, interrogatory answers), **or** by showing the materials cited don't establish a dispute, or that the adverse party **cannot produce admissible evidence** to support the fact.

**Rule 56(c)(4):** Affidavits must be made on **personal knowledge**, set out facts that would be **admissible**, and show the affiant is **competent** to testify.

**Rule 56(d):** If the nonmovant shows by affidavit that it **can't yet present essential facts**, the court may defer or deny the motion or allow more discovery.

**Rule 56(e):** If a party fails to properly address another's assertion of fact, the court may treat the fact as **undisputed**.

**Rule 56(f):** The court may grant SJ **sua sponte** or on grounds not raised, **after notice** and a reasonable time to respond.

**Rule 56(g):** The court may also enter an order stating that material facts are not genuinely in dispute (**partial SJ**).

**Rule: Anderson v. Liberty Lobby** (U.S. 1986) (discussed in the note)
- **Material** means the fact **might affect the outcome under the governing substantive law**. The substantive law defines materiality. Example: carelessness is material in negligence but **immaterial** in strict liability.
- **Genuine** means the evidence is such that a **reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmovant**. A "scintilla" isn't enough.
- **The SJ standard mirrors the directed-verdict (Rule 50) standard.** It also incorporates the **substantive evidentiary burden** (e.g., "clear and convincing" in libel).
- **Credibility determinations, weighing evidence, and drawing inferences are jury functions.** The nonmovant's evidence "**is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor**."
- Brennan's dissent warned this could make SJ "a full-blown paper trial on the merits."

**Rule: Matsushita v. Zenith** (U.S. 1986)
- The substantive law can **limit permissible inferences**. In an antitrust conspiracy case, evidence **equally consistent with independent, lawful conduct** doesn't create a genuine issue. The plaintiff must offer evidence that "**tends to exclude the possibility**" of independent action. An **economically implausible** theory (predatory pricing conspiracy) needs **more persuasive** evidence.
- **Compare Twombly** (pleading) with **Matsushita** (SJ): the same "parallel conduct isn't enough" logic at different stages.

**Mueller (Taylor Swift case)** (discussed in the note): SJ was **denied** on the interference-with-employment claims because they turned on dueling, **credibility-dependent** accounts of the groping incident, which is for the jury. SJ was **granted** on the slander claims, which could be resolved **as a matter of law**. The same event can produce different SJ outcomes for different claims.

### Reading: Introductory Note on the Celotex Trilogy (CB 578–81)
- **Trilogy (1986):** **Celotex** (the movant's burden), **Anderson** (the "genuine dispute" and "reasonable jury" standard, plus the substantive evidentiary standard), and **Matsushita** (implausible inferences). Together they made SJ a **robust** pretrial screening tool instead of a disfavored one.

### Reading: Introductory Note to *Celotex v. Catrett* (CB 582–83)
**Trial burdens:**
- **Burden of production:** If **nobody** puts on evidence, the party with this burden (usually the **plaintiff**) **loses**. It's satisfied by evidence that, if believed, would entitle the party to relief. Once met, the **other side must produce contrary evidence** or lose.
- **Burden of persuasion:** If the evidence is in **equipoise** (50/50), the party with this burden (the **plaintiff**, by **preponderance**) **loses**.

**SJ burdens:**
- The **movant always bears the burden of persuasion** on the motion.
- **Plaintiff as movant** (bears the trial burden): must present evidence supporting **every element** such that **no reasonable jury** could find for the defendant. A motion with no evidence is a "non-starter."
- **Defendant as movant (option 1):** present evidence that **affirmatively disproves** an element. The plaintiff must then come back with controverting evidence.
- **Defendant as movant (option 2):** **just point out the absence of evidence**, with no evidence of its own. That is **the Celotex question**.

### Reading: *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett* (CB 584–90)
**Rule:** Rule 56 mandates SJ, **after adequate time for discovery**, against a party who **fails to make a showing sufficient to establish an essential element** of its case **on which it bears the burden of proof at trial**. A movant without the trial burden may discharge its burden by "**pointing out** … **an absence of evidence**."

**Case: Celotex Corp. v. Catrett** (U.S. 1986)
- **Facts:** A widow sued asbestos makers for her husband's death. Celotex moved for SJ arguing the plaintiff **had no evidence** her husband was exposed to **Celotex's** products: she had **identified no witnesses** in **interrogatory answers**. Celotex attached **no affidavits** of its own. The D.C. Circuit called the motion "fatally defective."
- **Issue:** Must a defendant moving for SJ support its motion with evidence negating the plaintiff's claim, or can it simply point to the plaintiff's lack of evidence?
- **Holding:** It can point to the absence of evidence. **The Supreme Court reversed.**
  - A "**complete failure of proof**" on an essential element "renders all other facts immaterial."
  - The movant must still "**inform the court of the basis**" for the motion and identify the relevant parts of the record. A **conclusory** "they have no evidence" isn't enough (**White's concurrence**; **Brennan's dissent**).
  - The burden then **shifts**: the nonmovant must **go beyond the pleadings** and designate **specific facts** (with evidence, which **need not be in admissible form yet** but must be reducible to admissible evidence) showing a genuine issue for trial.
  - SJ is "**an integral part of the Federal Rules**," not a "disfavored procedural shortcut." Under **notice pleading**, SJ (not 12(b)(6)) is the main tool for weeding out factually unsupported claims.
  - On remand, the D.C. Circuit found the plaintiff's evidence (a letter, a deposition transcript) enough to survive.
- **Takeaway:** A movant without the burden of proof at trial can win summary judgment by pointing to the absence of evidence supporting the nonmovant's case.

**SJ framework:**
1. **State the Rule 56(a) standard** and the **Anderson** gloss.
2. **Lay out the substantive law, element by element.** Start here.
3. **Who bears the burden at trial on each element?** If the movant doesn't, Celotex lets it point to the absence of evidence.
4. **For each element:** what evidence exists? View it most favorably to the **nonmovant** and draw all **reasonable** inferences in its favor. **Could a reasonable jury** find for the nonmovant?
5. **Separate genuine disputes** (credibility contests, which go to the jury) from **speculation or opinion without basis** (which doesn't create a genuine issue).
6. **Cross-motions:** analyze **each motion separately**, flipping whose view of the evidence controls.

**Applications:**
- A genuine dispute on **who was driving** (P's testimony vs. D's) doesn't save P if **limitations**, an affirmative defense on which **D bears the burden**, is shown by D's evidence and **P offers nothing** in response. No genuine dispute on limitations means D gets SJ.
- Toxic exposure hypo: illness and damages are easily shown; the battleground is **causation**. A doctor's note saying "probably workplace" is undercut when the doctor **disavows** it at deposition, and the plaintiff's own belief ("I bet that's what happened") is **speculation**, not personal knowledge. The question is whether a reasonable jury can infer actual exposure or only guess.

## Class 14 (9/24) — Summary Judgment; Other Pre-Trial Dispositions

### Reading: *Scott v. Harris* (CB 613–21)
**Rule:** "**When opposing parties tell two different stories, one of which is blatantly contradicted by the record, so that no reasonable jury could believe it, a court should not adopt that version of the facts** for purposes of ruling on a motion for summary judgment."

**Case: Scott v. Harris** (U.S. 2007)
- **Facts:** Harris fled a speeding stop at **85+ mph**. Deputy Scott **rammed his car from behind**. Harris crashed and became **quadriplegic**, then sued for Fourth Amendment excessive force. The lower courts **denied SJ** based on Harris's account (careful driving, roads cleared). This came up on **interlocutory appeal** (qualified immunity).
- **Issue:** Must the court accept the nonmovant's version of events at SJ when an undisputed video blatantly contradicts it?
- **Holding:** No. SJ for Scott.
  - The **undisputed, authentic dashcam video** showed a "Hollywood-style car chase of the most frightening sort." The Court viewed the facts "**in the light depicted by the videotape**."
  - On the merits: ending a chase that endangers innocent bystanders is **reasonable** even if it risks the fleeing driver's life. That driver is culpable, and the bystanders are innocent.
  - **Stevens's dissent:** Eight appellate judges below watched the same tape and saw something different, which is itself evidence that reasonable jurors could disagree. Judges are substituting their own **factfinding** for the jury's.
  - Scott is a **narrow exception** to the "view facts most favorably to the nonmovant" rule. It needs objective evidence (like a video) of **unquestioned authenticity** that **blatantly** contradicts the account. Ordinary conflicting testimony is **not** enough (Tolan).
- **Takeaway:** When video evidence blatantly contradicts the nonmovant's version of events, a court need not adopt that version at summary judgment.

### Reading: *Tolan v. Cotton*
**Rule:** Even in **qualified immunity** cases, courts must **view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant** (Anderson). **Credibility disputes are for the jury**.

**Case: Tolan v. Cotton** (U.S. 2014)
- **Facts:** Police wrongly believed Tolan's car was stolen (a typo in the plate number) and confronted him on **his parents' porch**. Sgt. Cotton allegedly **slammed Tolan's mother** into the garage door. Tolan shouted "get your f---ing hands off my mom," and Cotton **shot him**. The district court granted SJ, and the **5th Circuit affirmed on qualified immunity**.
- **Issue:** Did the Fifth Circuit err by crediting the officer's version of disputed facts at summary judgment?
- **Holding:** Yes. **Vacated and remanded.** The 5th Circuit **credited the officer's version** on four disputed facts:
  1. **Lighting:** dim vs. floodlit
  2. **The mother's demeanor:** agitated vs. calm
  3. **Tolan's words:** a threat vs. a son's plea
  4. **Tolan's position:** charging vs. on his knees
  - Witnesses on both sides have their "own perceptions, recollections, and even potential biases."
  - **Scott vs. Tolan:** Scott had an **objective video** that blatantly contradicted one story. Tolan was **testimony vs. testimony**, a classic **genuine dispute**, so SJ is improper.
- **Takeaway:** At summary judgment a court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant, and testimony against testimony is a genuine dispute for the jury.

### Reading: Introductory Note on Default Judgment; Rules 41, 54(c), 55, and 60 (CB 309–13)
**Rule 55: Default.**
- **55(a) Entry of default:** The **clerk** enters default when a party **has failed to plead or otherwise defend**, as shown by affidavit or otherwise.
- **55(b)(1) Clerk's judgment:** Only for a **sum certain**, against a defendant who **hasn't appeared** and isn't a minor or incompetent.
- **55(b)(2) Court's judgment:** Required in all other cases. If the defaulting party **has appeared**, it gets **7 days' written notice** before the hearing.
- **55(c):** The court may set aside an **entry of default for good cause**, and a **default judgment under Rule 60(b)**.
- **55(d):** Default judgment against the **United States** only if the claimant establishes the claim **by evidence** satisfactory to the court.

**Rule 54(c):** A **default judgment must not differ in kind from, or exceed in amount, what is demanded in the pleadings**. Other final judgments should grant the relief the party is entitled to **even if not demanded**.

**Rule 60(b): Relief from a final judgment.** The grounds are:
1. **mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect**;
2. **newly discovered evidence** that couldn't have been found in time for a Rule 59(b) motion;
3. **fraud, misrepresentation, or misconduct** by the opposing party;
4. the judgment is **void** (e.g., no jurisdiction, or **no constitutionally adequate notice**, which links to Mullane);
5. it has been satisfied or released, or prospective application is **no longer equitable**;
6. **any other reason** that justifies relief (extraordinary circumstances).

**Rule 60(c):** The motion must be made within a **reasonable time**, and for **(1)–(3), no more than 1 year** after the judgment.

**Rule 41(a): Voluntary dismissal.**
- **41(a)(1)(A)(i):** The plaintiff may dismiss **without a court order** by notice **before the opposing party serves an answer or an SJ motion**.
- **41(a)(1)(A)(ii):** Or by a stipulation signed by all parties.
- These are **without prejudice** unless stated otherwise.
- **The two-dismissal rule:** If the plaintiff previously dismissed an action **based on the same claim**, a notice dismissal **operates as an adjudication on the merits**.
- **41(a)(2):** Otherwise, dismissal is **by court order** on terms the court considers proper.

**Rule 41(b): Involuntary dismissal.** For failure to **prosecute** or to comply with the rules or a court order. **Unless the order says otherwise, it operates as an adjudication on the merits** (which matters for claim preclusion), **except** dismissals for **lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, or failure to join a Rule 19 party**.

**Rule 41(d):** If a plaintiff who previously dismissed refiles the same claim, the court may order the plaintiff to pay the **costs** of the prior action.

# UNIT V — Trial (Classes 15–16 Part 1)

## Class 15 (9/29) — Trial by Jury

### Reading: U.S. Const. Amend. VII; Rules 38 and 39 (CB 474–77) — ⚠️ the Amendment text isn't in exam packet
**Rule: Seventh Amendment.** "In Suits at **common law**, where the value in controversy shall exceed **twenty dollars**, the right of trial by jury shall be **preserved**…"
- "**Common law**" means **law, not equity** (as of 1791 in England). Equity had **no jury**.
- "**Preserved**" means it keeps the 1791 right rather than creating new ones. That's why courts use **historical analogy** for modern claims.
- The Amendment applies only in **federal** court. It is **not incorporated** against the states.

**Rule 38(a):** The jury right under the 7th Amendment or a federal statute "is preserved to the parties **inviolate**."

**Rule 38(b): Demand.** Serve a **written demand** no later than **14 days after the last pleading** directed to the issue is served. It may be included in a pleading.

**Rule 38(c):** A demand may **specify issues**. Otherwise it covers **all** jury-triable issues.

**Rule 38(d): Waiver.** **Failing to properly serve and file a demand waives the jury right.** A demand can be withdrawn only with **all parties' consent**.

**Rule 39(a):** When a jury is demanded, the case is tried to a jury **unless** the parties stipulate to a bench trial, or the court finds there's **no federal right** to a jury on some issues.

**Rule 39(b):** Issues with no proper demand are tried by the court, but the court **may, on motion, order a jury** anyway.

**Rule 39(c):** In a non-jury case the court may use an **advisory jury**, or, with the parties' consent, a jury whose verdict is binding.

### Reading: *Curtis v. Loether* (CB 480–84)
**Rule:** The 7th Amendment applies to **statutory** causes of action, including ones that **didn't exist in 1791**, when the action is analogous to a common-law action and seeks **legal relief** (damages).

**Case: Curtis v. Loether** (U.S. 1974)
- **Facts:** A Black woman sued white landlords under the **Fair Housing Act (Title VIII, § 812)** for refusing to rent to her, seeking **actual and punitive damages**. The **defendants** demanded a jury. The plaintiff resisted, fearing local juror prejudice.
- **Issue:** Is there a Seventh Amendment right to a jury in a damages action under § 812 of the Fair Housing Act?
- **Holding:** Yes, a **jury right exists**.
  - A § 812 damages action "**sounds basically in tort**": the statute creates a legal duty and compensates for breach.
  - **Damages** are "the traditional form of relief offered in the courts of law."
  - **NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin** (no jury in **administrative** proceedings) doesn't apply when Congress lets parties sue in **ordinary federal court**.
  - **Title VII backpay is distinguished.** Congress **labeled it "equitable"** and made it **discretionary and restitutionary**, so there is no jury.
  - **Policy** concerns (delay, juror bias against civil-rights plaintiffs) can't override the 7th Amendment's command.
  - Either party can demand the jury. **The jury right belongs to both sides.**
- **Takeaway:** The Seventh Amendment jury right applies to statutory claims that are analogous to common-law actions and seek legal relief like damages.

### Reading: *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local 391 v. Terry* (CB 484–91)
**Rule: The two-step test** (from Tull v. United States):
1. **Historical analog:** Compare the statutory action to **18th-century actions in English courts before the merger of law and equity**.
2. **Remedy:** Is the remedy sought **legal or equitable** in nature? **This step is more important.**

**Case: Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers v. Terry** (U.S. 1990)
- **Facts:** Laid-off truck drivers sued their **union** for breaching its **duty of fair representation** (DFR) in handling their grievances, seeking **backpay** (compensatory damages).
- **Issue:** Do employees suing their union for breach of the duty of fair representation, seeking backpay, have a Seventh Amendment jury right?
- **Holding:** Yes, a **jury right exists**.
  - **Step 1 (history):** The suit requires proving both (a) the **employer's breach of the CBA**, which is **contract-like and legal**, and (b) the **union's DFR breach**, which is **trust/fiduciary-like and equitable**. That leaves the analogy **in equipoise**.
    - The **attorney malpractice** analogy was rejected because employees can't fire the union.
    - The **arbitration vacatur** analogy was rejected because no arbitrator ever decided the DFR claim.
    - Per **Ross v. Bernhard**, look at the **nature of each issue**, not the whole suit.
  - **Step 2 (remedy):** **Money damages are the classic legal remedy.** The two exceptions don't apply:
    - **Restitution or disgorgement:** The backpay isn't money **wrongfully held by the union**. It's wages the employer would have paid.
    - **Incidental to or intertwined with injunctive relief:** No injunction was sought.
  - **Title VII backpay is distinguished** (labeled equitable by Congress, and restitutionary when sought from the employer).
  - **Brennan (concurring):** Drop the historical step and look only at the remedy. **Kennedy (dissent):** The trust analogy should control.
- **Takeaway:** Whether a jury right exists turns on comparing the claim to an 18th-century action and, more importantly, on whether the remedy sought is legal or equitable, and money damages are usually legal.

**7th Amendment framework:**
1. Is the claim **new or statutory**? That's fine (Curtis). Congress can't strip the jury right from a legal claim tried in federal court.
2. **Find the closest 1791 analog.** Tort and contract are legal. Trust, fiduciary, and injunction claims are equitable.
3. **Remedy (decisive):** Money damages are legal **unless** they're restitutionary (disgorging the defendant's ill-gotten gains) or incidental to an injunction.
4. Conclude.

**Applications:**
- A new Medicare intentional-tort statute with **money damages** has a **tort analog** and a **legal remedy**, so there's a **jury right**.
- A disgorgement-of-profits claim calls for the **two-step analysis**. Disgorgement leans equitable, but the test still has to be run.

### Reading: *Colgrove v. Battin* (CB 511–18)
**Rule:** A six-person civil jury **satisfies the 7th Amendment**. The Amendment's reference to "common law" defines the **kind of case** that gets a jury; it does **not** freeze every **incident** of the 1791 jury.

**Case: Colgrove v. Battin** (U.S. 1973)
- **Facts:** A Montana federal district court **local rule** set **6-person** juries for civil cases.
- **Issue:** Does a six-person civil jury violate the Seventh Amendment?
- **Holding:** No.
  - **12 members** is an incident of the 1791 jury, not a substantive requirement.
  - **Functional test:** Studies showed **no meaningful difference** in reliability or deliberation between 6 and 12 jurors.
  - **Dissents:** History and tradition say 12. Smaller juries are less representative.
  - "The Constitution requires 12" is **wrong**.
- **Takeaway:** The Seventh Amendment does not require a 12-person civil jury, so a six-person jury is constitutional.

### Reading: Rule 48 (CB 521–22)
**Rule 48(a):** A jury must have **at least 6 and no more than 12** members. **Each juror must participate** in the verdict unless excused under **Rule 47(c)**.

**Rule 48(b):** **Unless the parties stipulate otherwise**, the verdict must be **unanimous** and returned by a jury of **at least 6**.

**Rule 48(c):** **Polling.** Jurors must be polled individually on a party's request or the court's own motion. If the poll shows a lack of unanimity or assent, the court may direct further deliberation or order a new trial.

- Example: 9 empaneled, 3 excused, 6 deliberate, verdict 5–1. Six is enough, but with **no stipulation, the verdict must be unanimous**, so the verdict fails.
- "**Unlike criminal juries, civil juries do not need to be unanimous**" is **wrong** under the FRCP.

### Reading: Rule 47 and 28 U.S.C. § 1870 (CB 536–37) — ⚠️ § 1870 isn't in exam packet
**Rule 47(a): Voir dire.** The court may examine prospective jurors itself or let the parties do it. If the court does it, it must allow the parties to supplement with further questions.

**Rule 47(b):** **Peremptory challenges** are governed by **28 U.S.C. § 1870**.

**Rule 47(c):** During trial or deliberation, the court may **excuse a juror for good cause**.

**28 U.S.C. § 1870:**
- Each party gets **3 peremptory challenges**. Several defendants or plaintiffs **may be treated as a single party**, or the court may allow additional challenges.
- **Challenges for cause** are **unlimited** and decided by the **court**.
- **Peremptory challenges** traditionally need no reason. **Limit:** they can't be used to discriminate by **race** (Batson, extended to **civil** cases in Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete) or **sex** (J.E.B.).
- **For-cause challenges** require a showing of bias (e.g., the juror knows a party or has prejudged the case).

## Class 16, Part 1 (9/30) — Taking the Case from the Jury

### Reading: Rule 50 (CB 627–28)
**Rule 50(a): Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL; formerly "directed verdict").**
- **Timing:** **After a party has been fully heard** on an issue and **before the case is submitted to the jury**.
- **Standard:** A **reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis** to find for the party on that issue. This is the **same standard as SJ** (Anderson): view the evidence most favorably to the nonmovant, with **no credibility determinations**.
- **Form:** The motion must specify the judgment sought and the law and facts that entitle the movant to it.

**Rule 50(b): Renewed JMOL (formerly "JNOV").**
- **Requires a prior 50(a) motion.** It is a *renewal*, so without a 50(a) motion before submission, there is no 50(b) motion.
- **Timing:** File **within 28 days after entry of judgment**. It may be joined with an alternative **Rule 59 new-trial** request.
- **The court may:** (1) allow judgment on the verdict, (2) order a new trial, or (3) direct entry of JMOL.

**Rule 50(c):** If the court **grants** renewed JMOL, it must also **conditionally rule** on the new-trial motion (stating its grounds), so an appellate court knows what happens if it reverses.

**Rule 50(d):** The party against whom JMOL was entered may move for a new trial **within 28 days**.

**Rule 50(e):** If the court **denies** JMOL, the winner (the **appellee**) may argue on appeal for a **new trial** as a fallback if the appellate court reverses. The appellate court may then order a new trial, remand, or **direct entry of judgment** (Neely).

- **Seventh Amendment:** A **pre-verdict** JMOL doesn't violate the jury right (**Galloway**).

### Reading: Rule 59 (CB 630)
**Rule 59(a)(1)(A): New trial after a jury trial**, "for any reason for which a new trial has heretofore been granted in an action at law in federal court." Examples:
- verdict against the **clear (great) weight of the evidence**;
- **excessive or inadequate damages** (→ **remittitur**, with a new trial if the plaintiff refuses the reduction);
- prejudicial errors in **instructions or evidence**;
- misconduct by jurors or attorneys;
- newly discovered evidence.

**Rule 59(a)(1)(B):** After a **bench** trial, for any reason a rehearing has traditionally been granted in equity.

**Rule 59(b):** Motion due **within 28 days after entry of judgment**. **59(d):** The court may order a new trial **on its own** within 28 days.

**Rule 59(e):** Motion to **alter or amend a judgment**, due **within 28 days**.

**Rule 50 vs. Rule 59:**
- **Rule 50 JMOL** **ends** the case for the movant. Its standard is "**no reasonable jury**," and the judge may **not** weigh credibility.
- **Rule 59 new trial** is **less drastic**: a new jury decides. So the judge **may weigh the evidence** and grant a new trial if the verdict is against the **great weight of the evidence** or a **miscarriage of justice**, **even if** some evidence supports it.

### Reading: Rule 61 (CB 634)
**Rule 61: Harmless error.** No error in admitting or excluding evidence, or any other error, justifies a new trial or disturbing a judgment **unless it affects a party's substantial rights** (unless justice requires otherwise). Courts **must disregard** harmless errors **at every stage**.
- Pairs with Rule 59 and with appeals. The question is whether the error **likely affected the outcome**.

### Reading: *Neely v. Martin K. Eby Construction Co.* (CB 637–42)
**Rule:** After reversing a denial of JNOV for insufficient evidence, an **appellate court may itself direct entry of judgment** for the movant; Rule 50(e) preserves the appellee's chance to argue for a new trial but doesn't require a remand.

**Case: Neely v. Martin K. Eby Constr. Co.** (U.S. 1967)
- **Facts:**
  - A worker fell to his death from a missile-silo scaffold. The **jury awarded $25,000** to his daughter.
  - Eby had moved for a directed verdict at the close of evidence, preserving the issue. The trial court **denied** its JNOV motion.
  - The **10th Circuit reversed** for insufficient evidence and **directed dismissal**.
- **Issue:** May a court of appeals, after reversing a denial of JNOV, direct entry of judgment for the defendant instead of remanding for a possible new trial?
- **Holding:** Yes.
  - (1) **No Seventh Amendment problem.** An appellate JNOV intrudes on the jury no more than a trial court's JNOV does.
  - (2) **Rule 50(e)** is permissive.
  - If reversal rests on a **dispositive legal issue**, direct judgment. If it rests on **insufficient evidence**, consider whether the gap is **curable** (e.g., evidence was wrongly excluded). The appellee should raise new-trial grounds in the **trial court, the appellate brief, or a petition for rehearing**.
  - Here Neely raised **no** new-trial grounds below and told the 10th Circuit the trial was "fairly tried." Her excluded-evidence argument came **too late** (first raised in the Supreme Court). **Affirmed.**
  - Lesson: **preserve** issues. Movants must make a **50(a) motion before submission** to later renew under 50(b). Verdict-winners must **raise new-trial grounds** as a fallback.
- **Takeaway:** An appellate court that reverses a denial of JMOL may direct entry of judgment for the movant, so the verdict-winner must raise any new-trial grounds as a fallback or lose them.

# UNIT VI — Finality & Appeal (Classes 16 Part 2–17)

**Roadmap: can the court of appeals hear this appeal NOW?** The routes, in order:
1. **§ 1291 final decision?** Does it end the litigation on the merits, leaving nothing but execution? If so, it is appealable.
2. **Rule 54(b)?** Multiple claims or parties, one or more **fully** resolved, and the district court **expressly finds "no just reason for delay."**
3. **§ 1292(a)(1)?** An order **granting, refusing, modifying, or dissolving an injunction** is appealable as of right.
4. **§ 1292(b)?** The district judge **certifies** the question **and** the court of appeals agrees to hear it.
5. **Rule 23(f)?** The court of appeals may permit an appeal from an order granting or denying **class certification** (Unit VIII).
6. **Collateral order doctrine (Cohen)?** The order is conclusive, important and separate from the merits, and effectively unreviewable after final judgment. Judged **by category**.
7. **Mandamus?** Only for clear abuse of discretion or usurpation of power.
8. **Contempt?** Defy the order, be held in contempt, then appeal the contempt (Microsoft).

**Standards of review** (lecture 22):
- **De novo:** the appeals court decides for itself (**questions of law**).
- **Clearly erroneous:** overturn only for clear error (**questions of fact**).
- **Abuse of discretion:** overturn only if the trial court abused its discretion (matters **within the trial court's discretion**, e.g., leave to amend, class certification).

## Class 16, Part 2 (9/30) — Finality

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (CB 669)
**Rule § 1291:** Courts of appeals have jurisdiction over appeals from **all final decisions** of the district courts.

**Rule: Catlin v. United States.** A final decision "**ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment**."
- Nuances: It must end the litigation **on the merits**. **Collateral** matters like attorney's fees can remain pending. "Nothing left" means nothing **but execution**.

**Policies behind the final judgment rule:**
- avoid **piecemeal** appeals;
- **efficiency**, since many errors become moot or harmless by the end;
- **deference** to trial judges;
- prevent **harassment by repeated appeals**;
- one appeal per case, after everything is decided.

**Not final** (just steps toward a judgment):
- **denials** of 12(b) motions;
- **denials** of SJ (see Wininger and Scott for exceptions);
- **partial SJ on liability alone**;
- **discovery orders**;
- an order to **refile** a complaint;
- an order **denying remand** after removal (Caterpillar, Unit IX).

### Reading: Rule 54(b) (CB 676)
**Rule 54(b): Partial final judgment.** In a case with **multiple claims or multiple parties**, the court **may direct entry of a final judgment** as to **one or more, but fewer than all**, claims or parties **only if** it **expressly determines that there is no just reason for delay**.
- Otherwise, an order resolving fewer than all claims or parties **doesn't end the action** and **may be revised** any time before final judgment.
- **It requires that a claim be completely resolved.** It does **not** apply to a **single claim** with multiple remedies (Wetzel).

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)–(b) (CB 677)
**Rule § 1292(a)(1):** Interlocutory orders "**granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions**, or refusing to dissolve or modify injunctions" are **appealable as of right**. This links to Winter (a preliminary injunction) and Rule 65.

**Rule § 1292(b): Certified interlocutory appeal.** It takes **two keys**:
- **(1) The district judge certifies in writing** that the order involves:
  - (a) a **controlling question of law**,
  - (b) with **substantial ground for difference of opinion**,
  - (c) where an immediate appeal **may materially advance the ultimate termination** of the litigation.
- **(2) The court of appeals, in its discretion, permits** the appeal. The application must be made **within 10 days** of the order.
- Filing does **not stay** proceedings unless ordered.
- **In re Text Messaging** came up this way.

### Reading: *Liberty Mutual Insurance Co. v. Wetzel* (CB 678–82)
**Rule:** **Appellate jurisdiction can't be created by the parties' consent or by a district court's say-so.** Courts check it **on their own** (sua sponte). Rule 54(b) **doesn't apply to single-claim actions**.

**Case: Liberty Mutual Ins. Co. v. Wetzel** (U.S. 1976)
- **Facts:** Female employees sued under **Title VII** over pregnancy-related policies, seeking an **injunction, backpay, damages, and fees**. The district court granted **partial SJ on liability only**, withheld the injunction, and **certified it under Rule 54(b)**. The 3d Circuit heard the appeal.
- **Issue:** Was a partial SJ on liability, with remedies unresolved and a Rule 54(b) certification, appealable?
- **Holding:** No. **The Supreme Court raised jurisdiction on its own** and held there was **no appellate jurisdiction**.
  - **Not § 1291:** Partial SJ on liability, with **remedies unresolved**, is interlocutory.
  - **Not Rule 54(b):** It was a **single claim** (one legal theory, one set of facts) with **multiple requested remedies** under Rule 8(a)(3), so the certification was **ineffective**.
  - **Not § 1292(a)(1):** No injunction was actually granted or denied. Even a *de facto* denial would have let only the **plaintiffs** appeal, not Liberty Mutual.
  - **Not § 1292(b):** The district court **didn't certify** under 1292(b), and the 10-day petition to the court of appeals never happened. A 54(b) certification **can't substitute** for 1292(b).
- **Takeaway:** A partial summary judgment on liability that leaves remedies undecided is not a final decision under § 1291, and appellate courts must check their own jurisdiction.

## Class 17 (10/1) — Collateral Order Doctrine

### Reading: *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.* (CB 685–87)
**Rule: The collateral order doctrine.** A "small class" of orders that **don't end the case** still count as "**final decisions**" under § 1291 if they:
1. **conclusively determine** the disputed question;
2. resolve an **important issue completely separate from the merits** ("too important to be denied review and too independent of the cause itself"); and
3. are **effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment**.

**Case: Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.** (U.S. 1949)
- **Facts:** A small shareholder brought a **derivative suit** alleging $100M+ of insider looting. A **new New Jersey statute** required small-stake derivative plaintiffs to **post security** (a bond for the defendants' expenses, here **$125,000**). The district court **refused to apply** it. The defendants appealed immediately.
- **Issue:** Is an order refusing to require security under the state statute immediately appealable even though the case isn't over?
- **Holding:** Yes, appealable.
  - The order was **conclusive**, not tentative.
  - **Whether security is required** is **separate** from whether insiders looted the company.
  - It **can't wait**: by the end of the case, the protection the security provides (against the expenses of litigating) would be **lost**.
  - **Caveat:** Not **every** security order is appealable. A **discretionary** ruling on the **amount** (which can be revisited) is not conclusive. Here the **right** to security was a serious, unsettled legal question.
  - On the merits, the NJ statute applies in federal diversity suits (an Erie issue).
- **Takeaway:** The collateral-order doctrine allows immediate appeal of an order that conclusively decides an important issue separate from the merits and would be effectively unreviewable after final judgment.

### Reading: *Mohawk Industries v. Carpenter* (CB 688–98)
**Rule:** The Cohen test is applied **CATEGORICALLY, to the whole class of orders**, not to the individual case. Disclosure orders adverse to the attorney-client privilege are **not** immediately appealable collateral orders.

**Case: Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter** (U.S. 2009)
- **Facts:** An employee claimed he was fired for reporting that Mohawk employed illegal alien workers. The district court found Mohawk **waived attorney-client privilege** and ordered it to disclose communications about the employee's meeting with counsel. Mohawk sought a collateral-order appeal.
- **Issue:** Are disclosure orders adverse to the attorney-client privilege immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine?
- **Holding:** No.
  - **Prongs 1 (conclusive) and 2 (separate from the merits) were satisfied.** It **failed prong 3** (effectively unreviewable).
  - The question is "whether deferring review until final judgment so imperils the interest as to justify the **cost of allowing immediate appeal of the entire class** of relevant orders." Importance "**in the abstract**" isn't enough.
  - **Why post-judgment review is adequate:** the appellate court can **vacate and order a new trial** without the privileged material (as with any evidentiary error); clients and lawyers won't really be chilled ex ante by the remote chance of an erroneous ruling; most privilege rulings are routine.
  - **Other safety valves** for particularly injurious or novel rulings: (1) **§ 1292(b)** certification; (2) **mandamus**; (3) **defy the order** and accept **Rule 37(b) sanctions** or **contempt**, then appeal; (4) **protective orders** to limit spillover.
  - The collateral order doctrine should stay "**narrow**" and must "**never be allowed to swallow the general rule**." Expansion should come through **rulemaking** (28 U.S.C. §§ 2072(c), 1292(e)), not case law.
  - **Thomas (concurring):** Stop expanding the doctrine judicially. Leave it all to rulemaking.
- **Takeaway:** Disclosure orders adverse to attorney-client privilege are not immediately appealable collateral orders because post-judgment appeal, mandamus, § 1292(b), and contempt provide enough review.

### Reading: *Microsoft I and Microsoft II* (CB 701–04)
**Rule:** An order denying a motion to quash or vacate a subpoena or warrant is **not final**. The established route to review is: **defy, be held in contempt, and appeal the contempt order** (which **is** final).

**Case: United States v. Microsoft Corp. (Microsoft I)** (S.D.N.Y. 2014)
- **Facts:** A **Stored Communications Act warrant** sought a user's emails stored in **Dublin, Ireland**. Microsoft's motion to vacate was **denied**, and it filed a notice of appeal.
- **Issue:** Is an order denying a motion to vacate an SCA warrant immediately appealable?
- **Holding:** No. It is not final; the route to review is contempt.
- **Takeaway:** A discovery order is not a final decision, so a party seeking review must usually defy it and appeal a contempt finding.

**Case: United States v. Microsoft Corp. (Microsoft II)** (S.D.N.Y. 2014)
- **Facts:** The parties **stipulated** that Microsoft wouldn't comply so it could appeal.
- **Issue:** Can the court enter a contempt order to create an appealable final order?
- **Holding:** Yes. The court entered a **contempt order with no sanctions**, purely to create a final order for appeal.
  - Shows **contempt is a genuine, available route** to immediate review. That **supports Mohawk's conclusion** that discovery orders are reviewable through other means, which weighs **against** collateral-order status.
  - Its "empty formality" quality is the discussion point. Contempt has real costs for **individuals** (risk, reputation) and fewer for a big company or the **government**.
- **Takeaway:** A court can enter a contempt order without sanctions just to create a final, appealable order.

# UNIT VII — The Effects of Judgment: Preclusion & Joinder (Classes 18–20)

**Two doctrines under the umbrella of "res judicata":**

| | **Claim preclusion** ("res judicata," "bar," "merger") | **Issue preclusion** ("collateral estoppel") |
|---|---|---|
| Bars | The **whole claim**, including everything that **was or could have been** raised | Only **specific issues actually litigated and necessarily decided** |
| Second suit | The **same claim** | Can be a **different claim** |
| Who's bound | Parties and **privies** | Traditionally parties and privies. Mutuality is now abandoned (Bernhard, Parklane) for **who may invoke** it, but **due process still limits who can be bound** (Hansberry, Taylor). |
| Raised as | An **affirmative defense** (8(c)). The **defendant bears the burden** (Taylor). | Same |

## Class 18 (10/6) — Preclusion

### Reading: Res Judicata (CB 735–38)
**Rule: Claim preclusion** (Petro-Hunt elements; matches lecture notes):
1. The **parties are identical or in privity**;
2. the prior judgment was rendered by a **court of competent jurisdiction**;
3. the prior action ended in a **final judgment on the merits**; and
4. the **same claim or cause of action** is involved in both suits.

It bars a losing plaintiff's **second shot** and a losing defendant's **new defenses after the fact**. It covers what **could have been raised**.

**"Final judgment on the merits":**
- **Counts:** A winner and loser were declared and the dispute ended. This **includes SJ on statute of limitations** (no facts tried, but still "on the merits") and **Rule 41(b)** involuntary dismissals.
- **Doesn't count:** dismissal **without prejudice**, or dismissal for lack of **jurisdiction, venue, or Rule 19 joinder** (41(b) exceptions).

**"Same claim":**
- The **modern transactional test**: the same **"nucleus of operative facts"** or "single group of operative facts," **regardless of legal theory or remedy**. Look at relation in **time, space, origin, and motivation**, whether the claims form a **convenient trial unit**, and the parties' **expectations**.
- **Older** tests: the "same evidence" test and the "same primary right" test.

**Privity:** The nonparty holds an interest in the subject matter that a party held. Example: an **assignee** of a contract claim.

**Rule: Issue preclusion** (Georgia-Pacific elements):
1. The **precise issue** was raised and **actually litigated** in the prior proceeding;
2. its determination was **necessary to the outcome**;
3. the prior proceeding resulted in a **final judgment on the merits**; and
4. the party **against whom** preclusion is sought had a **full and fair opportunity to litigate**.

- Here "final judgment on the merits" refers to **the issue**. A finally decided **jurisdictional or procedural** ruling can be issue-preclusive.

**Remedy for a wrong judgment:** **Appeal it.** Don't bring a new suit (lecture notes).

### Reading: *Rose v. Board of Election Commissioners* (CB 738–42)
**Rule:** Under **28 U.S.C. § 1738**, federal courts give a **state-court judgment the same preclusive effect it would get in that state's courts**. A new legal theory arising from the **same group of operative facts** is barred if it **could have been raised** in the first suit.

**Case: Rose v. Board of Election Comm'rs for City of Chicago** (7th Cir. 2016). **The baseline claim-preclusion case.**
- **Facts:** Rose tried to run for Chicago alderman, but his nominating petitions were challenged and he was kept off the ballot. He sued in **Illinois state court** and lost. He did **not appeal**. He then sued in **federal court** (adding a **§ 1983** constitutional claim) against the same defendants.
- **Issue:** Does the Illinois judgment bar the later federal suit raising a new § 1983 theory?
- **Holding:** Yes, **barred by claim preclusion**.
  - Illinois preclusion law applies (§ 1738).
  - **Same parties, a final judgment on the merits** (under Illinois Rule 273, an involuntary dismissal counts unless it was for jurisdiction, venue, or joinder), and the **same cause of action** under the "**single group of operative facts**" test, **regardless of the new legal theory**.
  - The **§ 1983 claim could have been raised** in state court, since **state courts are presumptively competent** over federal claims.
  - **Full and fair opportunity:** The earlier proceeding met due process minimums. **Choosing not to appeal doesn't make the opportunity illusory.**
  - "Fairness" arguments don't override settled preclusion doctrine.
- **Takeaway:** Claim preclusion bars a later suit arising from the same operative facts as a final judgment between the same parties, even if it raises a new legal theory that could have been brought the first time.

### Reading: *Rush v. City of Maple Heights* (CB 744–51)
**Rule: Rule against claim splitting** (the majority rule). "Where a person suffers both personal injuries and property damage as a result of the same wrongful act, **only a single cause of action** arises." One tort supports one action, so **all damages must be sought in one suit**.

**Case: Rush v. City of Maple Heights** (Ohio 1958)
- **Facts:** Rush fell from a motorcycle because of a **pothole**. **Suit 1** (municipal court) sought **property damage** to the motorcycle; she **won** ($100). **Suit 2** sought **personal injuries**. She used **issue preclusion** on the city's negligence from Suit 1 and won $12,000.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff bring separate suits for property damage and personal injury arising from the same accident?
- **Holding:** No, **Suit 2 is BARRED**. The different injuries are just **separate items of damage**.
  - **Overruled Vasu v. Kohlers**' paragraph 4, which allowed separate suits. That was **dicta**: Vasu actually turned on lack of **privity** between an insured and his insurer.
  - Rejected **Brunsden v. Humphrey**'s "same evidence" test, adopting **Lord Coleridge's dissent**: one act, one moment, one harm. It is "a subtlety not warranted by law" that **torn clothes and torn limbs** make two suits.
  - **Policy:** Prevent **vexatious litigation**. Otherwise a plaintiff could bring a **small** claim first (cheaper for the defendant to lose than to defend), **win**, and then use issue preclusion for a **big** second claim (lecture notes).
  - **Dissent:** **Stability.** People relied on Vasu, so apply the new rule prospectively only.
  - **Ironic twist:** Rush argued **issue** preclusion (offensively). The city won on **claim** preclusion. The **whole claim** has to be asserted the first time.
- **Takeaway:** Property damage and personal injury from the same wrongful act are a single cause of action, so all damages must be sought in one suit.

### Reading: *Cromwell v. County of Sac* (CB 754–57)
**Rule:**
- **(1) Same claim or demand:** The judgment is an "**absolute bar**," covering everything **offered and everything that might have been offered** (claim preclusion).
- **(2) Different claim or demand:** The judgment operates only as an **estoppel** on matters "**actually litigated and determined**," **not** on what "might have been" litigated (issue preclusion).

**Case: Cromwell v. County of Sac** (U.S. 1876). **The foundational statement of the two branches.**
- **Facts:** A county issued **courthouse bonds** that were tainted by fraud (the courthouse was never built). In **Suit 1** (Smith, as a front for Cromwell), suing on **25 coupons**, the county won because the holder **didn't prove he paid value** before maturity. In **Suit 2**, Cromwell sued on **different bonds and coupons** from the same issue and offered to prove he **did** pay value.
- **Issue:** Does the judgment on the first coupons preclude Cromwell from recovering on different bonds and coupons from the same issue?
- **Holding:** Only as to issues actually decided.
  - Cromwell **is estopped** from denying the bonds are **void against the county** except in the hands of a **bona fide holder for value** (that was decided).
  - He is **not** estopped from proving he **paid value for these different bonds**. Failing to prove value for one coupon is "not even presumptive" evidence about another.
  - **Why limit issue preclusion?** Parties may skip issues for **non-merits reasons**: a **small amount at stake**, **difficulty getting evidence**, **expense**, or their situation at the time.
  - **Default judgments** don't support **issue** preclusion on a different claim, because nothing was actually litigated.
  - **Privity:** Cromwell was bound by Smith's suit as the **real party in interest** (it was prosecuted for his "sole use and benefit").
  - First question: **same claim or different claim?** That tells which doctrine applies. Then identify **exactly what was decided**.
- **Takeaway:** Claim preclusion bars everything that was or could have been litigated on the same claim, while issue preclusion on a different claim reaches only issues actually litigated and decided.

## Class 19 (10/7) — Preclusion; Joinder

**Mutuality** (the old rule): Only someone who would have been **bound** by the first judgment could **use** it. Modern law abandons it.
- **Defensive nonmutual:** A **defendant** uses a prior judgment against a **plaintiff** who lost the issue to someone else (Bernhard; Blonder-Tongue).
- **Offensive nonmutual:** A **new plaintiff** uses a prior judgment against a **defendant** who lost the issue to someone else (Parklane, at the court's discretion).
- **Never** bind someone who **wasn't a party or privy** (due process: Hansberry, Taylor).

**Preclusion patterns** (lecture 20 notes, trespass examples):

| First suit | Second suit | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A v. B, **A wins** (B trespassed on A's land) | A v. B again for a new trespass | **B is precluded** on the common issue; A only has to prove it's A's land and B was on it |
| A v. B, **A loses** (the land is B's) | A v. B again for trespass | **A is precluded** on whose land it is |
| A v. B, **B wins** (the land is B's) | A v. C for trespass on the same land | C can use **non-mutual defensive** issue preclusion |
| A v. B, **B loses** | C v. B | **Non-mutual offensive**; allowed if fair to the defendant (Parklane) |
| A v. B, B wins | C v. B on the same issue | C generally **can't** be precluded; preclusion applies only to someone who was in the earlier case and lost |

### Reading: *Bernhard v. Bank of America* (CB 759–63)
**Rule:** **Mutuality is abolished.** Issue preclusion may be asserted by a stranger to the first suit if: (1) the **issue is identical**; (2) there was a **final judgment on the merits**; and (3) the **party AGAINST whom the plea is asserted** was a party or in privity in the prior case.

**Case: Bernhard v. Bank of America** (Cal. 1942)
- **Facts:**
  - Elderly Mrs. Sather's money was moved to an account controlled by **Cook**, who withdrew it.
  - In the **probate accounting**, Bernhard (a beneficiary) objected, but the probate court found Sather had **made a gift** to Cook.
  - Later Bernhard, now **administratrix** of the estate, sued **Bank of America** for paying out the money. The bank pleaded the probate finding.
- **Issue:** May a defendant who wasn't a party to the first suit use issue preclusion against a plaintiff who lost that issue there?
- **Holding:** Yes. The bank may use issue preclusion against Bernhard.
  - Identical issue (ownership of the money); final judgment (the probate order); and Bernhard was effectively a party, litigating the **same right** in a **different capacity** (beneficiary vs. administratrix) and representing the same interests.
  - **Due process** limits **who can be BOUND**, **not who can ASSERT** preclusion. "No satisfactory rationalization" supports mutuality.
  - This is **defensive nonmutual** issue preclusion.
- **Takeaway:** Mutuality is not required, so a nonparty can use issue preclusion defensively against someone who was a party to the earlier case and lost.

### Reading: *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore* (CB 768–73)
**Rule:** Federal courts **may allow offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel**, with **broad discretion**. **Don't allow it** where (1) **the plaintiff could easily have joined** the earlier action, or (2) it would be **unfair to the defendant**:
- **(a)** Small or nominal stakes in suit 1, so **little incentive to litigate vigorously**, especially if future suits weren't **foreseeable**;
- **(b)** The judgment relied on is **inconsistent with earlier judgments** favoring the defendant (the "50th plaintiff" problem);
- **(c)** The second action offers **procedural opportunities unavailable in the first** that **could readily cause a different result**.

**Case: Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore** (U.S. 1979)
- **Facts:** The **SEC** sued Parklane (an equitable action with **no jury**) and won a finding that its **proxy statement was materially false and misleading**. Shareholder **Shore** then brought a **class action for damages** and moved for partial SJ, using that finding **offensively**.
- **Issue:** May a plaintiff who was not a party to the SEC action use the SEC judgment offensively to preclude Parklane from relitigating the issue, and does that violate the Seventh Amendment?
- **Holding:** Yes, estoppel applies, and no Seventh Amendment violation.
  - **Why offensive use is different:** Defensive use encourages joining all defendants up front. Offensive use encourages potential plaintiffs to "**wait and see**" ("everything to gain, nothing to lose"), which can **increase** litigation.
  - **Applied:** Shore **couldn't easily have joined** the SEC suit; the stakes were serious and follow-on suits **foreseeable**, so Parklane had every incentive to fight; there were **no inconsistent judgments**; the only new procedural feature was a **jury**, which isn't likely to cause a different result.
  - **Seventh Amendment:** Precluding relitigation of facts found in a prior **equitable nonjury** proceeding **doesn't violate** the jury right. Common-law preclusion doctrine has evolved since 1791. Connects to Unit V.
  - **Rehnquist (dissent):** It denies a jury and coerces defendants.
- **Takeaway:** Offensive nonmutual issue preclusion is permitted at the trial court's discretion, subject to fairness factors (for example, whether the plaintiff could easily have joined the first suit), and doesn't violate the Seventh Amendment.

**Nonmutual issue preclusion framework:**
1. Run the **four issue-preclusion elements**.
2. **Who is invoking it**, against whom? Is it **offensive or defensive**?
3. **Was the party being bound a party** to suit 1? If not, stop. **Due process forbids it** (Taylor's six exceptions aside).
4. **If offensive, apply the Parklane factors**: could the plaintiff easily have joined, the stakes and foreseeability, inconsistent judgments, new procedural opportunities.

### Reading: Introductory Notes on Joinder; Rules 13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, and 24 (CB 712–21)
**Joinder decides who's a party, which decides who's bound.**

**Rule 13(a): Compulsory counterclaim.** A pleading **must** state any counterclaim that arises out of the **same transaction or occurrence** as the opposing party's claim and doesn't require adding a party the court can't get jurisdiction over. **If omitted, it's lost**, which is preclusion by rule.

**Rule 13(b): Permissive counterclaim.** **Any other** claim against an opposing party.

**Rule 13(g): Crossclaim** against a **co-party**, arising from the same transaction or occurrence. It is **permissive**.

**Rule 13(h):** Additional parties may be added to a counterclaim or crossclaim under Rules 19 and 20.

**Rule 14: Impleader (third-party practice).**
- **14(a)(1):** A defending party may bring in a nonparty "**who is or may be liable to it for all or part of the claim against it**." The liability must be **derivative**: **indemnity, contribution, subrogation**. Impleader can't be used for an "it was him, not me" alternative defendant. **Leave is needed** if it's filed **more than 14 days after serving the original answer**.
- **14(a)(2)–(3):** The third-party defendant may assert defenses and claims. The plaintiff may assert claims against the third-party defendant arising from the same transaction.
- **14(b):** A plaintiff facing a counterclaim may also implead.

**Rule 18(a): Joinder of claims.** A party may join **as many claims as it has** against an opposing party, **related or not**. But **preclusion** (Rush) effectively **forces** joining all transactionally related claims.

**Rule 19: Required parties.**
- **19(a)(1):** A person is required, and **must be joined if feasible** (subject to service and no destruction of subject-matter jurisdiction), if:
  - **(A)** without them the court **can't accord complete relief** among the existing parties; **or**
  - **(B)** they **claim an interest** in the subject, and disposing of the case without them may **(i) impair or impede their ability to protect that interest**, or **(ii)** leave an existing party at **substantial risk of double, multiple, or inconsistent obligations**.
- **19(b):** If joinder **isn't feasible**, decide whether to proceed or dismiss "**in equity and good conscience**," considering:
  1. the **prejudice** to the absent person or existing parties;
  2. whether prejudice can be **lessened** by shaping relief;
  3. whether a judgment without them would be **adequate**;
  4. whether the plaintiff would have an **adequate remedy** if the case were dismissed.
- Failure to join a Rule 19 party is a **12(b)(7)** defense (preserved by **12(h)(2)**).

**Rule 20: Permissive joinder of parties.** Plaintiffs may join together, and defendants may be joined, if:
1. a right to relief is asserted jointly, severally, or in the alternative arising out of the **same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences**; **and**
2. there is **any question of law or fact common** to all.

Under **20(b)** the court may order **separate trials** to prevent prejudice.

**Rule 21:** **Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissing the action.** The court may **add or drop parties** or **sever** claims at any time, on just terms, even on its own. (Also the tool for dropping a dispensable nondiverse party to save diversity jurisdiction: Grupo Dataflux, Unit IX.)

**Rule 24: Intervention.**
- **24(a) Of right:** On a **timely** motion, if the person has an unconditional statutory right, **or** claims an **interest** relating to the property or transaction, the disposition **may impair or impede** their ability to protect it, **and existing parties don't adequately represent** that interest.
- **24(b) Permissive:** On a timely motion, if the person has a conditional statutory right or a **claim or defense sharing a common question of law or fact**. The court considers **undue delay or prejudice**.
- **24(c):** The motion must be accompanied by a **pleading**.

### Reading: *Breaking Glass Pictures v. Does 1–188* (CB 729–32)
**Rule:** Permissive joinder under Rule 20(a)(2) requires the **same transaction, occurrence, or series**; even when it's met, the court may **sever in its discretion** under **Rule 21** to avoid prejudice and unfairness.

**Case: Breaking Glass Pictures, LLC v. Does 1–188** (E.D. Mo. 2013)
- **Facts:** A copyright suit against **188 anonymous BitTorrent users** in **one "swarm"** (over about 9 weeks) who downloaded *6 Degrees of Hell*. The court **sua sponte** considered **misjoinder**.
- **Issue:** Can 188 Doe defendants who participated in the same BitTorrent swarm be joined in one action under Rule 20?
- **Holding:** No. **All but Doe 1 were severed and dismissed without prejudice**; the plaintiff can refile individually.
  - **(1)** Being in the same swarm isn't the **same transaction, occurrence, or series**. BitTorrent doesn't require any Doe to have **contributed to any other Doe's** download, and the activity spanned **9 weeks**. The court adopted the *Hard Drive Productions* view, noting a **circuit split** among district courts.
  - **(2) Alternative:** **Even if Rule 20(a) were satisfied**, the court may sever under **Rule 21**. With 188 defendants, each could attend every deposition and must serve every other defendant, so discovery and a variety of defenses would be **unwieldy**.
  - **Policy subtext:** "Copyright troll" suits use mass joinder to cheaply subpoena ISPs and extract settlements.
  - Permissive joinder is a **balance**: **efficiency and consistency** vs. **fairness and manageability**. Rule 20 is construed **liberally**, but courts keep **discretion** under Rules 20(b) and 21.
- **Takeaway:** Participating in the same BitTorrent swarm is not the same transaction or occurrence under Rule 20, and even when Rule 20 is met a court may sever defendants under Rule 21 for manageability.

## Class 20 (10/8) — Preclusion & Representation

### Reading: Preclusion and Representation (CB 775)
- **Default rule:** **Joinder defines who's bound.** Binding a **nonparticipant** is the "taboo," the **outer limit** of preclusion.
- **The two frontier questions:**
  - **Taylor v. Sturgell:** When does a judgment bind people who weren't joined, **outside** a class action?
  - **Hansberry v. Lee:** When does a **class action** judgment **fail to bind** people within the defined class?

### Reading: *Taylor v. Sturgell* (CB 776–87)
**Rule: General rule (Hansberry).** "One is not bound by a judgment in personam in a litigation in which he is not designated as a party or to which he has not been made a party by service of process." This reflects the "deep-rooted historic tradition that **everyone should have his own day in court**."
- **Federal common law** governs the preclusive effect of **federal** judgments (Semtek).

**The SIX exceptions to the rule against nonparty preclusion:**
1. **Agreement:** The nonparty **agreed to be bound** by the determination.
2. **Pre-existing substantive legal relationship:** e.g., preceding and succeeding **owners of property**, **bailee and bailor**, **assignor and assignee**. This is traditional "privity."
3. **Adequate representation** in certain limited circumstances: properly conducted **class actions**, and suits by **trustees, guardians, and other fiduciaries**.
   - **Minimum requirements (Hansberry; Richards):** (a) **interests aligned**, **and** (b) either the party **understood itself to be acting in a representative capacity** or the original court **took care to protect** the nonparty's interests. **Sometimes** (c) **notice** is also required.
4. **Control:** The nonparty **assumed control** over the earlier litigation and had its day in court.
5. **Proxy:** A nonparty relitigates **through a proxy**, i.e., a **designated representative or agent** of the party bound.
6. **Special statutory scheme:** e.g., **bankruptcy** or **probate**, if consistent with due process.

**Case: Taylor v. Sturgell** (U.S. 2008)
- **Facts:**
  - **Herrick** filed a **FOIA** request for the FAA's technical documents on a vintage **F-45 airplane** and **lost** (trade-secret exemption). The court said he made two good arguments but couldn't restore trade-secret protection that was lost, and raised one too late.
  - His **friend Taylor** (of the same Antique Aircraft Association, represented by the **same lawyer**) then requested the **same documents** and sued in D.C., making the same arguments plus two new ones Herrick never pressed.
  - The D.C. Circuit barred Taylor's suit under "**virtual representation**" (a multi-factor "close enough" test).
- **Issue:** Can a nonparty be bound by a prior judgment under a theory of "virtual representation"?
- **Holding:** No. **"Virtual representation" is REJECTED.** **Remanded** to decide whether Taylor is acting as Herrick's **agent (exception 5)**.
  - **Why "virtual representation" fails:** (1) it conflicts with the **fundamental** rule and the "constrained approach" of **discrete exceptions**; (2) it would create "**de facto class actions at will**," bypassing **Rule 23's** protections (notice, adequacy, certification); (3) it is unworkable: there is no firm guidance, and it invites costly discovery. "**Crisp rules with sharp corners**" beat "opaque standards."
  - **No special rule for "public law" (FOIA) cases.** FOIA relief goes to the requester; **stare decisis** handles repetitive suits on the same legal question; any limits on repeat suits should come from **Congress**.
  - **Burden:** Claim preclusion is an **affirmative defense** (8(c)). **The defendant must prove** that Taylor is Herrick's **agent or proxy**. The burden doesn't shift to the plaintiff. Defendants can use discovery to fill information gaps.
- **Takeaway:** Nonparty preclusion is limited to six recognized exceptions, and the open-ended "virtual representation" theory is rejected.

**Applying the six exceptions to Taylor** (lecture 20 notes):

| # | Exception | Applies? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Agreement to be bound | No | No sign that Taylor agreed to be bound |
| 2 | Substantive legal relationship | No | The two men have "no legal relationship" |
| 3 | Adequate representation | No | Fails the Richards requirements |
| 4 | Control of the earlier suit | No | No evidence Taylor controlled Herrick's suit |
| 5 | Proxy / agent | Unresolved | Remanded to decide whether Taylor is Herrick's agent |
| 6 | Special statutory scheme | No | FOIA puts no limit on repeat suits |

- When someone not in suit 1 is said to be bound, walk through all six categories. "Same interests," "friends," or "same lawyer" **alone** fail.

### Reading: *Hansberry v. Lee* (CB 789–94)
**Rule:** Absent class members **can** be bound by a class judgment, but **only if they're adequately represented** by parties with the **same interests**, under a procedure that **fairly ensures protection** of absent parties' interests. Otherwise binding nonparties violates **due process**, and the judgment isn't entitled to **full faith and credit**.

**Case: Hansberry v. Lee** (U.S. 1940)
- **Facts:**
  - A Chicago **racially restrictive covenant** would take effect only if owners of **95% of the frontage** signed.
  - In the earlier case, **Burke v. Kleiman** (a "class" suit by one owner "for herself and others similarly situated"), the parties **falsely stipulated** that 95% had signed, and the covenant was enforced.
  - Later the **Hansberrys** (a Black family; Carl Hansberry was Lorraine Hansberry's father) bought a home there. Neighbors sued to enforce the covenant and argued **Burke was res judicata** on the 95% issue. The Illinois courts agreed, even though they found the stipulation was false.
- **Issue:** Does due process permit binding the Hansberrys and their seller to the Burke judgment as members of the "class"?
- **Holding:** No. **Due process forbids binding the Hansberrys** (and their seller) by the Burke judgment.
  - **Conflicting interests:** The covenant gave signers **both rights and obligations**. Those trying to **enforce** it (the Burke plaintiffs) **can't represent** those who want to **resist** it. Owners "**free alternatively either to assert rights or to challenge them**" **aren't a single class**.
  - **The representation must be assumed in the first suit.** Burke didn't designate a defendant class or seek relief beyond the named parties, and its decree didn't purport to bind others. A later court can't retroactively treat parties as representatives.
  - There was a risk of **fraudulent or collusive** sacrifice of absent parties' rights (the false stipulation).
  - **Legacy:** The **constitutional foundation of adequate representation** in class actions (Rule 23(a)(4)), cited by Taylor as exception #3's minimum requirements. Leads into **Rule 23** (Unit VIII).
- **Takeaway:** Due process forbids binding absent parties to a judgment in a class-style suit unless their interests were adequately represented.

# UNIT VIII — Settlement, ADR & Class Actions (Classes 22–23)

## Class 22 (10/14) — Settlement & ADR; Introduction to Rule 23

### Reading: Settlement and ADR (CB 1307–20)
**Rule 1:** The Rules aim at the "**just, speedy, and inexpensive** determination of every action." It is an aspiration, not a guarantee, and the three goals can conflict. It's the yardstick for judging any dispute-resolution process, litigation or ADR.
- Two course themes tie in: (1) "**procedure is power**," since lawyers can use procedure to serve clients, themselves, or society; and (2) parties are entitled to due process, "but **how much process is due?**"

**Rule: ADR isn't necessarily an "alternative."** ADR "can be part of the litigation process." Mediation can happen mid-case (by consent or court order), and a judge may order arbitration of a filed case based on a pre-existing agreement to arbitrate.
- **Why ADR exists:** It developed as a response to the shortcomings of formal litigation: **formality, expense, and emotional toll**, which are "interrelated and mutually reinforcing."

**Types of ADR** (lecture 22 notes):
- **Settlement:** Resolution by mutual agreement through **negotiation**, with no decisionmaker choosing a winner. "The most common and most important form of ADR."
- **Mediation:** A special form of settlement negotiation in which a **neutral third party** with no interest in the case helps the parties reach agreement but **does not bind** them. Even court-ordered "mandatory" mediation is just guided settlement negotiation.
- **Platform procedure:** Professor Rory Van Loo's term for the dispute-resolution processes online platforms (eBay, Facebook, Instagram, Airbnb, YouTube, Amazon) use to resolve disputes among users under the **platform's own rules**. Often the first, and in many cases the only, step.
- **Arbitration:** The parties **contractually agree to be bound** by the decision (the award) of a third party (the arbitrator).

**Settlement economics:**
- **Expected value** of a claim = the amount of the claim, discounted by the probability the plaintiff will lose. A "fair" settlement roughly tracks expected value while saving both sides litigation costs.
- **Risk aversion and unequal resources** can push a party into a settlement "highly unfavorable" relative to the merits. When injurers pay only a fraction of expected liability, it **weakens deterrence**.
- **"Lumping it":** the most common form of settlement, i.e., not pursuing a claim at all because cost is too high or recovery too unlikely. A claim that costs more to pursue than its expected recovery is a **negative expected value claim** (the reason (b)(3) class actions exist).

**Mediation:** Parties fail to settle because of **distrust** or because both overestimate their own case (**optimism bias**). A trusted mediator only needs "enough common ground to make settlement possible."

**Arbitration:**
- **A creature of contract:** "Just as one has a right to a 'day in court,' one also has the right to agree not to have a day in court." The parties choose the forum, decisionmaker, and procedures. Arbitration is **not subject to the FRCP**.
- **Mandatory (pre-dispute) arbitration clauses** reflect "not only a decision to pursue ADR, but a decision to forgo litigation." Not all arbitration agreements are mandatory or made before a dispute arises.
- **Federal Arbitration Act** (and state analogs): a party can go to court to **enforce** an arbitration agreement or award, or to **vacate** an award. The statutes **favor enforcement**, and the grounds for undoing an award are **strictly limited**.
- **Consumer vs. commercial arbitration:** Business-to-business arbitration is less controversial. Consumer arbitration is usually imposed through take-it-or-leave-it "terms of sale" or "terms of use" (**contracts of adhesion**).

### Reading: Rule 23 (CB 802–10)
**Purpose:** The Advisory Committee "had dominantly in mind vindication of '**the rights of groups of people who individually would be without effective strength to bring their opponents into court at all**'" (the (b)(3) damages class). Since the 1966 revision, class practice has grown "ever more 'adventuresome'" to handle claims "too numerous to secure their 'just, speedy, and inexpensive determination' one by one."

**Rule 23(a): Prerequisites.** One or more members may sue or be sued as representatives "**only if**" all four are met:
1. **Numerosity:** the class is "so numerous that joinder of all members is **impracticable**." Around **40** is usually enough (lecture 23 notes).
2. **Commonality:** there are "**questions of law or fact common** to the class."
3. **Typicality:** the representatives' claims or defenses are "**typical** of the claims or defenses of the class."
4. **Adequacy:** the representatives "will **fairly and adequately protect** the interests of the class" (the Hansberry requirement).

**Rule 23(b): Types of class actions.** Rule 23(a) **plus** one of:
- **(b)(1)(A) Incompatible standards:** separate actions risk "inconsistent or varying adjudications" that would establish **incompatible standards of conduct** for the party opposing the class.
- **(b)(1)(B) Impairment of interests:** separate actions would, as a practical matter, be dispositive of or **impair** the interests of absent members (e.g., there's only one **limited fund** to divide up).
- **(b)(2) Injunctive or declaratory relief:** the opposing party "has acted or refused to act on grounds that apply generally to the class," so **injunctive or declaratory relief** for the class as a whole is appropriate.
- **(b)(3) Damages:** common questions **predominate** over individual ones, **and** a class action is **superior** to other methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy. Members can **opt out**.

**Rule 23(c): Certification and notice.**
- **(c)(1)(A):** "At an early practicable time," the court must decide by order whether to certify.
- **(c)(1)(B):** The certification order must **define the class** and the class claims, issues, or defenses, and **appoint class counsel** under 23(g).
- **(c)(2)(A):** For (b)(1) or (b)(2) classes, the court **may** direct appropriate notice.
- **(c)(2)(B):** For (b)(3) classes, the court **must** direct "**the best notice that is practicable** under the circumstances, including **individual notice** to all members who can be identified through reasonable effort." Members may **opt out**.
- **(c)(4):** **Issue classes** for particular issues. **(c)(5):** **Subclasses**, each treated as a class.

**Rule 23(e): Settlement.** Class claims "may be settled, voluntarily dismissed, or compromised **only with the court's approval**."
- **(e)(1):** Notice of a proposed settlement "in a reasonable manner" to all members who would be bound.
- **(e)(2):** Approval only after a **hearing** and a finding that the deal is "**fair, reasonable, and adequate**," considering adequate representation and **arm's-length** negotiation. The point is to stop the named plaintiff and class counsel from negotiating a deal that helps them but not the rest of the class.
- **(e)(3):** The parties must file a statement identifying any **side agreements** made in connection with the proposal.
- **(e)(4):** For a (b)(3) class, the court may require a **second opt-out** opportunity at settlement.
- **(e)(5):** Class members may **object** (with specificity). No payment to objectors for dropping objections or appeals without court approval.

**Rule 23(f): Interlocutory appeal.** The court of appeals "**may permit** an appeal from an order granting or denying class-action certification" on a petition filed within **14 days** (45 if the U.S. is a party). It does **not stay** proceedings unless ordered.

**Rule 23(g): Class counsel.** A court that certifies a class **must appoint class counsel** and consider counsel's adequacy. With more than one adequate applicant, appoint the one "**best able** to represent the interests of the class." The court may designate **interim counsel** before certification. Class counsel "must fairly and adequately represent the interests of the class."

**Rule 23(h): Fees.** In a certified class action, the court may award **reasonable attorney's fees** and nontaxable costs. Counsel must move for fees; class members or the defendant may object.

## Class 23 (10/15) — Class Actions

### Reading: *In re Whirlpool Corp. Front-Loading Washer Products Liability Litigation* (CB 812–24)
**Rule: Certification standards.**
- The class action is "**an exception to the usual rule** that litigation is conducted by and on behalf of the individual named parties only" (Wal-Mart v. Dukes).
- **Burden:** The plaintiffs must prove the Rule 23 prerequisites are met.
- **Rigorous analysis (Dukes; Amgen):** The court may "probe behind the pleadings," but Rule 23 grants "no license to engage in **free-ranging merits inquiries**" at certification. Merits questions matter only to the extent they bear on Rule 23.
- **Commonality (Dukes):** Class members must have "suffered the same injury," and their claims must depend on a **common contention** capable of classwide resolution, meaning its truth or falsity "will resolve an issue that is central to the validity of each one of the claims **in one stroke**." One such question is enough.
- **Spencer's three steps for commonality:** (1) assume each plaintiff proceeds separately; (2) identify all legal and factual questions in adjudicating each claim; (3) ask whether a question would **recur in each action**.
- **Typicality:** Members' claims are "fairly encompassed by the named plaintiffs' claims," so that by pursuing their own interests the representatives also advocate for the class.
- **Predominance (Amgen):** Rule 23(b)(3) does **not** require that each element be susceptible to classwide proof; common questions just have to **predominate**.
- **"Fatal similarity" vs. "fatal dissimilarity" (Amgen):** A dissimilarity among members that makes the class unfair or inefficient bears on certification. A failure of proof common to **all** members goes to trial or SJ, not certification.
- **Individual damages:** "A class may be certified for **liability purposes only**, leaving individual damages calculations to subsequent proceedings."
- **Owners without the defect yet (Daffin):** A product-defect class may include owners who never experienced the defect if all were "injured at the **point of sale** upon paying a premium price."
- **Standard of review:** Certification is reviewed for **abuse of discretion**.
- **23(c)(2)(B)(v):** A (b)(3) member who wants to control their own litigation may **opt out**. The judgment **binds everyone who stays in**.

**Case: Glazer v. Whirlpool Corp. (In re Whirlpool Corp. Front-Loading Washer Prods. Liab. Litig.)** (6th Cir. 2013)
- **Facts:**
  - Whirlpool made Duet front-loading washers on two nearly identical platforms (21 models, mostly cosmetic differences).
  - Plaintiffs alleged a common **design defect**: the machines don't clean their own parts, so **biofilm** builds up and mold grows. Glazer and Allison both saw mold within months despite different laundry habits.
  - Internal documents showed Whirlpool knew of the problem across both platforms from 2003–2004, used "biofilm" to avoid alarming consumers, and had 1.3 million complaint calls by late 2006.
  - The district court certified a **liability-only (b)(3) class** of Ohio buyers for tortious breach of warranty, negligent design, and negligent failure to warn, leaving damages for individual determination.
  - Whirlpool took a **Rule 23(f)** appeal, arguing model differences and laundry habits defeat commonality, the class includes satisfied owners who never saw mold, and the named plaintiffs are atypical.
- **Issue:** Did the district court abuse its discretion in certifying a (b)(3) liability-only class despite model differences, varied consumer habits, owners who never had mold, and individualized damages?
- **Holding:** No, **affirmed**. The class satisfies all four Rule 23(a) requirements plus predominance and superiority.
  - Whether the design defect **proximately causes mold** and whether Whirlpool **adequately warned** are common questions that resolve liability "**in one stroke**."
  - Including owners who haven't seen mold is fine on a **point-of-sale** injury theory (premium price for a defective design).
  - **Individualized damages don't defeat** a liability-only class.
  - **Superiority:** individual suits are unlikely when "the cost of litigation would dwarf any potential recovery," and anyone who wants control can opt out. If Whirlpool wins, it gets a judgment binding everyone who stays in.
- **Takeaway:** A class can be certified when a common question (like a common design defect) predominates over individual damages issues, and individual suits would cost more than they could recover.

# UNIT IX — Subject Matter Jurisdiction (Classes 24–29)

**Jurisdiction** is the power of a court to determine the rights of the parties. A court needs **both**:
- **Subject matter jurisdiction (SMJ):** power over the **claims**.
- **Personal jurisdiction:** power over the **parties**.

**SMJ roadmap for federal court:**
1. **Article III** sets the **ceiling**. **A statute** must also grant jurisdiction (two-step).
2. **Federal question (§ 1331):** does the claim **arise under** federal law? (well-pleaded complaint rule; creation test; Grable/Gunn)
3. **Diversity (§ 1332):** **complete diversity** + amount in controversy **exceeds $75,000**, measured at the **time of filing**.
4. **Supplemental (§ 1367):** for claims with no independent basis, are they part of the **same case or controversy**? Does **§ 1367(b)** take it away in a diversity-only case? Will the court **decline** under (c)?
5. **Removal (§§ 1441, 1446, 1447):** could the case have been filed in federal court originally? **Forum-defendant rule**, **30-day** window, **1-year** limit for diversity removals.
6. SMJ **can't be waived or consented to**, and courts must check it **on their own** at any time (Rule 12(h)(3)).

## Class 24 (10/20) — Introduction to Subject Matter Jurisdiction; Diversity Jurisdiction

### Reading: U.S. Constitution Article III (CB 860–63)
**Rule: Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction.** Just as Article I limits Congress and Article II limits the President, **Article III enumerates and limits the judicial power**. Federal courts "cannot simply decide any case that comes before them."

**Rule: Threshold determination.** When a case is filed in federal court, the court "must first determine whether the case falls within the subject matter jurisdiction of the court." If not, it **must dismiss**.

**Art. III, § 1:** The judicial power "shall be vested in **one supreme Court**, and in such **inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish**."
- Congress may create, or not create, lower federal courts, so "the lower federal courts have **no jurisdiction except the jurisdiction granted to them by statute**."
- Without federal district courts, people would have to file in state court and then appeal to the Supreme Court (lecture 24 notes).

**Art. III, § 2, cl. 1:** The judicial power extends to the listed cases and controversies (including cases "**arising under** this Constitution, the Laws of the United States, and Treaties," and controversies "**between Citizens of different States**"). These are the **outer limits** of federal SMJ.

**Art. III, § 2, cl. 2:** The Supreme Court has **original** jurisdiction in cases "affecting Ambassadors, other public Ministers and Consuls, and those in which a State shall be Party." Everywhere else it has **appellate** jurisdiction, "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make."

**Rule: Two-step test.** "It is not enough that a case before a given federal court is within the 'judicial Power of the United States,' as defined by Article III; it must also be within a **statutory grant** of jurisdiction from Congress."
- **Congress may not exceed the Article III ceiling.** A statutory grant beyond Article III "is therefore invalid" (Marbury v. Madison).
- **Congress need not grant the full Article III power** to the lower federal courts.

**Rule: State courts are courts of general jurisdiction.** They existed before the Constitution and can hear "any type of case that comes before them unless their jurisdiction is taken away by statute or by state constitution." State courts can generally hear federal claims (Rose).

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331, 1332 (CB 863–64)
**Rule § 1331:** "The district courts shall have original jurisdiction of all civil actions **arising under** the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States." No amount-in-controversy requirement (since 1980).

**Rule § 1332(a):** "The district courts shall have original jurisdiction of all civil actions where the matter in controversy **exceeds the sum or value of $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs**," and is between:
1. **citizens of different States**;
2. **citizens of a State and citizens or subjects of a foreign state** (alienage);
3. **citizens of different States**, with citizens or subjects of a foreign state as **additional parties**.

**Citizenship of a natural person** (lecture 24 notes): a person is a citizen of a State if they are (1) a **U.S. citizen** **and** (2) **domiciled** in that State.
- A U.S. citizen **domiciled abroad** is not a citizen of any State, so they can't sue or be sued in diversity (lecture 25 notes, footnote 32).

### Reading: *Strawbridge v. Curtiss* (CB 872)
**Rule: Complete diversity.** "Each distinct interest should be represented by persons, all of whom are entitled to sue, or may be sued, in the federal courts." **No plaintiff may share citizenship with any defendant.** (This is a reading of the **statute**; Article III requires only **minimal** diversity.)

**Case: Strawbridge v. Curtiss** (U.S. 1806)
- **Facts:** The complainants filed a bill in chancery in federal circuit court in Massachusetts. Some complainants were Massachusetts citizens. All defendants were Massachusetts citizens **except Curtiss**, a Vermont citizen served in Vermont. So Massachusetts citizens were on **both sides**.
- **Issue:** Does the federal court have diversity jurisdiction when some plaintiffs and some defendants are citizens of the same state, even though one defendant is from a different state?
- **Holding:** No, jurisdiction "cannot be supported." Every person concerned in each distinct interest must be competent to sue, or liable to be sued, in federal court. With Massachusetts citizens on both sides, that wasn't met.
- **Takeaway:** Diversity jurisdiction requires complete diversity, meaning no plaintiff can share citizenship with any defendant.

### Reading: *ConnectU LLC v. Zuckerberg* (CB 877–83)
**Rule: Domicile.** A person's domicile is "the place where he has a **true, fixed home and principal establishment**, and to which, whenever he is absent he has the **intention of returning**."
- **Residence alone is not domicile.**
- **A person remains domiciled in a state until establishing domicile elsewhere.**
- **Factors:** where civil and political rights are exercised, taxes paid, real and personal property (furniture, cars) located, driver's and other licenses obtained, bank accounts kept, club and church memberships, and place of business or employment.

**Rule: Time-of-filing rule.** "The jurisdiction of the Court depends upon the state of things at the time of the action brought." Every diversity challenge, whether "shortly after filing, after the trial, or even for the first time on appeal," is measured against the facts **at filing**.
- A **12(b)(1)** motion can challenge diversity as a matter of fact. On disputed jurisdictional facts, the court may allow **limited discovery**, hold an **evidentiary hearing**, and make its own findings (including credibility).

**Case: ConnectU LLC v. Zuckerberg** (D. Mass. 2007)
- **Facts:**
  - ConnectU, a New York citizen, filed on **September 2, 2004**, relying only on diversity.
  - Zuckerberg grew up in Dobbs Ferry, New York. After spring 2004 he left his things in his Harvard dorm, took a leave of absence, and moved with one bag into a **shared student summer sublet in Palo Alto**.
  - On the filing date he intended to stay in California "for a semester or maybe two" and then return to Harvard.
  - He gave Dobbs Ferry as his permanent address and had a **New York driver's license** and only New York bank accounts. He was registered to vote in New York but had never voted.
  - Facts **after filing** pointed to California (a one-year Los Altos lease, a car, a California license in 2006, no plans to return to Harvard).
- **Issue:** Was Zuckerberg domiciled in New York or California on September 2, 2004, which decides whether diversity existed with New York's ConnectU?
- **Holding:** **New York.** California was not yet his "true, fixed home" to which he intended to return, so there was **no diversity** and no SMJ over ConnectU's original complaint.
  - Post-filing facts don't count (time-of-filing rule).
- **Takeaway:** A person's domicile does not change until the person is physically present in a new state with intent to remain there indefinitely, and domicile is measured when the complaint is filed.

### Reading: *Ceglia v. Zuckerberg* (CB 885–89)
**Rule: Change of domicile.** Requires **(1) residence in a new state** and **(2) intent to remain there indefinitely**.
- A person can have many residences but **only one domicile**.
- Domicile is **presumed to continue** until changed. The party asserting a change must prove it by **clear and convincing evidence**.
- Intent is judged from **objective indicators** under the **totality of the circumstances**; no single factor is conclusive.
- If the evidence is overwhelming, the court can decide domicile **on the papers**, without discovery or a hearing.

**Case: Ceglia v. Zuckerberg** (W.D.N.Y. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - Ceglia, a New York domiciliary, claimed an **84% interest in Facebook** under a 2003 contract and sued in New York state court; defendants removed on diversity.
  - In ConnectU, Zuckerberg had been found domiciled in New York as of September 2004.
  - By the **June 30, 2010** filing: continuous California residence since 2004, California taxes since 2004, California voter registration and voting since 2007, a California driver's license (2006) and car (2007), a rented Palo Alto apartment near Facebook's headquarters, and a sworn affidavit that he intends to stay in California indefinitely. His parents still lived in New York.
  - Ceglia argued Zuckerberg was still a New Yorker and invoked ConnectU (judicial estoppel / issue preclusion).
- **Issue:** Had Zuckerberg changed his domicile from New York to California by June 30, 2010, so that complete diversity existed?
- **Holding:** Yes. Zuckerberg proved the change by **clear and convincing evidence**; complete diversity exists, so the **motion to remand was denied**. No hearing was needed because the evidence was overwhelming.
  - ConnectU doesn't control: it decided domicile in **2004**, and domicile is measured at each filing date. Neither **judicial estoppel** (holding a party to a position it won on) nor **issue preclusion** applies to a different date.
- **Takeaway:** The party claiming a change of domicile must prove it by clear and convincing evidence, considering objective factors like residence, property, voter registration, and taxes.

**Domicile factors** (lecture 24 notes): residence, voter registration, location of property, place of employment, driver's license, car registration, taxes, bank statements.

## Class 25 (10/21) — Diversity Jurisdiction: Corporations, Associations, Amount in Controversy

### Reading: *Hertz Corp. v. Friend* (CB 892–900)
**Rule § 1332(c)(1):** "A corporation shall be deemed to be a citizen of **every State and foreign state by which it has been incorporated** and of the State or foreign state where it has its **principal place of business**." So a corporation has up to **two** domiciles: state(s) of incorporation and **one** principal place of business.

**Rule: Nerve center test.** The principal place of business is "the place where a corporation's **officers direct, control, and coordinate** the corporation's activities." Normally this is the **headquarters**, provided it's the actual center of direction, control, and coordination.
- **Anti-manipulation:** If the "nerve center" is "nothing more than a **mail drop box**, a bare office with a computer, or the location of an annual executive retreat," the court looks to the place of **actual** direction and control.
- **Burden:** The party asserting diversity bears the burden of persuasion and must support jurisdictional allegations with **competent proof**.
- Courts have an **independent obligation** to determine SMJ even when no party challenges it.

**Case: Hertz Corp. v. Friend** (U.S. 2010)
- **Facts:**
  - California citizens sued Hertz in California state court for violating California wage-and-hour laws, on behalf of a California class. **Hertz removed** on diversity.
  - The plaintiffs argued Hertz was a California citizen, which would defeat diversity.
  - Hertz's declaration: it operated in 44 states. California accounted for about 273 of 1,606 locations, 2,300 of 11,230 employees, and $811 million of $4.371 billion in revenue (California is about 12% of the U.S. population). Leadership was at headquarters in **Park Ridge, New Jersey**.
  - The Ninth Circuit applied a **business activities** test and found California was the principal place of business.
- **Issue:** Is a corporation's "principal place of business" found by measuring business activity state by state, or is it the place where high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate the corporation?
- **Holding:** The **nerve center**. Hertz's nerve center was in **New Jersey**, not California. Vacated and remanded.
  - **Administrative simplicity** is "a major virtue in a jurisdictional statute"; occasionally counterintuitive results are the price of avoiding complex jurisdictional tests.
  - A purpose-based approach (find the state where the corporation is least likely to face **local prejudice**) "seems doomed to failure," because prejudice turns on things courts "cannot easily measure," such as image, history, and advertising.
  - **Before Hertz:** courts weighed business activity, nerve center, and "center of gravity." **After Hertz:** one principal place of business, the nerve center (lecture 25 notes).
- **Takeaway:** A corporation's principal place of business under § 1332(c)(1) is its nerve center, meaning where its high-level officers direct, control, and coordinate its activities.

### Reading: *Lincoln Benefit Life Co. v. AEI Life, LLC* (CB 902–10)
**Rule: Citizenship of unincorporated associations.** Partnerships and LLCs "are not considered 'citizens'"; their citizenship is that of **every partner or member**, so "all of the LLC's members must be diverse from all parties on the opposing side." (A REIT likewise takes the citizenship of its members, including shareholders.)
- **Rule 12(b)(1) facial vs. factual attacks:** A **facial** attack tests the pleading, viewing the allegations favorably to the plaintiff. A **factual** attack challenges the actual jurisdictional facts; the plaintiff may respond with evidence and the court weighs it.
- **Rule 8(a)(1)** requires only "a short and plain statement of the grounds for the court's jurisdiction."
- **Negative allegation of diversity:** A plaintiff may allege that the defendant is **not a citizen of the plaintiff's state**, if based on a good-faith, reasonable investigation (Rule 11 reasonable inquiry).

**Case: Lincoln Benefit Life Co. v. AEI Life, LLC** (3d Cir. 2015)
- **Facts:**
  - Lincoln Benefit (Nebraska) sued for a declaratory judgment voiding two **$6.65 million** life insurance policies allegedly procured in a **STOLI** scheme for investors with no insurable interest.
  - Two defendants were **LLCs**. The complaint alleged "upon information and belief" that AEI Life was a New York citizen and ALS Capital a Delaware citizen, but **didn't identify the LLCs' members or their citizenship**.
  - Counsel searched public databases, dockets, and the New York Secretary of State and couldn't identify the members. No defendant claimed to be a Nebraska citizen.
  - The district court dismissed for failure to properly allege diversity.
- **Issue:** Must a plaintiff affirmatively allege the citizenship of each member of a defendant LLC to survive a facial 12(b)(1) challenge, or is a good-faith allegation that no member shares the plaintiff's citizenship enough?
- **Holding:** The good-faith allegation is enough. **Vacated and remanded.**
  - A plaintiff need not affirmatively allege each member's citizenship. If, **after a reasonable attempt** to identify the members, it can allege in good faith that it is diverse from all of them, the complaint survives a **facial** challenge.
  - If the defendant then mounts a **factual** challenge, the plaintiff is entitled to **limited jurisdictional discovery**.
  - Policy: LLC membership information is often not public, and the members' citizenship is known to the defendant, not the plaintiff.
- **Takeaway:** A plaintiff may allege on information and belief that an LLC's members are diverse when, after a reasonable inquiry, their citizenship is not public, and an LLC takes the citizenship of every member.

### Reading: *A.F.A. Tours, Inc. v. Whitchurch* (CB 912–18)
**Rule: Legal certainty test** (St. Paul Mercury). "Unless the law gives a different rule, the sum claimed by the plaintiff controls if the claim is apparently made in **good faith**." Dismissal requires that it "**appear to a legal certainty** that the claim is really for less than the jurisdictional amount."
- Before dismissing, the court must give the plaintiff an "**appropriate and reasonable opportunity to show good faith**" in believing recovery above the minimum is reasonably possible.
- **Punitive damages** count toward the amount if the controlling law permits them.
- **Injunctive relief** is valued by "the right [the plaintiff] seeks to protect" and "the extent of the impairment to be prevented by the injunction," including **potential future harm**, not just past losses.

**Case: A.F.A. Tours, Inc. v. Whitchurch** (2d Cir. 1991)
- **Facts:**
  - AFA ran deluxe tours to Australia, New Zealand, and New Guinea and treated its customer list as a **trade secret** (more than $1,000,000 to build, password-protected).
  - Whitchurch, AFA's exclusive escort for the region from 1972 to 1989, resigned, started a competing business, and solicited 100–200 former AFA customers for a 28-day tour. Only two signed up and he canceled, but he invited recipients onto his list for future tours.
  - AFA sought an **injunction**, compensatory damages "believed to exceed" $50,000, and at least **$250,000 in punitive damages**. The jurisdictional minimum was then **$50,000**.
  - A 28-day deluxe tour could cost about $10,000 per person, and the record showed many **repeat customers**.
  - The district court raised the amount sua sponte at the SJ hearing and dismissed.
- **Issue:** Was dismissal for failing to meet the $50,000 amount in controversy proper when AFA had no chance to present evidence and the record supported future damages, injunctive value, and punitive damages above $50,000?
- **Holding:** No, the dismissal was improper. **Vacated and remanded.**
  - AFA wasn't given a proper opportunity to show good faith.
  - In any event, the record did not show **to a legal certainty** that the claims were worth $50,000 or less: lost **future** profits from repeat customers, the **value of the injunction**, and **punitive damages** (available under New York law for "gross and wanton" misappropriation) could exceed the minimum.
- **Takeaway:** The amount in controversy is judged by the plaintiff's good-faith claim, and a case is dismissed only if it appears to a legal certainty that the plaintiff cannot recover more than $75,000.

### Reading: *Freeland v. Liberty Mutual Fire Insurance Co.* (CB 919–22)
**Rule:** The amount in controversy must **exceed** $75,000, so **exactly $75,000** is "one penny short."
- In **declaratory or injunctive** actions, the amount is "the **value of the object of the litigation**," i.e., "the value of the consequences which may result from the litigation."
- An amount the defendant **concedes** and the plaintiff "will keep even if they lose" is **not in controversy** and is subtracted.
- A **settlement offer** doesn't reduce the amount in controversy.
- Courts have an **independent obligation** to raise SMJ sua sponte. If a removed case lacks SMJ, the court **vacates and remands to state court**.

**Case: Freeland v. Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co.** (6th Cir. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - The Freelands' auto policy had $100,000 bodily-injury coverage but UM/UIM limits of **$25,000 per accident**. They claimed the UM/UIM selection form lacked required disclosures, which under Ohio law would raise UM/UIM coverage to **$100,000** by operation of law.
  - Their uninsured son ran a red light in their minivan, killing himself and his wife and injuring their three children.
  - Liberty Mutual offered the undisputed $25,000. The Freelands sought a declaration that coverage was $100,000. Liberty Mutual **removed**, alleging $100,000 in controversy.
  - Neither party challenged jurisdiction, and the district court didn't address it.
- **Issue:** Is there diversity jurisdiction when the difference between the coverage sought ($100,000) and the undisputed coverage ($25,000) is exactly $75,000?
- **Holding:** No. The amount in controversy was exactly **$75,000**, "one penny short," so the district court lacked SMJ. **Vacated and remanded** with instructions to send the case back to state court.
  - The $25,000 the Freelands would keep even if they lost is not in controversy.
- **Takeaway:** Diversity jurisdiction requires an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000, so exactly $75,000 is not enough.

**Amount in controversy** (lecture 25 notes): must be **over $75,000**, exclusive of interest and costs.

## Class 26 (10/22) — Federal Question Jurisdiction

**Arising-under at two levels:**
- **Article III** "arising under" is **broad** (Osborn: a federal "ingredient" is enough).
- **§ 1331** "arising under" is **narrower**. Courts read it to impose stricter requirements than Article III (Mottley; Gunn).

### Reading: Introductory Note to *Osborn* plus *Osborn v. Bank of the United States* (CB 925–32)
**Rule: Article III arising-under jurisdiction.** "When a question to which the judicial power of the Union is extended by the constitution, **forms an ingredient of the original cause**, it is in the power of Congress to give the Circuit Courts jurisdiction of that cause, although other questions of fact or of law may be involved in it."
- **Statute plus constitutional authority:** A lower federal court has jurisdiction only if (1) an act of Congress confers it and (2) Article III permits it.
- Once a federal question supports jurisdiction, the **other questions are decided as incidental** to it.
- **Limit:** Jurisdiction can't rest "merely on the ground that a question **might possibly** be raised" about federal law, but "a case may be such in its very existence, or it may become such in its progress."
- The right to sue "**cannot depend on the defence** which the defendant may choose to set up."

**Case: Osborn v. Bank of the United States** (U.S. 1824)
- **Facts:**
  - The Bank of the United States was created by Congress, and its **charter expressly authorized it to sue** in the federal Circuit Courts.
  - Ohio taxed each Bank office **$50,000** a year, collectible by forcible seizure. Auditor **Osborn** announced he would collect. The Bank got a federal injunction, but Osborn's agent **Harper** went to the Bank's Chillicothe office anyway and seized **$100,000**.
  - Osborn argued the case didn't arise under federal law because it turned on general state-law principles. The Court addressed whether **every** suit by the Bank, even a plain contract suit, could be heard in federal court.
- **Issue:** Does Article III permit Congress to authorize the Bank to sue in federal court in all cases, including ones involving questions of state law, on the theory that every such case "arises under" federal law?
- **Holding:** Yes. The charter's sue-and-be-sued clause "is consistent with the constitution." When a federal question forms an **ingredient** of the original cause, Congress may confer jurisdiction even though other questions are involved.
  - Every Bank suit meets that test because the Bank's **capacity to exist, contract, and sue** comes from its **federal charter**.
  - Also held: the Eleventh Amendment didn't bar suit against a state's **agents**, and the Ohio tax was **unconstitutional**.
- **Takeaway:** Article III "arising under" jurisdiction is broad and reaches any case in which a federal question forms an ingredient of the original cause.

### Reading: *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley* (CB 934–36)
**Rule: Well-pleaded complaint rule** (§ 1331). A suit arises under federal law "**only when the plaintiff's statement of his own cause of action shows that it is based upon those laws or that Constitution**." "It is not enough that the plaintiff alleges some **anticipated defense** to his cause of action, and asserts that the defense is invalidated by some provision of the Constitution."
- Courts have a **duty to police SMJ** even when "neither party has questioned that jurisdiction."

**Case: Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley** (U.S. 1908)
- **Facts:**
  - The Mottleys (Kentucky citizens) were injured in an 1871 train collision. They released their claims in exchange for **free lifetime passes**.
  - The railroad (also a Kentucky citizen, so **no diversity**) honored the passes until 1907, then refused, citing a **1906 federal statute** banning free passes.
  - The Mottleys sued for **specific performance** (a state-law contract claim), alleging in anticipation that the statute didn't apply, or if it did, it violated the **Fifth Amendment**.
  - Neither party questioned jurisdiction, and the Mottleys won below.
- **Issue:** Does a state-law contract suit arise under federal law when the only federal issues are an anticipated federal defense and the plaintiff's constitutional response to it?
- **Holding:** No. The Supreme Court raised jurisdiction **on its own**, reversed, and ordered dismissal for want of jurisdiction.
  - The federal issues appear only as a **defense** and a reply to it, not in the plaintiff's own cause of action.
  - The dismissal left the Mottleys free to refile in **state court**, which they did.
- **Takeaway:** Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, § 1331 jurisdiction exists only when the federal question appears in the plaintiff's own claim, not in an anticipated defense.

### Reading: *Gunn v. Minton* (CB 947–55)
**Rule: Two routes to "arising under" jurisdiction** (same meaning in § 1331 and § 1338(a)).
1. **Creation test:** "A suit arises under the law that **creates the cause of action**." This covers the vast majority of cases.
2. **Grable test** for **state-law claims** raising federal issues: a "special and small category" where the federal issue is:
   - **(1) necessarily raised:** the plaintiff can't win without a court applying federal law;
   - **(2) actually disputed:** genuinely contested;
   - **(3) substantial:** important "to the **federal system as a whole**," not just to the parties; and
   - **(4) capable of resolution in federal court without disrupting the federal-state balance** approved by Congress.
- **Master of the complaint:** A plaintiff may choose to plead only a state-law claim (absent complete preemption).
- A state negligence claim using a federal statute with **no private right of action** (FDCA misbranding) as a standard of care does **not** arise under federal law (Merrell Dow).
- **§ 1338(a):** "No State court shall have jurisdiction" over claims arising under the patent laws (exclusive federal jurisdiction).

**Case: Gunn v. Minton** (U.S. 2013)
- **Facts:**
  - Minton leased his securities-trading system (TEXCEN) to a brokerage, then applied for a patent more than a year later.
  - Represented by Gunn, he sued NASD and NASDAQ for **patent infringement** in federal court and lost: the patent was invalid under the **on-sale bar**. He raised the **experimental-use** exception too late, and the Federal Circuit held it waived.
  - Minton sued his lawyers for **legal malpractice** in **Texas state court** (a state-law claim), arguing the experimental-use argument would have won. After losing, he argued on appeal that his malpractice claim **arose under federal patent law**, so the Texas courts lacked jurisdiction and he could start over in federal court.
- **Issue:** Does a state-law legal malpractice claim alleging error in a patent case "arise under" federal patent law under § 1338(a), so that state courts lack jurisdiction?
- **Holding:** No. The Texas courts had jurisdiction.
  - **Necessarily raised:** yes, since the case-within-a-case requires deciding the patent question.
  - **Actually disputed:** yes.
  - **Substantial:** **no.** The issue is backward-looking and hypothetical ("what would have happened"), it won't change the patent's validity, and state courts' answers don't bind federal patent law. It matters to the parties, not the federal system.
  - **Federal-state balance:** states have a strong interest in regulating lawyers, so federal jurisdiction would be disruptive.
  - State malpractice claims based on patent matters "will rarely, if ever" arise under federal patent law.
- **Takeaway:** A state-law claim arises under federal law only if a federal issue is necessarily raised, actually disputed, substantial to the federal system as a whole, and resolvable federally without disrupting the federal-state balance.

## Class 27 (10/27) — Supplemental Jurisdiction

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. § 1367 (CB 956–57)
**Rule: The problem.** A state-law claim against a **nondiverse** party, or between diverse parties **below the amount in controversy**, can't be heard in federal court if it "stands alone." The only route is through a claim in the same action that **does** have original jurisdiction.

| Subsection | Function | Mandatory or discretionary | Applies to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **§ 1367(a)** | **Grants** supplemental jurisdiction over claims in the same "case or controversy" | Mandatory ("shall have") | Any civil action within original jurisdiction |
| **§ 1367(b)** | **Takes away** (a)-jurisdiction over certain claims | Mandatory ("shall not have") | Only actions founded **solely** on § 1332 (diversity) |
| **§ 1367(c)** | Lets the court **decline** (a)-jurisdiction on four grounds | Discretionary ("may decline") | Any claim under (a) |
| **§ 1367(e)** | Defines "State" (includes D.C., Puerto Rico, territories) | Definitional | Whole section |

**Rule § 1367(a):** In any civil action within original jurisdiction, district courts "shall have supplemental jurisdiction over all other claims that are **so related** to claims in the action within such original jurisdiction that they form part of the **same case or controversy under Article III**." This "shall include claims that involve the **joinder or intervention of additional parties**."
- **Relatedness test:** the claims must "derive from a **common nucleus of operative fact**" (Gibbs).

**Rule § 1367(b):** In actions founded **solely on § 1332**, no supplemental jurisdiction under (a) over the following, "when exercising supplemental jurisdiction over such claims would be inconsistent with the jurisdictional requirements of section 1332":
1. claims **by plaintiffs** against persons made parties under **Rule 14, 19, 20, or 24**;
2. claims by persons proposed to be joined **as plaintiffs under Rule 19**;
3. claims by persons seeking to **intervene as plaintiffs under Rule 24**.
- The idea: plaintiffs can't use joinder to get around **complete diversity** (e.g., Kroger: a plaintiff can't sue a nondiverse third-party defendant impleaded under Rule 14).

**Rule § 1367(c):** The court **may decline** supplemental jurisdiction if: (1) the claim raises a **novel or complex issue of state law**; (2) the claim **substantially predominates** over the original-jurisdiction claims; (3) the court has **dismissed all claims** with original jurisdiction; or (4) in **exceptional circumstances**, other compelling reasons.

**State National Insurance v. Yates** is introduced (CB 956–57) as an illustration of the § 1367(a) "common nucleus" test. Only the introductory note was in the assigned pages, and it ends before the opinion.

### Reading: *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc.* (CB 962–80)
**Rule: Background doctrines before § 1367.**
- **Pendent-claim jurisdiction (Gibbs):** With a federal claim, the court has power (not obligation) to hear related state claims from a **common nucleus of operative fact**.
- **Pendent-party jurisdiction:** Not allowed before § 1367 (Finley). Jurisdiction over claims by or against one party doesn't extend to **different parties**.
- **Ancillary jurisdiction:** Let courts hear related matters regardless of citizenship or amount, mainly to protect **defending** parties, but not a plaintiff's claims against a nondiverse impleaded party (Kroger).
- **Nonaggregation (Clark; Zahn):** Each plaintiff with a separate and distinct demand had to meet the amount on their own. A **single plaintiff may aggregate** all claims against a single defendant, even unrelated ones.
- **Minimal vs. complete diversity:** Article III requires only **minimal** diversity and has no amount requirement; § 1332 requires **complete** diversity.

**Rule: Exxon's reading of § 1367(a).** "If the court has original jurisdiction over a **single claim** in the complaint, it has original jurisdiction over a 'civil action' within the meaning of § 1367(a)." So if at least one named plaintiff satisfies the amount and diversity is complete, supplemental jurisdiction can reach other plaintiffs' smaller claims in the same case or controversy.
- **Contamination theory:** A **nondiverse party** contaminates every claim (it removes the reason for a federal forum), so complete diversity still applies. A claim **below the amount** "does nothing to reduce the importance" of the claims that meet it, so it contaminates nothing.
- Courts may **cure** jurisdictional defects by **dismissing the offending parties** instead of the whole action.

**Case: Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc.** (U.S. 2005), consolidated with *Ortega v. Star-Kist Foods*
- **Facts:**
  - **Exxon:** About **10,000 Exxon dealers** brought a diversity **class action** (Rule 23) alleging systematic fuel overcharges. Some class members' claims didn't meet the amount in controversy. The jury found for the dealers.
  - **Ortega:** A **9-year-old girl** sued Star-Kist in diversity after badly cutting her finger on a tuna can. Her **family joined** (Rule 20) for emotional distress and medical expenses. Her claim met the amount; her family's didn't.
  - The circuits were split on whether § 1367 reached the smaller claims.
- **Issue:** In a diversity action, may a federal court exercise supplemental jurisdiction under § 1367 over additional plaintiffs whose claims don't meet the amount in controversy, if their claims are part of the same case or controversy as a plaintiff whose claim does?
- **Holding:** Yes, for both **Rule 20 co-plaintiffs** and **Rule 23 class members**. § 1367 "by its plain text overruled **Clark and Zahn**." (Exxon affirmed; Ortega reversed.)
  - § 1367(b) lists Rule 19 and Rule 24 plaintiffs but **not Rule 20 or Rule 23 plaintiffs**, so it doesn't take away jurisdiction over their claims.
  - Ordinary statutory construction applies: no canon requires Congress to speak "with extraordinary clarity" to change jurisdiction.
  - **Dissent (Ginsburg):** Read "original jurisdiction" to incorporate the complete-diversity and nonaggregation rules as they stood, so § 1367 just codifies pendent and ancillary jurisdiction.
- **Takeaway:** Under § 1367, once one plaintiff meets the amount in controversy in a diversity case, supplemental jurisdiction extends to Rule 20 co-plaintiffs and Rule 23 class members with smaller claims, but complete diversity is still required.

## Class 28 (10/28) — Removal

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441, 1446, 1447 (CB 984–90)
**Rule § 1441(a): What can be removed.** "Any civil action brought in a State court of which the district courts of the United States have **original jurisdiction**, may be removed **by the defendant or the defendants**" to the federal district court "for the district and division embracing the place where such action is pending."
- **Defendant-only removal:** "Defendants," not "parties." A state-court **plaintiff can't remove**, even when the defendant files a counterclaim that would satisfy federal jurisdiction (Shamrock Oil).
- **Place:** Removal goes to the specific federal district and division covering the state courthouse.
- **One-way street:** No removal from federal to state court. A plaintiff's choice of federal court beats a defendant's preference for state court.

**Rule § 1441(b)(2): Forum-defendant rule.** A case removable **solely on diversity** "may not be removed if any of the parties in interest properly joined and served as defendants is a **citizen of the State in which such action is brought**." (Diversity protects out-of-state defendants from local prejudice; a home-state defendant doesn't need that.)

**Rule § 1441(f):** The federal court can hear a claim even if the state court lacked jurisdiction over it (e.g., exclusive federal claims).

**Rule § 1446: Procedure.**
- **(a)** File a **notice of removal** in federal court, **signed under Rule 11**, with a **short and plain statement of the grounds** and copies of all process, pleadings, and orders.
- **Removal is self-executing:** filing the notice puts the case in federal court, though the plaintiff can seek remand.
- **(b)(1) 30 days:** File within **30 days** after the defendant receives the initial pleading (through service or otherwise), or after service of summons if shorter.
- **(b)(2)(A) Unanimity:** For removal under § 1441(a), **all defendants** properly joined and served must **join in or consent**.
- **(b)(3) Later-removable cases:** If the initial pleading isn't removable, the defendant has **30 days** after receiving "an amended pleading, motion, order or other paper" showing the case **has become** removable.
- **(c)(1) One-year limit:** No removal on diversity under (b)(3) **more than 1 year after commencement**, unless the plaintiff acted in **bad faith** to prevent removal.
- **(c)(2) Amount in controversy:** The sum demanded **in good faith** in the initial pleading is deemed the amount. If the pleading seeks nonmonetary relief or state practice doesn't allow a specific demand, the notice may assert the amount, and removal is proper if the court finds by a **preponderance of the evidence** that it exceeds $75,000.
- **(c)(3):** State-court record or discovery information about the amount counts as an "**other paper**." Deliberately hiding the actual amount to prevent removal is **bad faith**.
- **(d)** Promptly notify adverse parties and file a copy with the **state court**, which "shall proceed no further unless and until the case is remanded."

**Rule § 1447: Remand.**
- **(c) Non-SMJ defects** (procedural problems like a late notice or the forum-defendant rule): a remand motion must be made within **30 days** after the notice of removal, or the objection is lost.
- **(c) Lack of SMJ:** "If **at any time before final judgment** it appears that the district court lacks subject matter jurisdiction, the case **shall be remanded**."
- A remand order may require payment of **just costs and actual expenses, including attorney fees**.
- **(d)** "An order **remanding** a case to the State court from which it was removed is **not reviewable on appeal or otherwise**." (Orders **denying** remand aren't immediately appealable either, but they can be reviewed after final judgment: Caterpillar.)
- **(e)** If after removal the plaintiff seeks to join defendants whose joinder would **destroy SMJ**, the court may **deny joinder**, or **permit joinder and remand**.

**Case: Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets** (U.S. 1941) (described in the reading)
- **Facts:** The plaintiff sued the defendant in state court. The parties were diverse. The defendant **counterclaimed** for more than the jurisdictional amount, and the **state-court plaintiff** removed.
- **Issue:** May a state-court plaintiff remove under § 1441(a) when the defendant asserts a qualifying counterclaim?
- **Holding:** No. Only the **defendant** may remove; the case had to be **remanded**.
- **Takeaway:** Only a defendant may remove, so a plaintiff facing a counterclaim cannot remove the case.

### Reading: *St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.* (CB 992–97)
**Rule: Legal certainty test.** "The sum claimed by the plaintiff controls if the claim is apparently made in **good faith**," and "it must appear to a **legal certainty** that the claim is really for less than the jurisdictional amount to justify dismissal."
- **Removed cases:** There's "a **strong presumption** that the plaintiff has not claimed a large amount in order to confer jurisdiction on a federal court." Remand follows only if it's **obvious from the face of the complaint** that the suit can't involve the necessary amount.
- **Post-removal reductions:** "Events occurring subsequent to removal which **reduce the amount recoverable**, whether beyond the plaintiff's control or the result of his volition, **do not oust** the district court's jurisdiction once it has attached."
- **Plaintiff controls removability:** A plaintiff who wants to stay in state court can **sue for less** than the jurisdictional amount, and the defendant can't remove.
- **Time-of-filing (or removal) rule:** Jurisdiction depends on the state of things when the action is brought or removed. A later change of citizenship doesn't oust jurisdiction.

**Case: St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.** (U.S. 1938)
- **Facts:**
  - Red Cab (Indiana) sued St. Paul Mercury (Minnesota) in Indiana state court on a workers' compensation insurance binder, claiming **$4,000** when the jurisdictional minimum was **$3,000**. St. Paul Mercury **removed**.
  - After removal, Red Cab amended. Its second amended complaint still demanded $4,000 but attached an exhibit itemizing only **$1,380.89**. There was no evidence St. Paul Mercury knew the claim was smaller when it removed.
  - The federal court found for Red Cab for **$1,162.98**. The Seventh Circuit ordered remand for lack of the jurisdictional amount.
- **Issue:** Must a removed diversity case be remanded when, after a proper removal, the plaintiff amends so its claim falls well below the jurisdictional amount?
- **Holding:** No. A post-removal reduction of the amount claimed, "whether by stipulation, affidavit or amendment," doesn't defeat jurisdiction once it attaches. On the face of the pleadings, removal was proper. **Reversed.**
  - Otherwise a plaintiff could manipulate jurisdiction after removal, and the defendant's right to remove would be at the plaintiff's mercy.
- **Takeaway:** Once jurisdiction attaches at removal, a later reduction of the amount claimed does not defeat it.

## Class 29 (10/29) — Removal (cont.)

### Reading: *Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis* (CB 998–1004)
**Rule:** "A district court's error in failing to remand a case improperly removed is **not fatal to the ensuing adjudication** if federal jurisdictional requirements are met **at the time judgment is entered**."
- The case must be "fit for federal adjudication **at the time the removal petition is filed**" (§ 1441(a)), but a **statutory** removal defect is cured if no **jurisdictional** defect remains by judgment.
- A plaintiff who **timely moves to remand** has done "all that was required to preserve his objection to removal."
- An order **denying remand**, standing alone, is **not final** or immediately appealable.
- If the jurisdictional defect is **never cured**, Rule 12(h)(3) requires vacating the judgment.

**Case: Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis** (U.S. 1996)
- **Facts:**
  - Lewis (Kentucky) sued in Kentucky state court after a bulldozer injury, naming **Caterpillar** (Delaware/Illinois) and **Whayne Supply** (Kentucky). **No complete diversity** from the start.
  - **Liberty Mutual** (Massachusetts), the employer's workers' comp insurer, intervened with subrogation claims against both defendants.
  - Lewis settled with Whayne Supply, and Caterpillar **removed one day before the one-year limit**. But **Liberty Mutual's claim against Whayne Supply was still pending**, so Whayne (a Kentucky citizen) was still a defendant.
  - Lewis **timely moved to remand**; the district court **erroneously denied** it.
  - Years later, Liberty Mutual settled with Whayne, which was dismissed, creating complete diversity. A jury then returned a verdict for **Caterpillar**. The Sixth Circuit vacated for lack of jurisdiction at removal.
- **Issue:** Must a federal judgment be vacated because complete diversity was missing at removal and remand was wrongly denied, when the nondiverse defendant was dismissed and complete diversity existed by trial and judgment?
- **Holding:** No. The judgment stands; the defect was cured before judgment. **Reversed.**
  - **Finality, efficiency, and economy** are "overwhelming" considerations once a case has been tried in a court with jurisdiction at the end. Requiring dismissal after years of litigation would impose "unnecessary and wasteful burdens."
  - Consistent with Congress's removal scheme, which relies on prompt district-court supervision and on deterrence: a "well-advised defendant" can expect a wrongful removal to end in "a swift and nonreviewable remand order."
- **Takeaway:** An improper removal does not require vacating the judgment if the jurisdictional defect was cured before judgment, because finality and efficiency outweigh the error.

### Reading: *Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group* (CB 1005–13)
**Rule: Time-of-filing rule.** Diversity is measured against "**the state of facts that existed at the time of filing**," whether the challenge comes shortly after filing, after trial, or for the first time on appeal. Challenges to SMJ can be raised **any time before final judgment**.
- **A partnership** is a citizen of each state or country of which **any partner** is a citizen.
- **Aliens on both sides** destroy diversity.
- **Rule 21 exception:** A **dispensable nondiverse party** can be dropped at any time, even after judgment, to cure a defect (a **change in the parties**).
- **No cure by a change in citizenship of a continuing party**, including a partnership's change caused by partners leaving.
- Caterpillar is distinguished: it cured a **statutory removal** defect through a **change in parties** (dismissing Whayne Supply), not a change in a party's citizenship.

**Case: Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group, L.P.** (U.S. 2004)
- **Facts:**
  - **Atlas**, a Texas limited partnership, sued **Dataflux**, a **Mexican** corporation, in federal court in Texas in 1997 on state-law contract claims (over $1.3 million), alleging alienage diversity.
  - At filing, **two of Atlas's partners were Mexican citizens**, so Atlas was also a Mexican citizen: **aliens on both sides**.
  - Nobody noticed for almost three years. The Mexican partners left Atlas the month **before trial**. A jury awarded Atlas **$750,000**.
  - Before judgment was entered, Dataflux moved to dismiss for lack of SMJ.
- **Issue:** Can a party's change in citizenship after filing (here, a partnership losing its Mexican partners) cure a lack of diversity that existed at filing, when the defect wasn't raised until after the verdict?
- **Holding:** No. The **time-of-filing rule** controls. A change in the citizenship of a **continuing party** doesn't cure a defect that existed at filing; only a **change in the parties** (like dropping a dispensable nondiverse party) does. Dismissal affirmed.
  - The Court rejected a "post-verdict cure" exception: a clear rule avoids litigation over jurisdiction and discourages parties from sitting on defects.
  - **Dissent (Ginsburg):** Caterpillar's efficiency logic should apply; after years of litigation and a verdict, dismissal wastes resources, especially since Dataflux had conceded jurisdiction in its answer.
- **Takeaway:** Under the time-of-filing rule, diversity is measured when the suit is filed, and a later change in a continuing party's citizenship cannot cure a defect that existed then.

# UNIT X — Personal Jurisdiction (Classes 30–33)

**Personal jurisdiction (PJ)** is the court's **power over the parties**. Without it, a court can't enter a judgment that **binds** the defendant, just as it can't without SMJ. PJ and SMJ are **independent**: a court can have one and lack the other.
- Unlike SMJ, PJ limits **both state and federal courts**. SMJ comes from Article III; PJ comes from the **Due Process Clause** (14th Amendment for state courts).
- Unlike SMJ, PJ is a **waivable, personal** defense. It's lost if left out of the first Rule 12 motion or answer (12(h)(1)), and a defendant can consent to it.

**PJ roadmap:**
1. **Statute:** Does the forum state's **long-arm statute** authorize jurisdiction? A **federal** court borrows the state's reach under **Rule 4(k)(1)(A)**. Most long-arm statutes go to the constitutional limit, so this step usually merges into step 2.
2. **Traditional bases** (no contacts analysis needed):
   - **Tag:** served while **physically present** in the state (Burnham). Not if brought in by **force or fraud**, or present as a party or witness in another case.
   - **Consent:** appearing and litigating without objecting, a forum-selection clause, or **registering to do business** under a consent statute (Mallory).
   - **Domicile** (individuals) or **at home** (corporations: state of incorporation + principal place of business).
3. **General jurisdiction** ("all-purpose"; any claim, even one unrelated to the forum): the defendant is **"essentially at home"** in the forum (Goodyear, Daimler).
4. **Specific jurisdiction** ("case-linked"): (a) **minimum contacts** that the **defendant itself purposefully** created with the **forum state** (WWVW, Nicastro, Walden); (b) the claim **arises out of or relates to** those contacts; (c) exercising jurisdiction is **fair and reasonable** (the WWVW factors).

| | **General jurisdiction** | **Specific jurisdiction** |
|---|---|---|
| Claims covered | **Any and all** claims | Only claims that **arise out of or relate to** the forum contacts |
| Test | Affiliations so continuous and systematic the defendant is **"essentially at home"** | **Minimum contacts** + **fair play and substantial justice** |
| Individuals | **Domicile**; tag (Burnham) | Purposeful contacts with the forum |
| Corporations | **State of incorporation** + **principal place of business**; maybe an "exceptional case" (Perkins); registration consent (Mallory) | Purposeful availment; stream of commerce "plus" (Nicastro) |
| Key cases | Goodyear, Daimler, Perkins, Helicopteros, Burnham, Mallory | Int'l Shoe, WWVW, Nicastro, Walden, Calder, be2 |

**Ways to challenge PJ:**
- **Special appearance** / Rule 12(b)(2) motion in the original case. Litigating without objecting **waives** the defense.
- **Default, then collaterally attack** the judgment as void (riskier). If the court had PJ, the defendant loses the chance to contest the merits. A defaulting defendant who later challenges PJ **bears the burden of proof** (be2).

## Class 30 (11/3) — Historical Development of the Doctrine

**Lecture wrap-up on removal:**
- **Caterpillar:** If complete diversity is missing at removal, **dropping the nondiverse party** before judgment cures the defect.
- **Grupo Dataflux:** Domicile is fixed **at filing**. A later **change in citizenship** can't cure a defect, which is different from dropping a party.
- **Removal and change of citizenship:** For diversity removal, courts require complete diversity **both at filing and at removal**. A defendant can't change domicile after filing to make the case removable. Some courts say a **plaintiff's** change of domicile after filing in state court can make the case removable.

### Reading: Introduction to Personal Jurisdiction (CB 1017–19)
**Rule: Connection principle.** "The courts of a state can adjudicate only the rights of those who have **enough of a connection to the state** to justify the exercise of the state's judicial authority over them."
- **Before the 14th Amendment:** Courts generally wouldn't exercise jurisdiction over defendants not present in their territory. This was a matter of **comity** (mutual respect among sovereigns), not constitutional compulsion.
- **After the 14th Amendment:** The Due Process Clause bars states, **including state courts**, from depriving anyone of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." Personal jurisdiction became a **constitutional** limit.

### Reading: *Pennoyer v. Neff* (CB 1019–26)
**Rule: Pennoyer's territorial framework.** A state's courts have power over **persons and property within its territory**, and none beyond it. A personal (in personam) judgment requires:
- **Presence:** service of process **within the state**, or
- **Consent:** a voluntary appearance, or
- **Property:** an in rem or quasi in rem case tied to property in the state (the property must be **attached at the start** of the suit).
- A judgment entered **without personal jurisdiction is void**. It violates due process and isn't entitled to full faith and credit.

**Case: Pennoyer v. Neff** (U.S. 1878)
- **Facts:**
  - **Mitchell**, an Oregon lawyer, sued **Neff**, a nonresident, in Oregon state court on a **contract claim** for unpaid fees. Neff was served only **by publication** and never appeared. Mitchell got a **default judgment**.
  - To satisfy it, Neff's Oregon land was sold at a sheriff's execution sale, and **Pennoyer** eventually bought it.
  - Neff later sued Pennoyer in federal court to recover the land, which was worth about $15,000. Neff claimed title through a U.S. land patent; Pennoyer claimed it through the sheriff's deed.
- **Issue:** Was the Oregon judgment against Neff valid? Does due process limit a state court's power to enter judgment against a defendant who can't be found in the state?
- **Holding:** No, the judgment was **void**. Neff wasn't served in Oregon and didn't appear. The land was within Oregon's territory, but it **wasn't attached at the outset**, and the case was a **personal contract claim** against Neff. So Pennoyer's title failed.
- **Takeaway:** Under Pennoyer, a personal judgment requires in-state service or consent, and a judgment entered without personal jurisdiction is void and can be attacked collaterally.

### Reading: *Hess v. Pawloski* (CB 1029–31)
**Rule: Implied consent.** A state may declare that a nonresident's **use of its highways** is **equivalent to appointing a state official as agent** for service of process in suits arising from accidents there, as long as the statute gives **actual notice** and a **reasonable opportunity to defend**.
- **Rationale:** The state has power to **exclude** nonresident drivers until they formally appoint an agent, so it can treat driving as the appointment. The difference between **formal and implied appointment** is "not substantial."
- **Foreign corporations:** A state's qualified power to exclude them supports implying their consent.
- **Individuals doing business:** A state **can't** exclude individuals from doing business, so their consent can't be implied from mere business activity.
- Motor vehicles are "**dangerous machines**," giving the state a police-power interest in regulating everyone on its roads. The statute puts nonresidents "on the same footing as residents."

**Case: Hess v. Pawloski** (U.S. 1927)
- **Facts:**
  - Hess, a **Pennsylvania** resident, allegedly drove negligently in **Massachusetts** and injured Pawloski, who sued in Massachusetts state court.
  - Hess wasn't personally served, and no property was attached. Service was made under a Massachusetts statute treating a nonresident's driving as appointing the **registrar** as agent for service. The statute required notice by **registered mail**, the defendant's return receipt, and continuances if needed to defend.
  - Hess **appeared specially** to contest jurisdiction and lost. The jury found for Pawloski.
- **Issue:** Does a statute treating a nonresident's driving on state highways as appointment of the registrar as agent for service violate due process?
- **Holding:** No. **Affirmed.** Since the state may require formal appointment before nonresidents drive on its roads, it may deem driving to be that appointment. Consent is limited to highway accident cases, and the statute ensures actual notice and a chance to defend.
- **Takeaway:** Hess was the first crack in Pennoyer. It kept Pennoyer's form (service on an authorized agent) while expanding its reach through the **fiction of implied consent**.

### Reading: *International Shoe Co. v. Washington* (CB 1034–39)
**Rule: Minimum contacts.** If the defendant is "not present within the territory of the forum," due process requires only "**certain minimum contacts** with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend '**traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice**.'"
- **Corporate "presence" is a fiction.** A corporation acts only through its agents, so asking whether it's "present" just "begs the question." The real question is whether its contacts make it **reasonable** to require it to defend this suit.
- **Not mechanical or quantitative.** The test depends on "the **quality and nature** of the activity" in relation to the fair and orderly administration of the laws.
- **Outer limit:** A state can't bind a defendant with "**no contacts, ties, or relations**" to it.
- **Reciprocity:** A corporation that conducts activities in a state "enjoys the **benefits and protection** of the laws of that state." Requiring it to answer suits on obligations that **arise out of** those activities is "hardly" undue.
- **Inconvenience** to the defendant of trial away from its "home" is relevant.
- The old "**mere solicitation**" vs. "**solicitation plus**" labels gave way to the functional test.

**International Shoe's two-variable grid** (the source of specific vs. general jurisdiction):

| Defendant's activity in the forum | Claim **related** to the activity | Claim **unrelated** to the activity |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuous and systematic** | **Jurisdiction** (this case) | Usually **no**, unless operations are "so substantial" (later: general jurisdiction) |
| **Single or isolated** | **Sometimes**, depending on the "nature and quality" of the acts | **No** |

**Case: International Shoe Co. v. Washington** (U.S. 1945)
- **Facts:**
  - International Shoe was a **Delaware** corporation with its principal place of business in **St. Louis, Missouri**. It had **no office, inventory, or contracts** in Washington.
  - From 1937 to 1940 it employed **11–13 salesmen** who lived and worked in Washington. They displayed samples and **solicited orders**, which were accepted in St. Louis and shipped from outside the state. Commissions topped **$31,000 a year**.
  - Washington sought unpaid **unemployment-fund contributions** based on those salesmen's wages. It served notice on a Washington salesman and mailed a copy to St. Louis.
  - The company **appeared specially**, arguing it wasn't "doing business" in Washington and had no agent there.
- **Issue:** Did the company's activities in Washington subject it to suit there, consistent with due process? Was the notice adequate?
- **Holding:** Yes. The salesmen's activities were "**systematic and continuous**," produced a large volume of business, and **gave rise to the very obligation sued on**. That's enough minimum contacts. Notice by service on a salesman plus registered mail to the home office was adequate. **Affirmed.**
  - **Justice Black (separately):** "Fair play" and "substantial justice" are too vague, giving courts room to second-guess state legislatures.
- **Takeaway:** International Shoe replaced Pennoyer's territorial-presence model with the minimum contacts and fair play test, which is the foundation of the modern specific/general framework.

**Lecture summary (from Pennoyer to Shoe).** There's PJ if:
- The defendant is a **resident** of the state, or the case is about **property** in the state (Pennoyer).
- The defendant **consents** by litigating in that court (Pennoyer).
- **Tag:** the defendant is served within the state (lecture ties this to Hess).
- The defendant **causes harm in the state** / implied consent (Hess), which International Shoe expands: an out-of-state defendant with **minimum contacts** can be sued on claims **arising from** those contacts.

## Class 31 (11/4) — Specific Jurisdiction

### Reading: Note on Long-Arm Statutes (CB 1041–42)
**Rule: Two sources of limits.** State-court PJ is limited by **(1) state law** and **(2) the 14th Amendment's Due Process Clause**.
- **Long-arm statute:** a state law that reaches defendants far from the state. States expanded these statutes after International Shoe's "more permissive paradigm."
- **Constitutional maximum:** Courts usually read long-arm statutes to reach as far as the Constitution allows (e.g., Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 410.10; 735 ILCS 5/2-209(c); Nev. Rev. Stat. § 14.065).
- **States may go narrower** than due process allows.
- **Two-step inquiry:** (1) Does state law authorize jurisdiction? (2) Is it consistent with due process? With a maximum-reach statute, the two steps **collapse into one**: the due process question.
- **Federal courts:** **Rule 4(k)(1)(A)** gives a federal court the same PJ as "a court of general jurisdiction in the state where the district court is located." So federal courts use the same two steps (Walden, Daimler, be2).

### Reading: *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson* (CB 1043–54)
**Rule: Foreseeability isn't enough.** "'Foreseeability' alone has never been a sufficient benchmark." What matters is that "the defendant's conduct and connection with the forum State are such that he should **reasonably anticipate being haled into court there**."
- **Purposeful availment:** A defendant that "purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State" has **clear notice** it can be sued there. It can manage the risk through insurance, pricing, or leaving the market.
- **Stream of commerce:** A state may assert jurisdiction over "a corporation that **delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State**."
- **Unilateral activity:** "The mere unilateral activity of those who claim some relationship with a nonresident defendant" can't supply the contact.
- **Collateral financial benefits** (e.g., revenue from cars that happen to be driven into the state) don't count.
- **Two functions of minimum contacts:** (1) protect the defendant from litigating in a distant or inconvenient forum; (2) keep states within their limits as **coequal sovereigns** (interstate federalism).
- **Federalism can override convenience.** Even if the defendant faces little inconvenience, the forum has a strong interest, and it's the most convenient place, due process can still bar jurisdiction.
- **Reasonableness (fairness) factors:**
  1. Burden on the defendant (always a primary concern)
  2. Forum state's interest in adjudicating the dispute
  3. Plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief
  4. Interstate judicial system's interest in efficiency
  5. States' shared interest in furthering fundamental substantive social policies

**Case: World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson** (U.S. 1980)
- **Facts:**
  - The **Robinsons**, New York residents, bought an Audi from **Seaway** (a New York dealer) in Massena, NY.
  - While driving to their new home in Arizona, they were rear-ended in **Oklahoma**. The car caught fire, severely burning Kay Robinson and two children.
  - They sued in Oklahoma state court: **Audi** (manufacturer), **Volkswagen of America** (importer), **World-Wide** (regional distributor serving only NY, NJ, CT), and **Seaway** (dealer).
  - World-Wide and Seaway did **no business in Oklahoma**: no sales, shipments, agents, or advertising aimed there. Only World-Wide and Seaway challenged jurisdiction in the Supreme Court.
- **Issue:** Can Oklahoma exercise PJ over a New York dealer and regional distributor when their only connection to Oklahoma is that a car sold in New York had an accident there?
- **Holding:** No. **Reversed.** There was "a **total absence** of those affiliating circumstances." The defendants' only link was the "**fortuitous circumstance**" of the Robinsons' **unilateral** decision to drive through Oklahoma. A car's mobility makes injury elsewhere foreseeable, but that's not enough. Otherwise every seller would make its products "agents for service of process" wherever they travel.
  - **Dissents (Brennan; Marshall and Blackmun):** The defendants joined a **nationwide** network for a product whose usefulness is its **mobility**, so its use in Oklahoma was a "virtual certainty." Blackmun also asked why the plaintiffs needed to sue the dealer and distributor at all.
- **Takeaway:** A defendant must have purposefully created contacts with the forum, and foreseeable harm there from a consumer's unilateral act isn't enough.

### Reading: *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro* (CB 1059–73)
**Rule: Stream of commerce, three competing approaches.**
- **Plurality (Kennedy, "stream of commerce plus" / targeting):** "The defendant's transmission of goods permits the exercise of jurisdiction **only where the defendant can be said to have targeted the forum**." Predicting goods will reach the forum isn't enough. "It is the defendant's **actions, not his expectations**." Jurisdiction is "a question of **authority rather than fairness**."
- **Concurrence (Breyer and Alito, decide narrowly):** A **single isolated sale** isn't enough under existing precedent. Jurisdiction needs a "**regular … flow**" of sales into the forum, or "**something more**," such as special state-related design, advertising, advice, or marketing.
- **Dissent (Ginsburg, Sotomayor, Kagan):** A manufacturer that targets the **entire U.S. market** through a distributor should be answerable where its product causes injury. Otherwise it can "**Pilate-like wash its hands**" of the product through an independent distributor.
- **Sovereign-by-sovereign analysis (plurality):** A defendant can be subject to the jurisdiction of the **United States** but **not of any particular state**.
- The **New Jersey Supreme Court's** rule (jurisdiction if the manufacturer knew its products were distributed nationally and didn't prevent sales in New Jersey) was **rejected** by six Justices.

**Case: J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro** (U.S. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - **Nicastro** lost four fingers to a **McIntyre** metal-shearing machine at work in **New Jersey**.
  - McIntyre is an **English** company. It sold to the U.S. only through an **independent Ohio distributor** and attended U.S. trade shows (never in New Jersey) hoping to reach buyers "anywhere in the United States."
  - **No more than four machines** (perhaps just one) ended up in New Jersey. McIntyre had no office, property, advertising, or employees there.
- **Issue:** Can New Jersey exercise specific jurisdiction over a foreign manufacturer that targeted the U.S. market generally but never targeted New Jersey?
- **Holding:** No (6–3, **no majority opinion**). **Reversed.** The plurality found no purposeful availment of **New Jersey** specifically. The concurrence found a single isolated sale insufficient under precedent.
- **Takeaway:** Targeting the U.S. as a whole, plus one product reaching the forum, doesn't create jurisdiction in that state. Because no rationale got five votes, an exam answer should **analyze stream-of-commerce facts under all three approaches**.

**Lecture summary (specific jurisdiction):**
- Stream of commerce = manufacturer → distributor → retailer → consumer.
- A **single sale** through the stream of commerce likely isn't enough, but **many sales** might be.
- WWVW suggested PJ where a company puts products into the stream "with the expectation" they'll be bought in the forum. **Nicastro limits** that: the plurality requires **targeting**; the concurrence requires more than an isolated sale.

## Class 32 (11/5) — Specific & General Jurisdiction

### Reading: *Walden v. Fiore* (CB 1082–89)
**Rule: The defendant's own contacts with the forum state.** "The defendant's **suit-related conduct** must create a **substantial connection with the forum State**."
- The contacts must be ones "the **defendant himself** creates." The plaintiff's contacts, however significant, can't decide it.
- Look at the defendant's contacts with **the forum state itself**, "not the defendant's contacts with persons who reside there." "**The plaintiff cannot be the only link** between the defendant and the forum."
- Contacts that are "**random, fortuitous, or attenuated**," or that come from the **unilateral activity** of a plaintiff or third party, don't count.
- **Physical entry** (in person, or through an agent, goods, or mail) isn't required, but it's a relevant contact.
- **Effects test (Calder), as Walden reframes it:** Out-of-state intentional conduct can support jurisdiction when its **effects connect the defendant to the forum state itself**, "not just to a plaintiff who lived there." "**Mere injury to a forum resident** is not a sufficient connection."

**Case: Walden v. Fiore** (U.S. 2014)
- **Facts:**
  - **Walden**, a Georgia police officer working as a deputized DEA agent at the **Atlanta airport**, seized almost **$97,000** in cash from Fiore and Gipson, who were flying home to Las Vegas.
  - He allegedly helped draft a **false probable-cause affidavit** in Georgia. No forfeiture complaint was filed, and the money was returned months later.
  - The plaintiffs sued Walden in **federal court in Nevada** (a Bivens claim). Walden never went to, contacted anyone in, or sent anything to Nevada.
- **Issue:** Can a Nevada court exercise PJ over a defendant whose conduct occurred entirely in Georgia, because he knew it would harm plaintiffs with Nevada connections?
- **Holding:** No. **Reversed.** All of Walden's conduct occurred in Georgia. The Ninth Circuit wrongly focused on his contacts with the **plaintiffs** instead of with **Nevada**. The plaintiffs felt the harm in Nevada only because that's where they **chose to be**; they would have suffered the same loss anywhere.
- **Takeaway:** Specific jurisdiction depends on the defendant's own contacts with the forum, and knowing that harm will be felt by a forum resident isn't enough.

**Case: Calder v. Jones** (U.S. 1984) (described in Walden)
- **Facts:** A California actress sued a **Florida**-based National Enquirer reporter and editor for libel over an article written and edited in Florida. The Enquirer had about **600,000 California readers**, the story drew on California sources, and it concerned her California activities.
- **Holding:** Jurisdiction in California was proper based on the "**effects**" of the Florida conduct in California. California was "the **focal point** both of the story and of the harm suffered."
- **Takeaway:** Under Walden's reading, Calder's effects test works because libel requires publication to third parties, so the tort itself happened in California and the defendants' conduct was connected to the state, not just to the plaintiff.

| | **Calder** | **Walden** |
|---|---|---|
| Where the defendant acted | Florida, calling California sources | Entirely in Georgia |
| Forum focus | Story about California, aimed at California readers | Nothing to do with Nevada itself |
| Injury | Reputational harm among California readers | Delayed access to money wherever the plaintiffs happened to be |
| Connection created | **Defendants → forum** | Defendant → **plaintiffs only** |
| Result | Jurisdiction | No jurisdiction |

### Reading: General Jurisdiction (CB 1091–92)
**Rule: General jurisdiction** lets the forum's courts hear **any and all claims** against the defendant, "even claims totally unrelated" to its forum activities. The key question is **how substantial** the connection must be. The Court said little about this for decades, then marked the limits in **Goodyear** and **Daimler**.
- **Specific jurisdiction can't be stretched.** If the claim doesn't arise out of the forum contacts, specific jurisdiction is unavailable "**even if those contacts are extensive**."
- International Shoe's minimum contacts test applies only to defendants "**not present**" in the forum. For defendants **present when served**, courts have long relied on Pennoyer: **tag jurisdiction**.

### Reading: *Burnham v. Superior Court of California* (CB 1092–1104)
**Rule: Tag (transient) jurisdiction.** Jurisdiction based on **personal service while the defendant is physically present** in the state satisfies due process, even if the suit is **unrelated** to the defendant's activities there and the visit is **brief**.
- **Scalia (plurality, 4 Justices): tradition.** Tag jurisdiction is "one of the **continuing traditions** of our legal system that define the due process standard of 'traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.'" A rule with that pedigree, still followed by every state, **is** due process. International Shoe's contacts test **replaces** presence for absent defendants; it doesn't make presence insufficient.
- **Brennan (concurrence in the judgment, 4 Justices): fairness.** History matters but isn't decisive; every jurisdictional rule must meet **contemporary notions of due process**. Tag jurisdiction is generally fair because a **voluntary** visitor **avails himself** of the state's benefits (police, roads, economy), knowingly takes on the risk of suit, and faces little modern burden in defending.
- **White:** The rule is so widely accepted that it can't be struck down; there's no need for case-by-case fairness challenges, at least where presence is **intentional**.
- **Stevens:** History, fairness, and common sense all make this "a very easy case."
- **Traditional exceptions:** No tag jurisdiction over someone brought into the state by **force or fraud**, or present as a **party or witness** in another proceeding.

**Case: Burnham v. Superior Court of California** (U.S. 1990)
- **Facts:**
  - Dennis and Francie Burnham lived in **New Jersey** and separated. She moved to **California** with the children and filed for divorce there.
  - Dennis visited California for **three days**, first on business and then to see his children. While returning a child to Francie's home, he was **served** with the California summons and divorce petition. He then went back to New Jersey.
  - He **appeared specially** to quash service, arguing he lacked minimum contacts with California. The California courts refused.
- **Issue:** Does due process bar California from exercising jurisdiction over a nonresident personally served while temporarily in the state, in a suit unrelated to his activities there?
- **Holding:** No. **Affirmed (unanimous in result, no majority rationale).** In-state personal service on a **voluntarily present** defendant is enough.
- **Takeaway:** Tag jurisdiction survives International Shoe; all nine Justices agreed on the result, but split 4–4 between tradition and fairness as the reason.

### Reading: *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown* (CB 1107–12)
**Rule: "At home."** A court may hear any and all claims against a foreign (sister-state or foreign-country) corporation only when its affiliations with the state are "so '**continuous and systematic**' as to render [it] **essentially at home** in the forum State."
- **Paradigm forums:** For an **individual**, **domicile**. For a **corporation**, "an equivalent place": **place of incorporation** and **principal place of business**.
- **Stream of commerce doesn't create general jurisdiction.** Product flow into the forum "may bolster an affiliation germane to **specific** jurisdiction," but those ties don't establish **general** jurisdiction.
- **Purchases** in the forum, "even if occurring at regular intervals," aren't enough (Helicopteros).
- **Specific jurisdiction** depends on an "affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy," mainly activity or an occurrence **in the forum state**.

**Case: Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown** (U.S. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - A bus overturned outside **Paris**, killing two **North Carolina** boys. Their parents sued in North Carolina state court, blaming a **tire made in Turkey**.
  - Defendants: **Goodyear USA** (Ohio; didn't contest jurisdiction) and three **foreign subsidiaries** (Luxembourg, Turkey, France).
  - The subsidiaries had no North Carolina registration, office, employees, or bank accounts, and didn't advertise or sell there themselves. Other Goodyear affiliates distributed **tens of thousands** of their tires in North Carolina (out of tens of millions made). The tire model in the crash was **never distributed** there.
- **Issue:** Are foreign subsidiaries subject to general jurisdiction in North Carolina on claims unrelated to the forum because some of their products reached the state through the stream of commerce?
- **Holding:** No. **Reversed.** Specific jurisdiction was unavailable because the accident occurred in France and the tire was made and sold abroad. The North Carolina courts "elided the essential difference" between specific and general jurisdiction. The subsidiaries were "**in no sense at home**" in North Carolina.
- **Takeaway:** General jurisdiction requires the defendant to be essentially at home in the forum, and stream-of-commerce sales that might support specific jurisdiction can't establish general jurisdiction.

**Case: Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.** (U.S. 1952) (described in Goodyear)
- **Facts:** A **Philippine** mining company stopped operating in the Philippines during the Japanese occupation in WWII. Its president ran its limited wartime business **from an office in Ohio** and kept company files there. It was sued in Ohio on a claim that didn't arise there.
- **Holding:** Ohio could adjudicate the claim consistent with due process.
- **Takeaway:** Perkins is "the textbook case" of general jurisdiction over a foreign corporation: Ohio was effectively the company's **principal, if temporary, place of business**.

**Case: Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall** (U.S. 1984) (described in Goodyear)
- **Facts:** A **Colombian** company's helicopter crashed in **Peru**, killing U.S. citizens, whose survivors sued in Texas. The company had sent its CEO to **Houston** to negotiate a contract, accepted checks drawn on a Houston bank, **bought helicopters and training** from a Texas company, and sent personnel there for training.
- **Holding:** These contacts were **not** "continuous and systematic general business contacts" and couldn't support jurisdiction over unrelated claims.
- **Takeaway:** Substantial, repeated **purchases** and business trips to a forum still fall short of general jurisdiction.

## Class 33 (11/10) — Specific & General Jurisdiction (cont.)

### Reading: *Daimler AG v. Bauman* (CB 1114–22)
**Rule: "At home" means at home.** General jurisdiction over a corporation requires affiliations "so continuous and systematic as to render it **essentially at home**." A corporation isn't subject to general jurisdiction in every state where it "engages in a **substantial, continuous, and systematic course of business**." That formulation is "**unacceptably grasping**."
- **Paradigm forums:** **place of incorporation** and **principal place of business**. They're unique and easy to identify, and they give plaintiffs **at least one clear forum**.
- **Exceptional case:** Daimler leaves open that a corporation **might** be at home somewhere else in an exceptional case (Perkins), but doesn't say where.
- **Predictability:** Defendants must be able to "structure their primary conduct with some minimum assurance as to where that conduct will and will not render them liable to suit."
- **Subsidiaries:** A parent isn't subject to general jurisdiction just because its subsidiary or agent is. The Ninth Circuit's agency test (are the subsidiary's services "**important**" to the parent?) is rejected. Even if a subsidiary's contacts are **imputed** to the parent, the **parent itself** must be at home in the forum.
- **International comity:** Other countries (e.g., the EU) limit general jurisdiction to a corporation's domicile. Expansive U.S. general jurisdiction risks friction with them, which reinforces that it would offend fair play and substantial justice.
- **Specific vs. general over time:** Since International Shoe, **specific jurisdiction has expanded**, while **general jurisdiction has shrunk**.

**Case: Daimler AG v. Bauman** (U.S. 2014)
- **Facts:**
  - **22 Argentinian residents** sued **Daimler** (a German company) in **federal court in California**. They alleged that its Argentine subsidiary, **MB Argentina**, collaborated with security forces to kidnap, torture, and kill workers during Argentina's 1976–1983 "**Dirty War**." Nothing happened in California or the United States.
  - Jurisdiction rested on the California contacts of **MBUSA**, Daimler's U.S. distributor (Delaware corporation, principal place of business in **New Jersey**). MBUSA had several California facilities and was the **largest luxury-car supplier** in California; its California sales were **2.4%** of Daimler's worldwide sales.
  - Neither Daimler nor MBUSA was incorporated or headquartered in California.
- **Issue:** Can a California court exercise general jurisdiction over a German parent, based on its U.S. subsidiary's California contacts, for foreign plaintiffs' claims about conduct in Argentina?
- **Holding:** No. **Reversed.** Even **assuming** MBUSA is at home in California and its contacts are imputed to Daimler, Daimler's "**slim contacts**" don't make Daimler itself at home there. If they did, the same reach would exist in every state where MBUSA's sales are sizable, which would be "**exorbitant**."
  - **Sotomayor (concurring in the judgment):** omitted from the casebook.
- **Takeaway:** General jurisdiction over a corporation is generally limited to its place of incorporation and principal place of business, and a subsidiary's in-state business doesn't make the parent at home there.

**Lecture summary (general jurisdiction):**
- **Tag** jurisdiction if served in the forum state, unless brought there by force or fraud or present as a party or witness in another case (Burnham). There's a **circuit split** on whether a corporation can be "tagged" by serving its CEO in the state.
- **Individuals:** general jurisdiction in their **domicile**.
- **Corporations:** general jurisdiction where **at home**: (1) **place of incorporation**; (2) **principal place of business** (Goodyear); maybe (3) another state where affiliations are so continuous and systematic as to make it essentially at home (Daimler).
- **Unincorporated associations:** many lower courts say (1) **state of formation** and (2) **principal place of business**.
- **Daimler in lecture:** Having a regional distribution office in California wasn't enough to make MBUSA (let alone Daimler) at home there. Lots of companies do business in California.

### Reading: *be2 LLC v. Ivanov* (CB 1123–27)
**Rule: Internet contacts require targeting.** A defendant isn't subject to jurisdiction "simply because the defendant owns or operates a website that is **accessible in the forum state**, even if that site is '**interactive**.'" The defendant "must in some way **target the forum state's market**," that is, **deliberately target or exploit** it.
- A few forum residents registering on the site are "**attenuated contacts**" that can't support jurisdiction.
- A defendant's **lack of credibility** isn't a forum contact. The question is still whether the record shows minimum contacts.
- **Default strategy:** A defendant who **knew about the suit and chose to default** bears the **burden of proof** on a post-judgment motion challenging PJ. A judgment entered without PJ is **void** and must be vacated.
- **Federal analysis:** The Lanham Act has no nationwide service, so **Rule 4(k)(1)(A)** applies. Illinois's long-arm statute reaches the constitutional limit, so the question becomes federal due process.
- The Supreme Court has left **virtual contacts** "for another day" (Walden), so lower courts have developed the doctrine.

**Case: be2 LLC v. Ivanov** (7th Cir. 2011)
- **Facts:**
  - **be2 LLC** (Delaware) and its German parent run the dating site **be2.com**. They sued **Ivanov** (New Jersey), allegedly CEO of the **confusingly similar be2.net**, in federal court in **Illinois** for trademark infringement.
  - Ivanov didn't answer, and the court entered a **default judgment**. Ivanov then moved to vacate for lack of PJ, claiming he was just an unpaid volunteer and that "CEO" meant "Centralized Expert Operator." The court found him **not credible** ("preposterous").
  - The only Illinois evidence: **20 free be2.net profiles** with Chicago-area addresses (in lecture notes: 26 Illinois sign-ups).
- **Issue:** Does running a website accessible in Illinois, on which about 20 Illinois residents created free profiles, give the operator minimum contacts with Illinois?
- **Holding:** No. **Reversed and remanded** with instructions to vacate the judgment and **dismiss for lack of PJ**. Nothing showed Ivanov targeted Illinois. A few resident registrations are attenuated contacts.
- **Takeaway:** For internet contacts, the defendant must target the forum's market; an accessible or interactive website plus a few forum users isn't enough.

**Lecture hypotheticals (internet and specific jurisdiction):**
- **Colorado ski resort buys Google ads targeted at Texas and New Mexico:** Likely **yes** in Texas or New Mexico, if a customer who came because of the ads sues there.
- **Texas news site publishes a story about a Las Vegas celebrity jailed there; the celebrity sues for libel:** Apply the **Calder effects test**: (a) an intentional tort, (b) **expressly aimed** at the forum, (c) knowing the **brunt of the harm** would be felt there.
- **Cayman Islands website called "Best Houston Barbecue":** Likely **yes** in Texas for claims about the site's content (e.g., a Houston restaurant suing over a defamatory review).
- **Washington seller's interactive website takes a Texas order and ships to Texas:** Likely **yes** if the seller regularly sells to Texans. That's the top of the **Zippo** sliding scale: the seller knowingly takes Texas orders and ships into Texas, and the claim arises from that sale. (*McGee v. International Life*: even **one contract** deliberately made with a forum resident can be enough.)

### Reading: *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.* (Canvas)
**Rule: Consent by registration.** A state may require an out-of-state corporation, as a **condition of registering to do business**, to **consent to general jurisdiction** on any claim, even an out-of-state plaintiff's claim unconnected to the state. Such statutes "comport with the Due Process Clause" under ***Pennsylvania Fire*** (1917).
- **Consent is an independent basis** for PJ, alongside International Shoe's contacts-based specific and general jurisdiction. International Shoe added "an **additional road**" for **non-consenting** defendants; it didn't close the consent road (plurality).
- **PJ is a waivable personal right.** Many arrangements count as express or implied consent: failing to appear specially, forum-selection clauses, pretrial orders, and registering.
- **Lower courts must follow directly controlling Supreme Court precedent**, even if later cases seem to undermine it. Only the Supreme Court can overrule its own precedents.
- **Open question: dormant Commerce Clause.** Such statutes may **unduly burden interstate commerce** (Alito). The issue was left for remand.

**Case: Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co.** (U.S. 2023)
- **Facts:**
  - **Mallory** worked for Norfolk Southern for nearly 20 years in **Ohio and Virginia**, allegedly exposed to asbestos and carcinogens. He developed cancer and, while living in **Virginia**, sued under FELA in **Pennsylvania** state court.
  - Norfolk Southern is incorporated and headquartered in **Virginia**. **No specific jurisdiction** (the claim arose in Ohio and Virginia) and **no contacts-based general jurisdiction** (it's at home only in Virginia).
  - But Norfolk Southern had **registered** as a foreign corporation in Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania law says registration lets its courts exercise **general jurisdiction** on "**any cause of action**."
  - It had a big Pennsylvania presence: about **5,000 employees**, **2,400+ miles of track**, and North America's largest locomotive shop.
- **Issue:** Does due process bar a state from requiring an out-of-state corporation to consent to general jurisdiction as a condition of registering to do business there?
- **Holding:** No (5–4). **Vacated and remanded.** *Pennsylvania Fire* controls, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court erred by treating it as implicitly overruled.
  - **Gorsuch (majority for Parts I and III-B; plurality otherwise):** Consent statutes have a long history. If a Virginia train conductor can be **tagged** while passing through Pennsylvania, it would be "incongruous" to shield the railroad. Norfolk Southern took "full advantage" of the Pennsylvania market and isn't a victim of local prejudice.
  - **Jackson (concurring):** Norfolk Southern **waived** its PJ rights by registering.
  - **Alito (concurring in part):** Due process is satisfied for a large corporation with substantial in-state operations, but there's a "good prospect" the scheme violates the **dormant Commerce Clause**.
  - **Dissent (Barrett, Roberts, Kagan, Kavanaugh):** "Consent" here is just a **relabeled** general-jurisdiction long-arm statute that **evades Daimler** and offends interstate federalism.
- **Takeaway:** Registering to do business under a consent statute can subject a corporation to general jurisdiction outside its at-home states, though a dormant Commerce Clause challenge to such statutes remains open.

**Lecture summary:** States can require corporations to consent to general jurisdiction in order to register to do business. Whether that violates the **dormant Commerce Clause** is an **open question**.

# UNIT XI — Venue & Transfer (Class 34)

**Venue** is a **separate requirement** layered on top of SMJ and PJ: among courts that have jurisdiction, **which district** is the proper place for the case? Like PJ, venue accounts for **inconvenience to the parties** and the **connection between the dispute and the forum**. Like PJ, an improper-venue objection is **waivable** (12(b)(3), 12(h)(1); § 1406(b)).

**Three tools for "where":**

| | **Venue (§ 1391)** | **Transfer (§§ 1404, 1406)** | **Forum non conveniens** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Statute | Statute (1948) | **Common law** |
| Question | Is this district a **proper** place to file? | Should the case be **moved** to another **federal** district? | Is another forum (state or **foreign**) much better suited? |
| Remedy | Dismissal or § 1406 transfer if venue is improper | **Transfer** within the federal system | **Dismissal** (plaintiff refiles elsewhere) |
| Key authorities | §§ 1390, 1391 | § 1404(a) (proper venue); § 1406(a) (improper venue) | Gulf Oil, Piper |

## Class 34 (11/11) — Venue; Transfer

### Reading: Venue and Transfer; 28 U.S.C. §§ 1390, 1391 (CB 1167–69)
**Rule § 1390(a): Definition.** "Venue" means "the **geographic specification** of the proper court or courts for the litigation of a civil action that is **within the subject-matter jurisdiction** of the district courts in general." A statute limiting which district has **SMJ** isn't a venue provision.

**Rule § 1390(c): Removed cases.** The venue chapter **doesn't determine** which district a state case is **removed** to (§ 1441(a) does: the district embracing the state court). But it **does govern transfer** of a removed case between districts and divisions.

**Rule § 1391(a):** "Except as otherwise provided by law," § 1391 governs venue in **all civil actions** in federal district court.

**Rule § 1391(b): Where venue is proper.**
- **(b)(1) Residence:** "a judicial district in which **any defendant resides**, if **all defendants are residents of the State** in which the district is located." The plaintiff's residence doesn't matter.
- **(b)(2) Events or property:** "a judicial district in which a **substantial part of the events or omissions** giving rise to the claim occurred, or a substantial part of **property** that is the subject of the action is situated." More than one district can qualify ("a substantial part," not "the most substantial part").
- **(b)(3) Fallback:** **Only if no district qualifies** under (b)(1) or (b)(2), "any judicial district in which **any defendant is subject to the court's personal jurisdiction** with respect to such action."

**Rule § 1391(c): Residence.**
- **(c)(1) Natural persons** (including lawful permanent residents) reside in the district of their **domicile**.
- **(c)(2) Entities** (incorporated or not), **as defendants**, reside in **any district where they're subject to PJ** for the action in question.
- **(c)(3) Defendants not resident in the U.S.** may be sued in **any district**, and they're **disregarded** when determining venue for the other defendants.

**Rule § 1391(d): Corporations in multi-district states.** A corporation resides in **any district** in the state where its contacts **would support PJ if that district were a separate state**. If no district qualifies, it resides in the district with the **most significant contacts**.

**Venue roadmap:**
1. Is this an originally filed case? (Removed cases go where § 1441(a) says; § 1390(c).)
2. Determine each defendant's **residence**: (c)(1) individuals, (c)(2) entities, (d) corporations in multi-district states, and drop non-U.S. residents under (c)(3).
3. **(b)(1):** Do all remaining defendants reside in one state? If so, any district there where any defendant resides works.
4. **(b)(2):** Where did a **substantial part** of the events or omissions occur, or where is a substantial part of the property?
5. **(b)(3):** Only if steps 3 and 4 produce nothing: any district where any defendant is subject to PJ.

### Reading: *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert* (CB 1171–77)
**Rule: Forum non conveniens.** Even when jurisdiction and venue are proper, a court may **decline to hear** a case because another forum is more convenient, after weighing **private** and **public** interest factors. The doctrine **never applies** if jurisdiction is lacking or venue is wrong.
- **Strong presumption for the plaintiff's choice:** "Unless the balance is **strongly in favor of the defendant**, the plaintiff's choice of forum should **rarely be disturbed**." A plaintiff may not choose an inconvenient forum to "vex," "harass," or "oppress" the defendant.
- **Private interest factors** (the litigants):
  - Relative **ease of access to sources of proof**
  - Availability of **compulsory process** for unwilling witnesses, and the cost of bringing willing ones
  - Possibility of a **view of the premises**
  - All other practical problems that make trial "easy, expeditious and inexpensive"
  - **Enforceability** of a judgment
- **Public interest factors** (the courts and community):
  - **Administrative difficulties** of court congestion
  - The burden of **jury duty** on a community with no relation to the case
  - The **local interest** in having localized controversies decided at home
  - Having a diversity case tried in a forum **at home with the governing state law**, avoiding conflict-of-laws problems and foreign law

**Case: Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert** (U.S. 1947)
- **Facts:**
  - **Gilbert**, who ran a public warehouse in **Lynchburg, Virginia**, sued **Gulf Oil** (a Pennsylvania corporation qualified to do business in Virginia and New York) in the **Southern District of New York**, based on diversity.
  - He alleged Gulf Oil **carelessly delivered gasoline**, causing an explosion and fire that destroyed the warehouse and customers' goods. He sought about **$365,000**.
  - Every event and almost every witness (about **350 customers** who stored goods, firefighters, and others) was in or near Lynchburg, about 400 miles from New York. Gulf Oil also wanted to implead a Virginia contractor that it couldn't bring into a New York court.
  - Gilbert's only stated reason for New York: a Virginia jury might be "staggered" by a claim near **$400,000**.
- **Issue:** Does a federal district court have inherent power to dismiss a case under forum non conveniens? If so, was dismissal an abuse of discretion?
- **Holding:** Yes, the power exists, and dismissal was **not an abuse of discretion**. **Reversed** (reinstating the dismissal). The Court refused to "entertain a provincial fear of the provincialism of a Virginia jury." Virginia was the source of all proof, and a Virginia court would know the governing law.
  - **Dissent (Black, joined by Rutledge):** Whether federal courts should decline cases for convenience is a policy question for **Congress**: "Whether the doctrine of forum non conveniens is good or bad, I should wait for Congress to adopt it."
- **Takeaway:** A federal court may dismiss for forum non conveniens when the private and public interest factors strongly favor another forum, but the plaintiff's choice of forum should rarely be disturbed.

### Reading: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1404, 1406 (CB 1177–78)
**Introductory note:** In **1948** (after Gulf Oil), Congress authorized **transfer** between federal districts "for the convenience of the parties and witnesses [and] the interest of justice." The statutes allow transfer **only within the federal system**. Sending a case to a **state or foreign court** still requires **dismissal without prejudice** (i.e., forum non conveniens).
- A **division** is the part of a district served by a single courthouse. A one-courthouse district has no divisions.

**Rule § 1404(a): Transfer from a proper venue.** "For the **convenience of parties and witnesses**, in the **interest of justice**, a district court **may** transfer any civil action to any other district or division **where it might have been brought** or to any district or division to which **all parties have consented**."
- Discretionary ("may"). The original venue is **proper**; transfer is for **convenience**.
- The transferee court must be one where the case **could have been filed** (proper venue and jurisdiction there), unless all parties consent.

**Rule § 1404(b):** On motion, consent, or stipulation of **all parties**, a case (or any motion or hearing in it) may be transferred to **another division in the same district**, in the court's discretion.

**Rule § 1404(c):** A court may order a civil action **tried at any place within its division**.

**Rule § 1406(a): Wrong venue.** A district court where a case is filed "laying venue in the **wrong** division or district **shall dismiss**, or **if it be in the interest of justice, transfer** such case to any district or division in which it **could have been brought**."
- Mandatory to act ("shall"): the court must dismiss or transfer.

**Rule § 1406(b): Waiver.** The chapter doesn't impair a court's jurisdiction over a party who doesn't make a **timely and sufficient objection to venue**. Venue objections are **waivable** (and under 12(h)(1), lost if left out of the first Rule 12 motion or answer).

| | **§ 1404(a)** | **§ 1406(a)** | **Forum non conveniens** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Original venue | **Proper** | **Improper** | Proper |
| Destination | Another **federal** district where the case might have been brought (or all parties consent) | Another **federal** district where it could have been brought | Any better forum, usually **state or foreign** |
| Remedy | Transfer (discretionary) | Dismiss, or transfer in the interest of justice | Dismissal |

### Reading: *Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno* (CB 1180–90)
**Rule: An unfavorable change in law doesn't bar forum non conveniens dismissal.** "The possibility of a change in substantive law should ordinarily **not be given conclusive or even substantial weight** in the forum non conveniens inquiry."
- **Exception:** If the alternative forum's remedy is "**so clearly inadequate or unsatisfactory that it is no remedy at all**," the change in law may get substantial weight.
- **An alternative forum must exist.** Here the defendants agreed to submit to Scottish jurisdiction and waive statute-of-limitations defenses.
- **Less deference to foreign plaintiffs:** A plaintiff's choice gets a strong presumption, especially when the plaintiff chose its **home forum** (it's reasonable to assume that's convenient). When the plaintiff or real parties in interest are **foreign**, that assumption is "much less reasonable," so the choice gets **less deference**.
- **Review:** Forum non conveniens rests in the trial court's **sound discretion** and is reversed only for a **clear abuse of discretion**.
- **Defendants needn't detail every unavailable witness.** They must give enough information for the court to balance the interests.
- **Why change in law can't control:** It would make the doctrine "virtually useless," since plaintiffs pick the forum with the most favorable law. It would force courts into complex comparative law. And it would draw even more foreign plaintiffs into already crowded U.S. courts.

**Case: Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno** (U.S. 1981)
- **Facts:**
  - A small plane crashed in the **Scottish highlands**, killing the pilot and five passengers, all **Scottish**. The plane was made in **Pennsylvania** (Piper); the propellers were made in **Ohio** (Hartzell). The plane was registered in Britain and owned, maintained, and operated by British companies. A British investigation suggested possible **pilot error**.
  - **Reyno**, a legal secretary to the plaintiffs' lawyer, was appointed administratrix by a California court and sued Piper and Hartzell in **California state court**. She admitted she chose the U.S. because its law was **more favorable**: Scotland didn't recognize **strict liability** and allowed only relatives to sue for limited damages.
  - **Procedural moves:** The defendants **removed** to federal court in California. Piper moved to **transfer under § 1404(a)** to the Middle District of Pennsylvania; Hartzell moved to dismiss for lack of **PJ** or transfer. The court **transferred** the case to Pennsylvania. The defendants then moved to dismiss for **forum non conveniens**, and the court granted it. The Third Circuit reversed.
- **Issue:** Can a plaintiff defeat a forum non conveniens dismissal by showing the alternative forum's law is less favorable? Did the district court abuse its discretion?
- **Holding:** No and no. **Reversed** (dismissal reinstated). Scotland's remedy was less generous but not "no remedy at all." The district court reasonably gave **less deference** to foreign real parties in interest and reasonably weighed the factors:
  - **Private:** Key evidence and witnesses (maintenance, pilot training, the investigation, damages) were in Britain. The defendants couldn't **implead** the Scottish pilot's estate, owner, and operator in the U.S., risking inconsistent verdicts.
  - **Public:** Trial in Pennsylvania would apply Pennsylvania law to Piper and **Scottish law** to Hartzell, confusing the jury. Scotland had a **very strong interest** in the case.
- **Takeaway:** A forum non conveniens dismissal isn't barred just because the alternative forum's law is less favorable to the plaintiff, and a foreign plaintiff's choice of a U.S. forum gets less deference.

**Exam angle (sequence of motions in Piper):** Removal → § 1404(a) transfer to a district with a strong forum non conveniens case → forum non conveniens dismissal to a foreign court. Consider why the defendants made each move, and what court interests the sequence served or disserved.

## Must-know tests

**Due Process and notice**
- **Mullane:** notice must be **reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances**, to apprise parties, with time to respond. It need not be actual notice.
- **Jones:** if the sender **knows** notice failed, take **additional reasonable steps** if practicable. **Greene:** posting alone fails where notices get torn down.
- **Doehr:** weigh (1) the private interest, (2) the **risk of erroneous deprivation** and the value of safeguards, (3) the interest of the party seeking the remedy plus the government's interest.

**Preliminary relief**
- **Winter PI:** likely success, **likely** irreparable harm, balance of equities, public interest.

**Pleading**
- **Twombly/Iqbal:** (1) disregard conclusions, (2) ask whether the remaining facts **plausibly** show liability, in light of obvious alternative explanations.
- **Rule 8(d):** alternative and **inconsistent** pleading is OK if genuinely uncertain (McCormick).
- **Rule 11(b):** no improper purpose, warranted law, **evidentiary support after reasonable inquiry**, warranted denials.
- **12(h):** (b)(2)–(5) waived if omitted from the first motion or answer. (b)(6) can be raised through trial. SMJ can be raised anytime.
- **Foman (amendment):** undue delay, bad faith, repeated failure to cure, **undue prejudice**, futility.

**Discovery**
- **Rule 26(b)(1):** nonprivileged + relevant + **proportional**. Admissibility isn't required.
- **Work product (Hickman, 26(b)(3)):** prepared in anticipation of litigation by a party or its representative. Fact work product yields to **substantial need + undue hardship**. Opinion work product is protected. **Facts aren't protected.**
- **Upjohn:** employee communications to counsel at superiors' direction, for legal advice, confidential. The control-group test is rejected.
- **Crime-fraud:** probable cause that the communication **furthered** a crime or fraud. The client's purpose controls.

**Summary judgment**
- **Rule 56 / Anderson:** **no genuine dispute of material fact** (could a reasonable jury find for the nonmovant?) + entitled to judgment as a matter of law. View evidence most favorably to the nonmovant. No credibility calls.
- **Celotex:** a movant without the trial burden can just **point to the absence of evidence**. The nonmovant must then show specific facts.
- **Scott vs. Tolan:** a video that **blatantly contradicts** one side ends the dispute. Testimony against testimony goes to the jury.

**Trial**
- **7th Amendment (Terry):** (1) an 18th-century analog, (2) **legal vs. equitable remedy** (more important). Damages are legal unless restitutionary or incidental to an injunction.

**Appeal**
- **Collateral order (Cohen/Mohawk):** **conclusive**; **important and separate from the merits**; **effectively unreviewable** after final judgment. Judged **categorically**. Alternatives: § 1292(b), mandamus, contempt (Microsoft).
- **Standards of review:** law → de novo; facts → clearly erroneous; discretionary calls → abuse of discretion.

**Preclusion and joinder**
- **Claim preclusion:** same parties/privies, competent court, **final judgment on the merits**, **same claim** (transactional nucleus of facts). It bars what was or **could have been** raised.
- **Issue preclusion:** same issue **actually litigated**, **necessarily decided**, final judgment, **full and fair opportunity** for the party bound.
- **Parklane (offensive nonmutual):** discretion. Refuse if the plaintiff **could easily have joined** or it's **unfair** (low stakes or unforeseeable future suits, inconsistent judgments, new procedural opportunities).
- **Taylor's six nonparty exceptions:** agreement, substantive legal relationship, adequate representation, control, proxy, statutory scheme.
- **Rule 19(a):** complete relief / impair the absentee's interest / risk of inconsistent obligations. **19(b):** the equity-and-good-conscience factors.
- **Rule 20:** same transaction, occurrence, or series **+** a common question.

**Class actions**
- **Rule 23(a):** numerosity, commonality (Dukes: a common contention resolvable "in one stroke"), typicality, adequacy (Hansberry).
- **Rule 23(b):** (b)(1) inconsistent standards / limited fund; (b)(2) injunction for the class as a whole; (b)(3) **predominance + superiority**, with **best practicable notice** and **opt-out**.
- **Whirlpool:** a liability-only (b)(3) class is fine despite individual damages; owners without the defect yet can be included on a point-of-sale theory.
- **23(e):** settlement needs court approval after a hearing: **fair, reasonable, and adequate**.

**Subject matter jurisdiction**
- **Two steps:** within Article III **and** within a statute.
- **§ 1331 / Mottley:** the federal issue must be in the **well-pleaded complaint**; anticipated federal defenses don't count.
- **Gunn/Grable** (state claim with a federal issue): **necessarily raised, actually disputed, substantial** (to the federal system), **no disruption** of the federal-state balance.
- **§ 1332:** **complete diversity** (Strawbridge) + **more than $75,000**, measured at **filing** (Grupo Dataflux).
- **Citizenship:** people → **domicile** (residence + intent to remain indefinitely; presumption of continuity; clear and convincing proof of change). Corporations → **every state of incorporation + nerve center** (Hertz). LLCs/partnerships → **every member** (Lincoln Benefit).
- **Amount:** **legal certainty** test (St. Paul Mercury); count punitive damages and injunction value (AFA Tours); declaratory actions use the value of the object, minus undisputed amounts (Freeland). Post-removal reductions don't oust jurisdiction.
- **§ 1367:** (a) same case or controversy (**common nucleus**); (b) in diversity-only cases, no jurisdiction over plaintiffs' claims against Rule 14/19/20/24 parties or Rule 19/24 plaintiffs; (c) discretion to decline. **Exxon:** one plaintiff meeting the amount lets Rule 20 and Rule 23 co-plaintiffs ride along; complete diversity still required.
- **Removal:** defendants only; all defendants consent; **30 days**; **forum-defendant rule** for diversity; **1-year** limit for diversity; remand orders unreviewable. **Caterpillar:** a defect cured before judgment saves the judgment. **Grupo Dataflux:** a change in a continuing party's citizenship can't cure; dropping a dispensable nondiverse party can.

**Personal jurisdiction**
- **Two steps:** (1) state **long-arm statute** (federal courts borrow it under **Rule 4(k)(1)(A)**); (2) **due process**. Most long-arm statutes reach the constitutional limit, so the steps usually collapse.
- **Int'l Shoe:** **minimum contacts** such that the suit doesn't offend "**traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice**."
- **Specific jurisdiction:** (1) the **defendant's own purposeful** contacts with the **forum state** (not with the plaintiff: Walden); (2) the claim **arises out of or relates to** them; (3) **reasonableness** (WWVW factors: burden on the defendant, forum state's interest, plaintiff's interest in relief, judicial efficiency, shared social policies).
- **Foreseeability alone fails;** the defendant must "reasonably anticipate being haled into court there" (WWVW). **Unilateral** acts of the plaintiff or third parties don't count.
- **Stream of commerce (Nicastro):** plurality = **targeting** the forum; Breyer = **regular flow** or "**something more**"; dissent = targeting the U.S. as a whole is enough. Argue all three.
- **Effects test (Calder, as read by Walden):** intentional tort **expressly aimed** at the forum, with the brunt of the harm felt there, connecting the defendant to the **state itself**. Mere injury to a forum resident isn't enough.
- **Internet (be2):** an accessible or even interactive website isn't enough; the defendant must **target** the forum's market.
- **General jurisdiction:** "**essentially at home**" (Goodyear, Daimler): individuals → **domicile**; corporations → **state of incorporation + principal place of business** (maybe an exceptional case like Perkins). Doing substantial business isn't enough, and a subsidiary's contacts don't make the parent at home.
- **Traditional bases:** **tag** (Burnham; not by force or fraud), **consent** (appearing without objection; registration statutes per Mallory, subject to an open dormant Commerce Clause question).
- **Waiver:** PJ is lost if not raised in the first Rule 12 motion or answer (12(h)(1)). A defendant who **defaults** can attack the judgment as void later but bears the burden (be2).

**Venue and transfer**
- **§ 1391(b):** (1) any district where **any defendant resides** if **all defendants reside in that state**; (2) where a **substantial part of the events or omissions** occurred or property is; (3) **only if neither works**, any district where any defendant is subject to PJ.
- **Residence (§ 1391(c)–(d)):** people → **domicile** district; entity defendants → **anywhere subject to PJ** for this action; corporations in multi-district states → districts whose contacts would support PJ **as if each were a separate state**; non-U.S. residents → sued anywhere and **ignored** for the others.
- **§ 1404(a):** venue **proper**; transfer for **convenience and interest of justice** to a district where the case **might have been brought** (or all parties consent).
- **§ 1406(a):** venue **improper**; court **shall dismiss** or, in the interest of justice, **transfer**.
- **Forum non conveniens (Gulf Oil, Piper):** an **adequate alternative forum** exists + private and public interest factors **strongly** favor it → **dismiss**. Plaintiff's choice is rarely disturbed, but gets **less deference** if the plaintiff is **foreign**. **Less favorable law** in the other forum isn't a bar unless the remedy is **no remedy at all**. Used when the better forum is **state or foreign** (transfer statutes can't send cases there).
