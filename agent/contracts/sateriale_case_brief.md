# Case Brief: Sateriale v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co.

**Citation:** 697 F.3d 777 (9th Cir. 2012)
**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
**Judges:** Noonan & Fisher, Circuit Judges; Mueller, District Judge (sitting by designation); opinion by Fisher, C.J.
**Posture:** Appeal from dismissal for failure to state a claim, Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6); reviewed de novo.
**Governing law:** California.

## Facts

From 1991 to 2007, R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Company (RJR) ran "Camel Cash," a customer loyalty program for Camel cigarettes. Camel Cash certificates ("C-Notes") were included in cigarette packages and stated, in part: *"USE THIS NEW C-NOTE AND THE C-NOTES YOU'VE BEEN SAVING TO GET THE BEST GOODS CAMEL HAS TO OFFER,"* with a phone number for a free catalog, plus restrictions ("Offer restricted to smokers 21 years of age or older," "Offer good only in the USA," "Limit 5 requests for a catalog per household"). Ten plaintiffs joined by purchasing Camel products, saving C-Notes, and submitting signed registration forms; RJR issued each an enrollment number and periodically mailed merchandise catalogs. Camel's market share nearly doubled (roughly 4% to over 7%) over the life of the program.

In October 2006, RJR announced the program would terminate March 31, 2007, telling members they still had until then to redeem C-Notes. Despite that assurance, RJR allegedly stopped printing and issuing catalogs after October 2006 and told consumers no redemption merchandise was available, leaving plaintiffs holding worthless, unredeemed certificates.

## Procedural History

Plaintiffs sued RJR for breach of contract, promissory estoppel, and violations of California's Unfair Competition Law (UCL) and Consumer Legal Remedies Act (CLRA). The district court dismissed the entire third amended complaint under Rule 12(b)(6). Plaintiffs appealed.

## Issues

1. Did the plaintiffs adequately allege the existence of an **offer** — specifically, an offer to enter a **unilateral contract** — rather than a mere advertisement/invitation to negotiate?
2. If so, was any resulting contract too **indefinite** to enforce?
3. Did RJR's reserved right to terminate the program defeat the contract for lack of **mutuality of obligation**?

## Holding

The Ninth Circuit affirmed dismissal of the UCL and CLRA claims but **reversed** dismissal of the breach-of-contract and promissory-estoppel claims, holding that the complaint adequately alleged an offer to form a **unilateral contract** — not a mere advertisement — and that neither indefiniteness nor lack of mutuality barred the claim at the pleading stage.

## Reasoning

**No offer to form a bilateral contract.** A bilateral contract requires mutual promises exchanged by both sides. Plaintiffs identified only a promise by RJR (to allow redemption); they pointed to no return promise they made, nor did RJR seek one. So no bilateral-contract offer was adequately alleged.

**An offer to form a unilateral contract, however, was adequately alleged — and this is the case's central holding.** A unilateral contract is a promise exchanged for a *performance*, accepted by rendering that performance rather than by promising in return (Restatement (Second) § 45 cmt. a; typified by reward and prize offers).

RJR argued the C-Notes and catalogs were, like ordinary advertisements, mere "invitations to make an offer" under the common-law rule that "[a]dvertisements of goods by display, sign, handbill, newspaper, radio or television are not ordinarily intended or understood as offers to sell" (Restatement (Second) § 26 cmt. b), citing analogous rulings for other customer-rewards programs (*Leonard v. Pepsico*; *Alligood v. Procter & Gamble*). **The court rejected this characterization** on two independent grounds:

1. It was unclear California law even adopted the general common-law advertisement rule in the first place (*Donovan v. RRL Corp.*, criticizing the rule as inconsistent with consumers' reasonable expectations).
2. Even assuming the general rule applied, it carries a long-recognized **exception for offers of a reward** — including rewards for the redemption of coupons (Corbin on Contracts § 2.4) — and Camel Cash fit squarely within that exception, as other courts had found for similar loyalty/rewards programs (e.g., *Wolens v. American Airlines*, frequent-flyer miles).

**The court rejected treating this as an ordinary advertisement because the policy behind the general rule — protection against over-acceptance — does not apply here.** The general rule exists because a seller who advertises a limited stock at a set price could otherwise face liability to far more acceptances than it has goods to satisfy (quoting Farnsworth § 3.10: a sold-out shopkeeper could be swarmed by customers "demand[ing] to be served," each with "a right of action... for not performing"). That concern is absent from Camel Cash for two reasons:

- **RJR was not selling a limited inventory of cigarettes to the public via the C-Notes — it was trying to sell as many cigarettes as possible.** The program's entire purpose was to induce *more* purchases, not to move a capped stock at a fixed price.
- **RJR alone controlled the number of possible acceptances**, because it alone decided how many C-Notes to print and distribute and what catalogs to issue. It could never be "trapped" by acceptances exceeding its own supply, since it set that supply. As Farnsworth put it, "if the very nature of a proposal restricts its maker's potential liability to a reasonable number of people, there is no reason why it cannot be an offer."

Because the over-acceptance/limited-supply rationale for treating ads as non-offers had no purchase on these facts, the court found "no reason to presume" RJR's communications were not offers simply because they were addressed to the general public. Applying California's operative test — whether the advertiser, in clear and positive terms, promised to render performance in exchange for something requested, such that the recipient reasonably could conclude a contract would form by complying — the court held the totality of circumstances supported an offer: repeated use of the word "offer"; the absence of any disclaimer of intent to be bound; specific eligibility/quantity restrictions typical of a binding offer rather than mere puffery; a formal enrollment process with registration forms and enrollment numbers; and the substantial consumer reliance RJR invited and expected.

**Indefiniteness did not bar the claim.** RJR's broad discretion over catalog timing and merchandise did not defeat definiteness because (a) the alleged breach — ceasing to make *any* merchandise available — was readily discernible without pinning down every term, and (b) a rational basis for damages existed (e.g., RJR's own internal valuation of C-Notes at fixed cents-per-note, or its final catalog and pre-breach performance), especially where RJR had reserved a financial liability for outstanding C-Notes internally, and where plaintiffs' substantial reliance and RJR's substantial resulting benefit (nearly doubling Camel's market share) counseled courts to "go to great lengths" to find a construction that would salvage the agreement.

**Mutuality of obligation did not apply.** RJR argued that its unrestricted right to terminate the program at will meant the relationship lacked "mutuality of obligation" (the notion that "both parties must be bound or neither will be bound") and so could not be a binding contract. The court held this doctrine simply **does not apply to unilateral contracts**, where the offeree (here, the consumer) was never bound to keep performing in the first place — RJR's reserved discretion to end the program is not inconsistent with a unilateral-contract offer that consumers had already begun accepting by performance.

## Disposition

Affirmed in part (dismissal of UCL and CLRA claims), reversed in part (contract and promissory estoppel claims reinstated), and remanded.

## Significance

*Sateriale* is the modern, "new-style" counterpart to *Hamer v. Sidway*-era unilateral-contract analysis (per Professor Pettit): courts now invoke unilateral-contract doctrine to *impose* liability on a large, sophisticated offeror, rather than to let an offeror escape it. It is frequently paired with *Izadi v. Machado (Gus) Ford, Inc.* as the unilateral-contract counterpart to Izadi's bilateral, "advertisement-as-offer" analysis — see `agent/contracts/sateriale_izadi_analysis.md`.
