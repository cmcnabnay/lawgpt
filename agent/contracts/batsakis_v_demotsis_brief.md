# Case Brief: Batsakis v. Demotsis

**Citation:** 226 S.W.2d 673 (Tex. Civ. App.—El Paso 1949)

## Facts

In April 1942, during the German occupation of Greece, Eugenia Demotsis was in Athens/Peiraeus and in severe financial distress, unable to access her money and property in the United States. George Batsakis agreed to lend her 500,000 drachmae (worth approximately $25 in U.S. currency at the time) on the condition that she sign a written instrument promising to repay him $2,000 in American money, plus 8% interest, either at the end of the war or whenever he could arrange collection through his representative in America. Demotsis signed the instrument and received the drachmae. She later refused to pay the full $2,000, arguing that the instrument was unsupported by adequate consideration — she had received only $25 worth of value in exchange for a promise to pay $2,000. The trial court agreed in part, awarding Batsakis only $750 plus interest. Batsakis appealed, seeking the full $2,000.

## Issue

Does a gross disparity between the value given and the value promised in return render a contract unenforceable for want (or failure) of consideration?

## Rule of Law

Courts will not inquire into the adequacy of consideration so long as consideration exists. A plea of *want* of consideration asserts that the instrument never became a valid obligation at all, while a plea of *failure* of consideration asserts that consideration promised was not in fact delivered. Where a party bargains for and receives exactly what was agreed upon, mere inadequacy — even gross inadequacy — of the exchanged value will not void the contract.

## Analysis

The court characterized the transaction as a sale by Batsakis of the 500,000 drachmae, with the consideration for that sale being Demotsis's execution of the instrument promising to repay $2,000. Demotsis's own testimony established that the parties had specifically bargained for this exchange: Batsakis told her he would give her the drachmae "provided" she signed the instrument agreeing to repay $2,000. Because she received precisely what she bargained for (the drachmae, in exchange for her promise), there was no *failure* of consideration. And because some value did pass between the parties — the drachmae were not worthless — there was no *want* of consideration either. The court expressly declined to weigh whether $25 (or even $750) in drachmae was fair exchange for a $2,000 promise; adequacy of consideration is not a judicial inquiry once a bargained-for exchange is shown to exist. The court also rejected Demotsis's attempt to value the drachmae by their U.S.-dollar exchange rate, noting the value to her — money usable in wartime Greece when she could not access her American assets — was a matter for the fact-finder, and in any event was irrelevant to the want/failure-of-consideration defenses actually pleaded.

## Conclusion

The Texas Court of Civil Appeals held the plea of want and failure of consideration unavailing and reformed the judgment to award Batsakis the full $2,000 principal plus 8% interest from April 2, 1942. **Rule takeaway:** courts examine whether consideration exists, not whether it is adequate or economically fair.

## Doctrinal significance

*Batsakis* stands for the classic "peppercorn theory" of consideration — courts do not sit as arbiters of a fair bargain. It is frequently paired with *[[dohrmann_v_swaney_brief|Dohrmann v. Swaney]]*, which shows the doctrine's limit: where the disparity in value is so extreme that it "shocks the conscience" *and* is accompanied by circumstances of unfair bargaining power, a contract may be set aside as unconscionable notwithstanding the general rule that adequacy of consideration is not examined.
