# Case Brief: Greiner v. Greiner

**Citation:** 131 Kan. 760, 293 P. 759 (1930)

## Facts

Peter Greiner died testate, disinheriting several of his children — including his sons Frank and Nicholas — leaving them only $5 apiece, while favoring other children in his will. After one of the favored sons, Henry, died intestate and his mother Maggie Greiner inherited substantial property from him, Maggie decided to equalize matters by giving Frank and Nicholas land. She summoned Frank, who had homesteaded and lived on land in a different county for sixteen or seventeen years, back to Mitchell County. In their conversation, Frank said he wanted land for a home rather than money; Maggie told him she had the land, wanted him to move onto it, and they would sort out details later. Frank agreed to move back. Maggie subsequently paid off a mortgage obstructing Frank's move, moved existing farm buildings from another tract onto a specific 80-acre tract, and told multiple family members (corroborated by several witnesses) that she was giving that 80-acre tract to Frank as his share, as a home. Frank moved his family onto the tract in 1927 and lived there for nearly a year, making valuable and lasting improvements, before Maggie changed course — after a dispute involving another son — and sued Frank to evict him and recover possession, refusing to execute a deed. The trial court ordered Maggie to execute a deed to Frank, and she appealed, arguing (1) her statements were merely expressions of future intention, not a binding promise, and (2) even if a promise existed, it lacked consideration since Frank gave nothing in return.

## Issue

Is an otherwise gratuitous promise of land, initially too indefinite to enforce, rendered binding where the promisor later makes the promise definite through her own conduct and the promisee detrimentally and substantially relies on it?

## Rule of Law

A contract requires only a promise for breach of which the law gives a remedy; it need not use any particular "crystallized" words ("I promise," "I agree"), and any manifestation by words or conduct that the promisee is justified in understanding as an expression of intent to promise is sufficient. Where a promise is initially too indefinite to enforce (e.g., no specific property identified), the promisor's subsequent conduct can render the promise definite and complete. Separately, under the doctrine later codified at Restatement of Contracts § 90 (promissory estoppel), a promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce definite and substantial action or forbearance by the promisee, and which does induce such action, is binding without traditional bargained-for consideration if injustice can be avoided only by enforcing the promise.

## Analysis

The court first addressed whether a "contract" existed at all, rejecting the argument that Maggie's statements were mere expressions of future intention. It found she unmistakably promised to give Frank land for a home if he moved back — the promise was simply indefinite at first because no specific tract had been identified. That indefiniteness was cured when Maggie later segregated the specific 80-acre tract for Frank, moved the buildings onto it to make it suitable for his home, and gave him possession — conduct that made the earlier promise perfectly definite. On the separate question of consideration, the court did not need to locate a traditional bargained-for exchange (Frank arguably gave nothing directly in return — his moving back was itself induced by, not exchanged for, the promise). Instead the court invoked § 90: Frank gave up his established homestead in another county, relocated his family, and made lasting, valuable improvements to the 80-acre tract in reliance on his mother's promise, precisely the kind of definite and substantial reliance the promisor should have expected. Denying him a deed after nearly a year of such reliance — where a money judgment could not adequately compensate him for the home he built — would work an injustice that only enforcement of the promise could avoid.

## Conclusion

The Kansas Supreme Court affirmed the judgment requiring Maggie Greiner to execute a deed conveying the 80-acre tract to Frank, holding the promise enforceable both as a promise that became sufficiently definite through the promisor's conduct and, independently, under the doctrine that would become promissory estoppel.

## Doctrinal significance

*Greiner* sits alongside *Kirksey v. Kirksey* (the brother-in-law's gratuitous invitation to the widow, discussed in `agent/contracts/episode_5_6_summary.md`) as a family gift-promise case, but reaches the opposite result: where *Kirksey* denied relief because the promise was purely gratuitous and unrelied-upon in a legally cognizable way, *Greiner* enforces the promise precisely because of the promisee's definite and substantial detrimental reliance — an early, explicit application of § 90 promissory estoppel to excuse the absence of bargained-for consideration. It also foreshadows *[[wright_v_newman_brief|Wright v. Newman]]*, decided under the same § 90 framework six decades later.
