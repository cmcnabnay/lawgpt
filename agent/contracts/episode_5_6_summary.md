## Episode 5: Unilateral Contracts — Offers, Revocation, and Advertisements

Building on *Cook v. Coldwell Banker/Frank Laiben Realty Co.*, 967 S.W.2d 654 (Mo. Ct. App. 1998), Episode 5 continues the unilateral-contract thread into *Sateriale v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co.*, 697 F.3d 777 (9th Cir. 2012) — the "Camel Cash" case.

Key concepts:

- **Unilateral vs. bilateral contracts.** A bilateral contract is an exchange of mutual promises; a unilateral contract is a promise exchanged for a performance, accepted only by rendering that performance (Restatement (Second) § 45 cmt. a). RJR's Camel Cash program could not be read as a bilateral offer because the plaintiffs never identified a return promise they made to RJR — but it could be read as an offer to enter a unilateral contract.
- **Advertisements as offers — the general rule and its exception.** The common-law default is that advertisements are invitations to negotiate, not offers (Restatement (Second) § 26 cmt. b), because of the risk of "over-acceptance." But a long-recognized exception treats offers of a **reward** — for capture of a fugitive, return of lost property, contest prizes, or **redemption of coupons** — as true offers, since the "very nature of the proposal" caps the offeror's exposure to a reasonable number of people (Farnsworth § 3.10). The Ninth Circuit held Camel Cash fell within the reward exception, not the general advertisement rule.
- **The over-acceptance rationale, and why it didn't apply.** The traditional rule protecting sellers from advertisements exists so a shopkeeper with limited stock isn't exposed to unlimited acceptances. That rationale doesn't fit Camel Cash: RJR's goal was to sell as many cigarettes as possible (not to unload a fixed, limited inventory), and RJR alone controlled how many C-Notes it issued and how many catalogs it distributed — it could never be "trapped" by acceptances exceeding its own inventory.
- **Indefiniteness and discretion.** RJR retained broad discretion over catalog timing, contents, and required certificate quantities, but discretion of that kind doesn't defeat contract formation where it is cabined by the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and where the plaintiffs did not treat those specifics as essential terms.
- **Mutuality of obligation.** RJR argued its unrestricted right to terminate the program meant neither side was bound, so there could be no contract. The court held the "mutuality of obligation" doctrine — "both parties must be bound or neither will be" — simply doesn't apply to unilateral contracts, where the offeree was never bound to keep performing in the first place.

## Episode 6: The Doctrine of Consideration

Episode 6 shifts from contract *formation via offer/acceptance* to contract *enforceability via consideration* — using the classic trio of nephew/aunt/relative gift-promise cases.

Key concepts:

- **Bargained-for exchange vs. gratuitous promise.** Consideration is traditionally defined as either a benefit to the promisor or a legal detriment to the promisee, bargained for in exchange for the promise. A promise to make a gift, with nothing sought in return, is not enforceable no matter how sincerely intended.
- ***Hamer v. Sidway***, 124 N.Y. 538 (1891) — the "5K inducement" case referenced in the email. An uncle promised his nephew $5,000 if the nephew would refrain from drinking, using tobacco, swearing, and playing cards or billiards for money until age 21. The nephew complied. The court held this was supported by consideration: the nephew surrendered a *legal right* he was otherwise free to exercise (a legal detriment), and it is irrelevant whether the uncle actually benefited — **the nephew changed his behavior because of the $5,000 inducement**, which is enough. Courts will not inquire into the adequacy of consideration, only its presence.
- ***Dougherty v. Salt***, 227 N.Y. 200 (1919) (Cardozo, J.) — contrasted with *Hamer*. An aunt gave her nephew a promissory note reciting "value received," but there was no actual bargained-for exchange — just an expression of affection. Cardozo held a bare recital of consideration, without a real bargain, is not enough; "a mere pretense of bargain does not suffice" (Restatement (Second) § 71 cmt. b).
- ***Kirksey v. Kirksey***, 8 Ala. 131 (1845) — a brother-in-law's invitation to a widow to move onto his land, followed by her eviction, was held to be a gratuitous promise rather than a bargained-for exchange, even though she detrimentally relied on it (foreshadowing the promissory-estoppel doctrine taken up later in the course).
- **Vehicles for enforcing gift promises without consideration.** Where a bargain is genuinely absent, the law offers other devices to carry out a donor's intent: a formal *testamentary gift* (a will), or a *gift in trust* — both discussed in the notes following *Dougherty*.

## How Episodes 5 and 6 connect

Episode 5 asks whether an **offer** exists at all (turning on whether the advertiser's proposal is the kind the law will hold it to). Episode 6 assumes an offer/promise exists and asks whether it is **enforceable** — i.e., whether something was actually bargained for in exchange. Sateriale and Hamer both illustrate the same doctrinal move: courts refusing to let a promisor escape liability on formal/technical grounds (advertisement, revocability, gift-like language) once the promisee has actually changed position in reliance on — or performance of — the promised exchange.
