# Case Brief: Dohrmann v. Swaney

**Citation:** 2014 IL App (1st) 131524

## Facts

George Dohrmann III and Virginia Rogers were neighbors in a Chicago cooperative apartment building; Dohrmann's apartment was not his primary residence, while the much larger apartment was Rogers's primary residence. Rogers was a 73-year-old childless widow when the two met in 1984, and the two grew close over the following years. After an attempted (and abandoned) adult-adoption plan fell through, Dohrmann and Rogers — then 89 years old, with no witnesses present and without consulting her longtime attorney — signed a one-page document on April 1, 2000, titled "Agreement." Under its terms, in exchange for Dohrmann's "past and future services" and for "helping the Rogers name to continue" by incorporating "Rogers" into his children's names, Rogers agreed to leave Dohrmann, upon her death, her cooperative apartment and its contents (appraised around $1,438,000, plus roughly $100,045 in furnishings) and $4,000,000 in cash — over $5.5 million total. Two months later, Dohrmann legally added "Rogers" as one of several middle names for each of his two sons; the boys used the name only inconsistently thereafter (e.g., not on driver's licenses, credit cards, or Facebook), and nothing in the contract required them to keep or use it. Rogers's actual estate plan never mentioned Dohrmann. She was later adjudicated disabled due to dementia/Alzheimer's, and her guardian/executor, Thomas Swaney, refused to honor the agreement after her death. Dohrmann sued for a declaratory judgment and a constructive trust; the estate counterclaimed that the contract was procured by fraud and was unenforceable. The trial court granted summary judgment for the estate, and Dohrmann appealed.

## Issue

Is a contract void for lack of sufficient consideration where the consideration furnished by one party is so grossly inadequate relative to what was promised in return that it shocks the conscience of the court, particularly where the transaction was also marked by grossly unequal bargaining power?

## Rule of Law

Under Illinois law, the existence of consideration is a question of law for the court. Ordinarily courts will not weigh the relative value or adequacy of consideration exchanged. However, where the amount of consideration passing is so grossly inadequate as to shock the conscience of the court, a court may treat the contract as void; this is treated as tantamount to evidence of fraud (actual or constructive) or as rendering the contract unconscionable. Where the inadequacy of consideration is accompanied by circumstances of unfairness — such as extreme disparity in the parties' bargaining positions, age, or capacity — the contract may be set aside even more readily; "where the inadequacy is great, the circumstances of unfairness need only be slight" to justify rescission. Consideration that is illusory (i.e., not actually binding or enforceable against the promisor) is likewise grossly inadequate and will not support a contract.

## Analysis

The court rejected Dohrmann's argument that a court may never examine the relative value of consideration exchanged, holding that this limitation yields where the disparity is extreme enough to shock the conscience. It then found the disparity here shocking as a matter of law: Rogers promised over $5.5 million in exchange for Dohrmann adding "Rogers" as one of several middle names to his sons' legal names. The court further found this "consideration" illusory — the contract contained no provision requiring the boys (who were minors and non-parties to the contract) to actually use or retain the name, and the record showed they used it only sporadically; nothing bound them to "continue the Rogers name" in any meaningful, enforceable sense. Separately and independently, the court found circumstances of unfairness sufficient on their own to void the contract: at signing, Rogers was an 89-year-old widow with no children, entering a multi-million-dollar transaction with a physician neighbor, without any witnesses and without consulting the longtime attorney who had otherwise handled her affairs — evidence of the "extremely disproportionate bargaining power of the parties." The court noted these two grounds — gross inadequacy of consideration and circumstances of unfairness — could each independently support voiding the contract, but were mutually reinforcing here.

## Conclusion

The Illinois Appellate Court affirmed summary judgment for the estate, holding the agreement unenforceable both because the consideration Rogers received was so grossly inadequate as to shock the conscience (and effectively illusory), and because the circumstances surrounding execution reflected grossly unequal bargaining power.

## Doctrinal significance

*Dohrmann* is the counterpoint to *[[batsakis_v_demotsis_brief|Batsakis v. Demotsis]]*: while courts generally refuse to weigh adequacy of consideration, that refusal is not absolute. When inadequacy becomes so extreme that it "shocks the conscience" — especially when paired with an unfair bargaining dynamic (elderly/incapacitated party, no independent counsel, no witnesses, vast wealth disparity) — courts will treat the promise as unsupported by real consideration or as unconscionable, and decline to enforce it.
