# Synthesis: Adequacy of Consideration and Promissory Estoppel

Written response drawing together the four cases attached to Prof. Bush's "Summary Episodes 5-6 and other important information" email (2026-09-03): *Batsakis v. Demotsis*, *Dohrmann v. Swaney*, *Greiner v. Greiner*, and *Wright v. Newman*. These four extend the consideration doctrine introduced in Episode 6 (*Hamer v. Sidway*, *Dougherty v. Salt*, *Kirksey v. Kirksey* — see `agent/contracts/episode_5_6_summary.md`) along two further axes: how far courts will go in refusing to weigh the *adequacy* of consideration, and when a promise can be enforced *without* bargained-for consideration at all under promissory estoppel.

## Axis 1 — Adequacy of consideration: the general rule and its outer limit

- **[[batsakis_v_demotsis_brief|Batsakis v. Demotsis]]** states the default rule in its starkest form: a promise to repay $2,000 in exchange for $25 worth of wartime drachmae is fully enforceable, because courts ask only whether consideration exists, not whether it was a fair bargain. "Mere inadequacy of consideration will not void a contract."
- **[[dohrmann_v_swaney_brief|Dohrmann v. Swaney]]** shows the limit of that rule: a promise to leave $5.5 million in exchange for adding a name as one of several middle names for two children — with no obligation on anyone to actually keep using it — is *so* grossly inadequate that it "shocks the conscience," and is treated as illusory/tantamount to fraud. The court also found independently that the extreme disparity in the parties' bargaining position (an 89-year-old widow with no independent counsel present, versus a much younger neighbor) was itself sufficient to void the deal as unconscionable.
- **Reading the two together:** the general rule (no judicial inquiry into fairness) survives *Dohrmann* — the court did not overrule *Batsakis*-style deference, it applied a narrow exception that only fires when inadequacy is extreme *and/or* the bargaining dynamics are markedly unfair. The relevant question for exam purposes is not "was this a bad deal?" but "is this the kind of shockingly one-sided, illusory, or procedurally unfair bargain where courts will look past the nominal existence of consideration?"

## Axis 2 — Enforcing promises without bargained-for consideration: promissory estoppel

- **[[greiner_v_greiner_brief|Greiner v. Greiner]]** and **[[wright_v_newman_brief|Wright v. Newman]]** both enforce a promise that lacks a traditional bargained-for exchange, relying on the doctrine that became Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90: a promise reasonably expected to induce, and that does induce, definite and substantial reliance is binding if only enforcement can avoid injustice.
  - In *Greiner*, Frank gave up an established homestead and rebuilt his life around his mother's promise of land; a money judgment could not undo the injustice of taking that land back.
  - In *Wright*, Newman gave up the practical ability to pursue support from the child's biological father for a decade in reliance on Wright's sustained conduct as father; by the time he reneged, that alternative was effectively foreclosed.
- Both cases required the court to find (1) a promise (in *Greiner*, initially indefinite but later made definite by conduct; in *Wright*, implied entirely from a course of conduct rather than words), and (2) reasonable, detrimental, and substantial reliance actually induced by that promise. *Wright*'s dissent is the important cautionary note: promissory estoppel is not a free pass around consideration — a court must still find the record actually establishes reasonable reliance *to the promisee's detriment*, not merely that the promisor later changed course.

## How this connects back to Episode 6

*Hamer*, *Dougherty*, and *Kirksey* establish that a promise is enforceable only where something was actually bargained for — gift promises, however sincere, are not enough. *Batsakis* and *Dohrmann* refine that inquiry by addressing how much scrutiny courts give to the *value* of what was bargained for. *Greiner* and *Wright* address the doctrine's escape valve: even where no bargain exists (as in *Kirksey*), a promise can still bind the promisor if the promisee reasonably and substantially changed position in reliance on it — precisely the reliance *Kirksey*'s widow arguably had, but which the *Kirksey* court did not yet have a doctrinal vehicle to enforce.
