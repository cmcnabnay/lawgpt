# Analysis: Sateriale's Implications and Its Relationship to Izadi

## The email's framing

Professor Bush's episode summary describes *Sateriale v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co.*, 697 F.3d 777 (9th Cir. 2012), as "the unilateral contract version of *Izadi*" — i.e., *Izadi v. Machado (Gus) Ford, Inc.*, 550 So. 2d 1135 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1989). Both cases confront the same background common-law presumption — that an advertisement is merely an invitation to negotiate, not an offer — and both hold that presumption overcome on their facts. But they get there by different doctrinal routes, and the difference in route is the more interesting half of the comparison.

## Where the cases converge

1. **Both reject the "an ad is not an offer" default.** In *Izadi*, the Florida court held Gus Machado Ford's newspaper ad — promising a "$3,000 minimum trade-in allowance" toward any Ford, with the real limitation buried in fine print — was, viewed as a whole and from the perspective of an objectively reasonable reader, an offer that Izadi accepted by presenting cash and a trade-in. In *Sateriale*, the Ninth Circuit likewise refused to let RJR hide behind the general advertisement rule for its Camel Cash certificates.
2. **Both apply an objective theory of offer.** Neither court cares what the advertiser subjectively intended. *Izadi* quotes Williston: the test is "not what the party making it thought it meant... but what a reasonable person in the position of the parties would have thought it meant." *Sateriale* asks whether "the recipient of the advertisement reasonably might have concluded that by acting in accordance with the request a contract would be formed" (quoting *Donovan v. RRL Corp.*). In both cases, the advertiser's protestation that it "didn't really mean it that way" fails.
3. **Both find the advertiser trying to have it both ways.** Gus Machado Ford wanted to draw customers in with a bold $3,000 figure while disclaiming it in fine print; RJR wanted to induce cigarette purchases with a formal-sounding rewards program while later disclaiming any binding obligation to keep the program open. Both courts treat this asymmetry — inviting reliance while reserving an escape hatch — with skepticism.

## Where the cases diverge — and why the divergence matters

1. **Bilateral vs. unilateral theory of contract.** *Izadi* is a bilateral-contract case: the court treats the ad's $3,000 trade-in language as an offer that Izadi *accepted* the moment he showed up ready to buy on those terms, forming mutual obligations then and there. *Sateriale* expressly holds the opposite is true for Camel Cash — the plaintiffs alleged no return promise to RJR, so there was no bilateral offer at all. The claim survives only under **unilateral-contract** theory: RJR's "offer" was accepted not by a promise but by the *performance* of purchasing cigarettes, saving C-Notes, and redeeming them. This is why the professor calls Sateriale "the unilateral contract version of Izadi" — same underlying skepticism toward advertiser disclaimers, but routed through the *other* branch of offer-and-acceptance doctrine.
2. **The bait-and-switch / misleading-advertising rationale versus the reward exception.** *Izadi*'s holding leans heavily on a bait-and-switch theory: the ad's fine print existed to lure customers with one deal and switch them to another, and Florida law (plus the Restatement (Second) § 20(2)(a) misunderstanding doctrine) will treat deliberately misleading advertising as an offer despite the advertiser's private intent otherwise. *Sateriale*, by contrast, does not rest on any theory that RJR intended to deceive; it rests on the **reward exception** to the general advertisement rule — the settled principle that offers of rewards (for capture of a fugitive, return of lost property, or **redemption of coupons**) are treated as true offers because they invite performance of a specific act without further negotiation. *Sateriale* is a "no-fault" application of ordinary offer doctrine to a coupon/rewards program; *Izadi* is closer to a doctrine of policing deceptive sales tactics.
3. **The over-acceptance and limited-supply policy is central in Sateriale but plays essentially no role in Izadi.** This is the sharpest doctrinal difference between the two cases, and it is the policy point the professor's email specifically flags.
   - The general common-law rule against treating ads as offers exists, per Professor Farnsworth, to protect a seller of a **limited supply** of goods from being deemed to have made an offer to every reader of the ad — otherwise a sold-out shopkeeper could face a "multitude of acceptances" it has no inventory to satisfy.
   - *Izadi* never has to grapple with this rationale directly, because Gus Machado Ford was offering a defined trade-in allowance on specific, identified vehicles to whichever individual customer showed up and met the ad's terms — a garden-variety single-transaction dispute, not a mass-acceptance problem.
   - *Sateriale* confronts the over-acceptance rationale head-on and holds it **inapplicable**: RJR's purpose was not to sell a fixed, limited stock at a set price, but to induce as many consumers as possible to buy Camel cigarettes — the opposite of a limited-supply scenario. And critically, RJR itself controlled the number of possible "acceptances" by controlling how many C-Notes it printed and how many catalogs it issued, so it could never be trapped by demand outstripping supply the way an ordinary retailer with fixed stock could be. Where "the very nature of a proposal restricts its maker's potential liability to a reasonable number of people," the rationale for denying offer status disappears — and a reward/loyalty program, administered entirely at the promisor's own discretion, is exactly such a case.
4. **Mutuality of obligation surfaces only in Sateriale.** Because *Izadi* is analyzed as a completed, accepted bilateral offer for a single transaction, there is no occasion to ask whether an ongoing "program" lacked mutuality. *Sateriale* has to address it because RJR ran a multi-year program it reserved the right to terminate; the court's answer — that mutuality-of-obligation doctrine simply doesn't apply to unilateral contracts, since the offeree was never bound to continue performing anyway — is a distinctly unilateral-contract move with no analogue needed in *Izadi*.

## Broader implications of the ruling

- **Sateriale extends "new-style" unilateral-contract enforcement to mass-market loyalty programs.** As the casebook notes observe (citing Professor Pettit), modern courts increasingly use unilateral-contract doctrine not to let a promisor escape liability (the classical, Wormser-era concern) but to *impose* liability on a sophisticated commercial actor that induced broad public reliance. Sateriale is a leading example: it treats a decades-long, mass-market rewards program the same way older doctrine treated a reward for a lost dog or captured fugitive.
- **The reward exception has real teeth against companies that try to disclaim binding effect after the fact.** By locating Camel Cash within the reward/coupon-redemption exception rather than resting on a bait-and-switch theory, *Sateriale* offers a doctrinally cleaner path than *Izadi* for future plaintiffs challenging loyalty, rewards, and points programs generally (airline miles, credit-card rewards, retailer loyalty cards) — companies cannot assume that broad "advertisement" language shields these programs from contract exposure, particularly once they control their own liability exposure by controlling supply.
- **Together, the cases stand for a unifying principle:** advertisers and program sponsors who invite public reliance through specific, restriction-laden, seemingly binding language will have a hard time later recharacterizing that language as "mere advertising" — whether the doctrinal vehicle is bilateral offer-and-acceptance policed for bait-and-switch (*Izadi*) or unilateral offer-and-acceptance freed from the over-acceptance/limited-supply justification for treating ads skeptically (*Sateriale*).
