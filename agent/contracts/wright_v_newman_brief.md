# Case Brief: Wright v. Newman

**Citation:** 266 Ga. 519, 467 S.E.2d 533 (1996)

## Facts

Kim Newman sued her former boyfriend, Bruce Wright, seeking child support for her daughter and son. Wright admitted paternity of the daughter, but DNA testing established he was not the biological father of the son. Nevertheless, for approximately ten years, Wright had listed himself as the father on the son's birth certificate, given the child his own surname, and otherwise held himself out to the child and to others as the boy's father, knowing at the time he did so that he was not the biological father. The trial court found that, in reliance on Wright's conduct, Newman had refrained from identifying and pursuing support from the child's actual biological father — a source of support she could have pursued at the time but which, a decade later, would likely be practically impossible to locate and establish. The trial court ordered Wright to pay child support for the son despite the lack of biological or adoptive paternity, and Wright appealed.

## Issue

Can a person who is neither the biological nor legally adoptive parent of a child nonetheless be held legally obligated to pay child support, based on a promise (implied through conduct) to assume parental support obligations, under the doctrine of promissory estoppel?

## Rule of Law

Georgia's promissory-estoppel statute, OCGA § 13-3-44(a) (codifying Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90), provides that a promise which the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance by the promisee (or a third person), and which does induce such action or forbearance, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcing the promise; the remedy may be limited as justice requires. Consideration sufficient to support such a promise may be found in any benefit to the promisor or any reliance, loss, or disadvantage imposed on the promisee. Although Wright had no support obligation grounded in parentage (OCGA § 19-7-2) or formal adoption (OCGA § 19-8-19(a)(2)), and the "virtual adoption" doctrine did not apply, an enforceable obligation could still arise under ordinary contract/promissory-estoppel principles.

## Analysis

The majority held the evidence supported finding that Wright made an implicit but clear promise — through his affirmative acts of listing himself as father on the birth certificate, giving the child his surname, and maintaining a father-son relationship for a decade — to assume the full obligations of fatherhood, including support. He is presumed to know the legal consequence of such acts (that a parent is obligated to support a child), and he undertook the commitment knowingly and voluntarily, sustaining it for ten years. Newman and the child relied on that promise to their detriment: by not identifying or pursuing the biological father while Wright fulfilled the paternal role, Newman lost the practical ability to now obtain support from that individual after a decade's delay. Allowing Wright to walk away after ten years of voluntarily assumed fatherhood would work an injustice remediable only by enforcing the promise. Justice Sears's concurrence emphasized that promissory estoppel requires only *reasonable* reliance, not that the promisee first exhaust every alternative source of support before enforcing the promise. Chief Justice Benham's dissent argued the majority skipped the required showing of detrimental reliance: the record did not establish Newman was actually unable to locate or proceed against the biological father, and undisputed facts showed Newman and Wright had been estranged and Wright had provided no actual support for roughly seven of the ten years — undercutting any claim that the passage of time (rather than the estrangement itself) caused whatever prejudice existed.

## Conclusion

The Georgia Supreme Court affirmed the order requiring Wright to pay child support for Newman's son, holding his sustained course of conduct in acting as the child's father constituted a promise enforceable under the doctrine of promissory estoppel, given Newman's detrimental reliance on it.

## Doctrinal significance

*Wright* is a modern application of the same § 90 promissory-estoppel framework at work in *[[greiner_v_greiner_brief|Greiner v. Greiner]]*: a promise lacking traditional bargained-for consideration is nonetheless enforced because the promisor should have reasonably expected — and did in fact induce — substantial, detrimental reliance by the promisee, and only enforcement can avoid injustice. The Benham dissent is worth studying alongside the majority for how courts can disagree sharply over whether "detriment" and "reasonable reliance" are actually established on a given record, a recurring fault line in promissory-estoppel cases.
